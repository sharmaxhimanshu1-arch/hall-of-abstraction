Liberalism is the political philosophy that places individual liberty at its centre. It emerged from the religious wars and constitutional struggles of seventeenth-century Europe and matured during the Enlightenment. Its foundational thinkers include **John Locke**, who grounded government in consent and natural rights; **Montesquieu**, who analysed the separation of powers; and **Adam Smith**, who described the workings of free markets and commercial society. In the nineteenth century **John Stuart Mill** gave it its classic defence of free thought and expression.

## Core ideas

- **Natural rights.** Individuals possess rights, classically to life, liberty and property, that do not depend on the permission of rulers.
- **Government by consent.** Political authority is legitimate only if it rests on the consent of the governed and serves to protect their rights.
- **Limited and divided power.** Power tends to corrupt, so it must be checked: by constitutions, the rule of law, an independent judiciary, and the separation of legislative, executive and judicial functions.
- **Toleration and free expression.** The state should not enforce a single religion or way of life. Open debate is the best route to truth.
- **Markets and commerce.** Free exchange, under the rule of law, tends to increase prosperity and to soften manners and conflicts between peoples.

## Varieties

Liberalism has never been a single doctrine. "Classical" liberals stress economic freedom and minimal government. "Social" or "modern" liberals, from the later Mill onward, argue that real liberty requires education, welfare and protection from poverty. In American usage "liberal" usually means the latter, while in Europe it often means the former.

## Legacy

Liberal ideas shaped the English Bill of Rights (1689), the American Declaration of Independence and Constitution, the French Declaration of the Rights of Man, and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Critics from both conservatism and socialism have challenged its picture of the individual and its tolerance of inequality, and those debates continue to define modern politics.
