Modern Indian thought grew out of a shock and a question. The shock was colonial rule: by the nineteenth century a trading company, and then the British Crown, governed a civilisation thousands of years old. The question was how India should answer. Should it imitate the West, return to its own traditions, or create something new from both? Reformers like Raja Rammohun Roy, Jyotirao Phule and Savitribai Phule began the argument. In the twentieth century it produced thinkers who changed the world far beyond India.

## Core ideas

- **Religion as experience and service.** Swami Vivekananda presented Vedanta as a universal, practical religion: the divine in every person, so that serving the poor is worship.
- **A humanism without walls.** Rabindranath Tagore distrusted every narrow loyalty, including nationalism, and built a school open to the whole world.
- **Truth-force and non-violence.** M. K. Gandhi's *satyagraha* turned suffering accepted without hatred into a political weapon, and made swaraj mean self-rule over oneself as much as over the country.
- **Annihilation of caste.** B. R. Ambedkar showed that caste is "graded inequality" sanctified by scripture. He fought it through law, education, organisation, the Constitution and finally conversion to Buddhism.
- **Arguing with each other.** These thinkers disagreed sharply: Tagore with Gandhi over the charkha and nationalism, Ambedkar with Gandhi over caste and the Poona Pact. Much of their power comes from the argument itself.

## Legacy

Their ideas are built into the Indian Constitution (equality before the law, the abolition of untouchability, and the directive principles). They also shaped movements far beyond India: Martin Luther King Jr. learned non-violent resistance from Gandhi, Dalit movements across the world draw on Ambedkar, and Tagore remains one of the great voices of a shared humanity. Their debates about religion and the secular state, caste and justice, the nation and the world are still India's debates today.
