Classical Greek philosophy names the extraordinary century and a half, roughly 470 to 322 BCE, in which Athens became the centre of Western intellectual life. Earlier Greek thinkers, the so-called Presocratics, had already asked what the world is ultimately made of. The classical philosophers turned that curiosity inward and outward at once, toward ethics, politics, knowledge and the soul, and toward a single, demanding method: reasoned argument that anyone could follow and test.

## Core ideas

- **Reason as the path to truth.** Claims should be examined through definition, argument and counter-argument, not accepted on authority or custom.
- **Virtue and the good life.** The central ethical question is not "what should I do?" but "what kind of person should I be?" A flourishing life (*eudaimonia*) depends on cultivating virtues such as justice, courage, wisdom and moderation.
- **Reality beyond appearances.** Behind the changing world of the senses lie stable structures (Plato's Forms, Aristotle's essences and causes) that reason can grasp.
- **The political animal.** Human beings realise their nature in communities, so ethics and politics are two halves of one inquiry.

## History and influence

The tradition runs through three linked generations: Socrates, who wrote nothing but questioned everyone; his student Plato, who founded the Academy; and Plato's student Aristotle, who founded the Lyceum and systematised almost every field of knowledge. Their schools, and those of their successors, endured for centuries.

Nearly every later movement in this Hall defines itself in conversation with them. Stoicism and Epicureanism grew directly out of Socratic ethics. Medieval Christian, Jewish and Islamic thinkers rebuilt theology on Platonic and Aristotelian foundations. The modern sciences began, in part, as a revolt against Aristotle's physics, and modern ethics has repeatedly returned to his account of virtue.

## Key debates

Do the Forms exist apart from things, as Plato held, or only in them, as Aristotle argued? Can virtue be taught? Is the best city ruled by the wise, by law, or by a mixed constitution? These questions remain live, which is one reason the classical philosophers are still read rather than merely studied.
