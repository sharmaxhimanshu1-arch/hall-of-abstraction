Conservatism, as a self-conscious political philosophy, was born in reaction to the French Revolution. Its founding text is **Edmund Burke**'s *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790), which warned that sweeping away inherited institutions in the name of abstract reason would end in tyranny and bloodshed, a warning many thought confirmed by the Terror of 1793–94.

## Core ideas

- **Respect for tradition.** Customs, institutions and moral habits embody the accumulated wisdom of generations, often in ways no single person can fully articulate. They deserve a presumption in their favour.
- **Scepticism of abstract reason.** Human nature and society are complex. Grand schemes for remaking society according to theory tend to have unintended and often disastrous consequences.
- **Reform, not revolution.** Conservatism is not opposition to all change. Burke wrote that "a state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation." Change should be gradual, practical and continuous with what came before.
- **Society as a partnership across generations.** We inherit society from the dead and hold it in trust for the unborn. We are not free to treat it as our own experiment.
- **Intermediate institutions.** Families, churches, local communities and voluntary associations, Burke's "little platoons", are the roots of public affection and a buffer between the individual and the state.

## Varieties

Conservatism takes different forms in different places, since what is to be conserved differs. It has allied at various times with monarchy, religion, nationalism and, in the twentieth century, free-market liberalism. Thinkers such as Michael Oakeshott and Russell Kirk developed its philosophical side.

## Legacy

Burke's ideas shaped British and American conservatism and influenced critics of revolutionary utopianism from Alexis de Tocqueville to Friedrich Hayek. Even opponents often credit conservatism with an insight that every political tradition must reckon with: the limits of human knowledge and the fragility of social order.
