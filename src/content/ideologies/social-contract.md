For most of history, rulers claimed authority from God, conquest or ancient custom. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, amid wars of religion, civil wars and revolutions, a group of thinkers asked a startling question: if we imagine people without any government at all, in a "state of nature", why would they agree to set one up, and on what terms? Their answers became the foundation of modern democratic thought.

## Core ideas

- **The state of nature.** A thought experiment about life without government. Hobbes pictured a war of all against all; Locke a world of natural rights that are insecure; Rousseau a peaceful solitude spoiled by property and comparison.
- **Consent.** Legitimate authority comes from the agreement of the governed, not from birth or force.
- **The contract.** People give up some of their natural liberty in exchange for security, rights or freedom secured together. What they give up, and to whom, divides the theorists.
- **Sovereignty.** For Hobbes, an absolute sovereign above the people; for Locke, a limited government held in trust; for Rousseau, the people themselves, expressing a general will.
- **The right to resist.** If rulers break the contract, may the people resist? Hobbes said almost never; Locke said yes; Rousseau said the people never gave up sovereignty in the first place.

## Legacy

Social contract theory shaped the American Declaration of Independence, the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and the opening words of modern constitutions, including India's "We, the people". In the twentieth century, John Rawls revived it as a way to think about justice: what rules would people agree to if they did not know what position they would occupy in society?
