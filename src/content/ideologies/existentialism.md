Existentialism is less a doctrine than a family resemblance among thinkers who began their philosophy from the concrete situation of the individual human being: born without choosing it, facing death, and forced to decide how to live without guaranteed answers. Its nineteenth-century forerunners were **Søren Kierkegaard** and **Friedrich Nietzsche**. The movement received its name, and enormous popularity, in post-war Paris through **Jean-Paul Sartre** and **Simone de Beauvoir**.

## Core ideas

- **Existence precedes essence.** Human beings have no fixed nature or purpose given in advance, whether by God or by nature. We first exist, and then define ourselves through our choices and actions.
- **Radical freedom and responsibility.** Because nothing determines us, we are, in Sartre's phrase, "condemned to be free", and fully responsible for what we make of ourselves.
- **Anxiety and authenticity.** The recognition of our freedom brings anguish. We are tempted to flee into "bad faith", pretending we had no choice. Authentic existence means owning our freedom.
- **The absurd.** The universe offers no inherent meaning. The question is how to live in the face of that silence, whether by a "leap of faith" (Kierkegaard), the creation of new values (Nietzsche), or committed action (Sartre and Beauvoir).
- **Lived experience over abstraction.** Existentialists distrusted grand systems, Hegel's above all, that seemed to forget the individual. Many wrote novels and plays as much as treatises.

## Religious and atheist existentialism

Kierkegaard was a passionate Christian whose philosophy aimed at a more authentic faith. Nietzsche announced "the death of God". Sartre's existentialism was explicitly atheist, while thinkers such as Gabriel Marcel and Paul Tillich developed religious versions. Albert Camus, often grouped with the existentialists, rejected the label.

## Legacy

Existentialism shaped twentieth-century literature, theatre, theology and psychotherapy, and Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* became a founding text of modern feminism. Its central questions about freedom, authenticity and meaning remain among the most personal that philosophy asks.
