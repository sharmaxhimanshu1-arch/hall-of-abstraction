Around 1900, a few philosophers in Cambridge, Vienna and Jena (Gottlob Frege, Bertrand Russell, G. E. Moore and, soon, Ludwig Wittgenstein) made a bold move. Instead of asking directly "What is truth?" or "What is the mind?", they asked: what do these words mean, and how does language connect to the world? Armed with the new mathematical logic, they hoped to make philosophy as clear and rigorous as science. The movement they started now dominates philosophy departments in Britain, North America, Australia and much of the world, including India.

## Core ideas

- **Clarity first.** Many philosophical problems, they argued, come from vague or confused language. State the question precisely and half the work is done; sometimes the problem disappears.
- **Logic as a tool.** Modern logic can reveal the hidden structure of sentences, and show which arguments are valid.
- **Language and meaning.** What makes a sentence meaningful? Early analytic philosophers said it must picture facts or be checkable by experience; later ones, following Wittgenstein, said meaning lies in how words are used in everyday life.
- **Philosophy and science.** Most analytic philosophers respect science deeply. Some saw philosophy's job as clarifying science's concepts; others insisted that ordinary language and common sense have their own wisdom.
- **Argument in small steps.** Analytic writing favours clear premises, explicit arguments and careful objections, often on narrow questions, rather than grand systems.

## Legacy

Analytic philosophy shaped computer science and artificial intelligence (through logic and the study of language), linguistics, cognitive science, and modern debates about justice, rights and ethics: John Rawls's theory of justice and Amartya Sen's work on capability and welfare grew from it. Its critics say it can become narrow and technical, losing sight of the big questions of how to live. Its defenders reply that clear thinking is the first step to answering any question at all.
