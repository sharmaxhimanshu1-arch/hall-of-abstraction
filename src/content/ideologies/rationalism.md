Rationalism is the view that reason, operating independently of sensory experience, is a primary source of knowledge, and that some of our most important knowledge is *a priori*: knowable by thought alone. The label is most closely tied to three seventeenth-century philosophers: **René Descartes**, **Baruch Spinoza** and **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz**. They wrote in the shadow of the Scientific Revolution, when mathematics had just shown its astonishing power to describe nature.

## Core ideas

- **Innate ideas.** The mind is not born empty. Concepts such as God, substance, cause, number and the self are present in reason itself, waiting to be uncovered rather than learned from experience.
- **Certainty through deduction.** Like geometry, philosophy should start from self-evident first principles and proceed by rigorous proof. Spinoza went so far as to write his *Ethics* in the format of Euclid.
- **The intelligibility of the world.** Reality has a rational structure. Leibniz's **principle of sufficient reason** holds that nothing is so without a reason why it is so and not otherwise.
- **Substance and mind.** The rationalists built ambitious systems about what ultimately exists. Descartes posited two substances, mind and matter. Spinoza held that there is only one, God or Nature. Leibniz proposed infinitely many simple substances, the monads.

## Rationalism versus empiricism

Textbooks often frame early modern philosophy as a contest between the Continental rationalists and the British empiricists (Locke, Berkeley and Hume), who held that all knowledge comes from experience. The division is a useful map but an oversimplification: Descartes did experiments, and Locke valued demonstration. The debate was eventually reshaped by Immanuel Kant, who argued that knowledge requires both the concepts of reason and the content of experience.

## Legacy

Rationalist ideals live on in mathematics, logic and theoretical physics, and in the hope that the universe is fully intelligible. Leibniz's dream of a universal calculus of reasoning anticipated symbolic logic and computing. Descartes' mind–body problem remains one of the central puzzles of philosophy of mind, and Chomsky's theory of an innate universal grammar revived the debate over innate ideas in the twentieth century.
