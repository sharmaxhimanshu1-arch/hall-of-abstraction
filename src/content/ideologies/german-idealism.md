German Idealism is the name for an extraordinarily fertile period of German philosophy, from the 1780s to the 1840s. It began with **Immanuel Kant**'s "Copernican revolution" in philosophy and was carried forward by Fichte and Schelling, culminating in the vast system of **G. W. F. Hegel**. Few movements have been as demanding to read or as influential to inherit.

## Core ideas

- **The mind shapes experience.** Kant argued that we never know things as they are in themselves. The mind actively organises experience through forms of space and time and categories such as causality. The world as we know it is partly the mind's construction.
- **Autonomy and freedom.** Morality is grounded not in God's commands or in happiness but in reason's ability to give itself law. Freedom, understood as rational self-determination, becomes the central value of the movement.
- **Overcoming dualisms.** Kant's successors sought to heal the splits he left between mind and world, freedom and nature, and the knowable and the unknowable "thing in itself".
- **History and dialectic.** For Hegel, reality is a dynamic process in which Spirit (*Geist*) comes to know itself through conflict and resolution in history, art, religion and philosophy. Each stage contains contradictions that drive it toward a richer whole.

## Note on "idealism"

"Idealism" here does not mean optimism. It is the philosophical thesis that mind or reason is fundamental to the structure of reality. The German Idealists differed sharply on what this meant: Kant's "transcendental idealism" is quite modest compared with Hegel's "absolute idealism".

## Legacy

The movement's influence is almost impossible to overstate. Hegel's dialectic, turned "right side up" into materialism, became the foundation of Marxism. Reaction against Hegel helped produce existentialism (Kierkegaard) and, later, analytic philosophy. Kant's ethics of human dignity underpins modern human-rights thinking, and his account of knowledge set the terms for the philosophy of science and mind.
