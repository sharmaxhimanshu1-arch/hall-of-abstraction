Between the fall of Rome and the scientific revolution, philosophy did not sleep. It moved. Greek texts travelled from Athens and Alexandria to Baghdad, where they were translated into Arabic; from Baghdad to Córdoba in Muslim Spain; and from there, translated again into Latin, to the new universities of Paris and Oxford. Along the way, Christian, Muslim and Jewish thinkers faced the same hard question: if God has spoken in scripture, what is left for human reason to do? And if reason reaches a conclusion that seems to contradict scripture, which one wins?

## Core ideas

- **Faith and reason.** Most medieval thinkers held that truth cannot contradict truth. Reason and revelation both come from God, so a real conflict must mean a mistake in our reasoning or in our reading of scripture.
- **The inner life.** Augustine turned philosophy inward, to memory, desire, habit and the divided will, and wrote the first great autobiography of the soul.
- **The recovery of Aristotle.** Ibn Rushd wrote the commentaries that taught Europe how to read Aristotle, and Thomas Aquinas built a vast system joining Aristotle's philosophy with Christian theology.
- **Natural law.** There is a moral law written into human nature, knowable by reason, that stands above the laws of kings. An unjust law is not truly a law.
- **The science of society.** Ibn Khaldun studied why dynasties rise and fall, how group solidarity holds societies together, and why luxury, injustice and heavy taxes ruin them, centuries before sociology had a name.

## Legacy

Medieval philosophy gave the modern world its universities, its idea of a moral law above the state, the habit of stating your opponent's best argument before answering it, and the bridge across which Greek philosophy reached Europe. Its debates about faith and reason continue wherever people ask how science and religion can live together, from school biology classes to the Indian Constitution's duty "to develop the scientific temper".
