Utilitarianism is a moral theory with a deceptively simple principle: actions are right insofar as they promote happiness, and wrong insofar as they produce the reverse. It was systematised by the English reformer **Jeremy Bentham** in the late eighteenth century and refined by **John Stuart Mill** in the nineteenth. It remains one of the most influential and most debated approaches to ethics and public policy.

## Core ideas

- **Consequentialism.** The rightness of an action depends entirely on its consequences, not on the motive behind it or on whether it conforms to a rule.
- **Welfarism.** What ultimately matters is well-being, understood by the classical utilitarians as pleasure and the absence of pain.
- **Impartiality.** Everyone's happiness counts equally: "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one," in the dictum Mill attributed to Bentham.
- **Maximisation.** We should act to produce the greatest total balance of happiness over suffering.

## Bentham and Mill

Bentham proposed a "felicific calculus" to measure pleasures by their intensity, duration, certainty and so on, and he held that pleasures differ only in quantity: "prejudice apart, the game of push-pin is of equal value with the arts and sciences of music and poetry." Mill disagreed, arguing that some pleasures, those of the intellect, feeling and imagination, are higher in quality than others. He also tried to reconcile utilitarianism with individual rights and liberty.

## Criticisms

Critics have long objected that utilitarianism could justify terrible acts, such as punishing an innocent person, if they maximised overall happiness. They also argue that it ignores the separateness of persons and makes impossible demands on individuals. Defenders reply with refinements such as rule utilitarianism, which judges rules rather than individual acts.

## Legacy

Utilitarian reasoning transformed law, prisons, public health and economics: cost–benefit analysis is utilitarianism in a spreadsheet. Bentham and Mill were early advocates of women's rights, the decriminalisation of homosexuality, and the welfare of animals. In recent decades philosophers such as Peter Singer have drawn on utilitarianism to argue for animal liberation and global poverty relief, and it underpins the effective altruism movement.
