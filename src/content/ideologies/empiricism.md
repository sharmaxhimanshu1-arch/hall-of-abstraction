Empiricism holds that knowledge comes primarily, or entirely, from sensory experience. The tradition has ancient roots in Aristotle and the Epicureans, but it took definitive form in Britain in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries with **John Locke**, **George Berkeley** and **David Hume**. Francis Bacon, who championed observation and experiment, is often counted as its forerunner.

## Core ideas

- **The mind as a blank slate.** At birth the mind contains no innate ideas. Everything we think is built up from impressions of the senses and from reflection on our own mental operations.
- **Simple and complex ideas.** Complex concepts are assembled from simple ones. To understand an idea, trace it back to the experiences it came from, and if it came from none, suspect that it is empty.
- **The limits of knowledge.** Empiricists were wary of metaphysical systems that claimed to prove the nature of God, the soul or substance by reason alone. Hume pushed this furthest, arguing that our belief in causation and in an enduring self rests on habit rather than proof.
- **Probability over certainty.** Knowledge about the world is never absolutely certain. A wise person "proportions his belief to the evidence."

## The problem of induction

Hume's most famous challenge is to inductive reasoning. We expect the sun to rise tomorrow because it always has, but no logical rule guarantees that the future will resemble the past. The problem has never been fully solved and still drives debate in philosophy of science.

## Legacy

Empiricism became the working philosophy of modern science, with its emphasis on observation, experiment and testable hypotheses. It shaped the liberal political thought of Locke, the utilitarianism of Bentham and Mill, and the logical positivism of the twentieth century. Kant credited Hume with waking him from his "dogmatic slumber", and the dialogue between empiricism and rationalism still frames how philosophers think about knowledge.
