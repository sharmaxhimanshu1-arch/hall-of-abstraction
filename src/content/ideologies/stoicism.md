Stoicism was founded in Athens around 300 BCE by Zeno of Citium, who taught in the *Stoa Poikilē*, the Painted Porch, from which the school took its name. It became the most influential philosophy of the Roman world, attracting slaves and senators, playwrights and emperors. What survives most fully today is its late Roman phase, in the writings of Seneca, Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius.

## Core ideas

- **Virtue is the only good.** Wisdom, justice, courage and self-control are sufficient for a happy life. Wealth, health and reputation are "indifferents": some are preferable, but none is truly good or bad in itself.
- **The dichotomy of control.** Some things are "up to us" (our judgements, intentions and choices) and everything else is not. Distress comes from treating what is not up to us as if it were.
- **Living according to nature.** The cosmos is ordered by a rational principle, the *logos*. To live well is to live in harmony with that order and with our own nature as rational, social beings.
- **The passions as errors.** Destructive emotions such as fear, anger and envy arise from false judgements about what is good or bad, and can be corrected through reason and practice.
- **Cosmopolitanism.** Every person shares in reason, so all human beings are fellow citizens of one world-city.

## Practice

Stoicism was meant to be lived, not just studied. Its teachers recommended daily exercises: reviewing one's conduct each evening, rehearsing possible misfortunes in advance (*premeditatio malorum*), and reminding oneself of the transience of all things.

## Legacy

Stoic ideas shaped Christian moral thought, Renaissance humanism, and early modern theories of natural law and universal human rights. In the twentieth century they inspired cognitive behavioural therapy, whose founders acknowledged a debt to Epictetus. Today Stoicism enjoys a broad popular revival as a practical philosophy for resilience.
