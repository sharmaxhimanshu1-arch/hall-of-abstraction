## Start here

In April 1963, a Baptist minister sat in a jail cell in Birmingham, Alabama. He had been arrested for leading a peaceful march against racial segregation, which local laws required. Eight white clergymen had published a letter calling his protests "unwise and untimely". They asked: how can you urge people to obey some laws, like the Supreme Court's ruling against school segregation, and break others?

**Martin Luther King Jr.** wrote his answer on the margins of a newspaper and on scraps of paper smuggled out of the jail. There are two kinds of laws, he said: just and unjust. One has a moral duty to obey just laws, and a moral duty to disobey unjust ones. And how do you tell them apart? He reached back seven hundred years for the answer:

"To put it in the terms of St. Thomas Aquinas: An unjust law is a human law that is not rooted in eternal law and natural law."

Who was this medieval friar whose ideas could help a civil rights leader in an American jail?

**Thomas Aquinas** was a large, quiet, slow-moving Italian whose classmates nicknamed him "the dumb ox". He wrote more than eight million words, often dictating to several secretaries at once on different subjects. He took the newly rediscovered philosophy of Aristotle, which many Christians feared, and built from it, together with Christian faith, the most complete system of thought the Middle Ages produced. Then, three months before his death, he stopped writing, saying that everything he had written seemed like straw.

You do not need to share his faith to learn from him. He believed that a great deal can be known by reason alone, by anyone, of any religion or none. And his ideas about natural law, the common good, just war, the duties of the rich, and how to argue fairly still shape law courts, human rights, and debates in India and around the world. By the end of this page, you'll know him from the beginning to the end.

## The world he was born into

### The thirteenth century: Europe wakes up

Thomas was born around **1225**. It was one of the most dynamic centuries in European history. Towns and trade were growing. Great Gothic cathedrals were rising at Chartres, Reims and Paris. And a new institution had appeared: the **university**. Bologna, Paris and Oxford had begun as associations of teachers and students around 1200. For the first time in Europe, thousands of young men gathered in one place to study, argue and debate.

### Aristotle arrives

At the same time, Europe was receiving a flood of new books. For centuries, Latin Europe had known only a few of Aristotle's works on logic. Now, through translations from Arabic in Spain and Sicily, and from Greek in Constantinople, almost all of Aristotle arrived: his physics, metaphysics, biology, ethics, politics and psychology. They came accompanied by the commentaries of **Ibn Rushd** and **Ibn Sina**, Muslim philosophers, and by the work of the Jewish philosopher **Maimonides**.

This was exciting and frightening. Aristotle offered a complete, rational account of the world without any help from the Bible. And on some points he seemed to contradict Christian faith: he thought the world was **eternal**, with no beginning; he seemed to say the individual soul does not survive death. The church at first **banned** the teaching of Aristotle's natural philosophy at Paris (1210 and 1215). But the ban could not hold. By the 1250s, Aristotle was at the heart of the curriculum.

The question of the age was: what should Christians do with this pagan philosophy? Reject it? Swallow it whole? Or find a way to bring it together with the faith?

### The new orders of friars

Another new force was the **friars**. In the early 1200s, two new religious orders appeared: the **Franciscans**, founded by Francis of Assisi, and the **Dominicans**, founded by the Spaniard Dominic. Unlike monks, who lived in rural monasteries, friars lived in towns, owned nothing, begged for their food, and preached. The Dominicans especially were devoted to study and preaching, to answer the heresies spreading in southern France.

Many respectable families saw the friars as embarrassing: wandering beggars, a little like a well-born young person today announcing they are leaving university to join an unknown religious movement.

### Popes and emperors

Thomas's family lived on the border between the lands of the **Pope** and those of the **Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II**, who also ruled southern Italy. Pope and emperor were locked in a long struggle for power, and his family served the emperor.

## The life, as a story

### The castle and the abbey

Thomas was born around **1225** in the family castle of **Roccasecca**, near the town of **Aquino**, between Rome and Naples. (His name means "Thomas of Aquino".) His family were minor nobility. He was the youngest son.

At about **five**, his parents sent him to the great Benedictine abbey of **Monte Cassino**, founded by St Benedict seven centuries earlier. They had a plan: Thomas would become a monk and, one day, the abbot, a position of great wealth and power that would serve the family well.

### Naples: Aristotle and the beggars

In **1239**, fighting between the Pope and the emperor reached Monte Cassino, and the teenage Thomas was sent to study at the **University of Naples**, founded by Frederick II. There he met two things that changed his life. The first was **Aristotle**, whose natural philosophy was taught freely at Naples. The second was the **Dominicans**.

### The kidnapping

In **1244**, at about nineteen, Thomas joined the Dominican order. His family was horrified. A Benedictine abbot was a powerful lord; a Dominican was a beggar.

The Dominicans tried to send him north, to Paris, out of his family's reach. But on the road, his brothers, soldiers in the emperor's army, **seized him** and carried him back to the family castle. He was kept there for about **a year**.

His family tried everything to change his mind. According to the story told by his early biographers, his brothers even sent a prostitute into his room to seduce him. Thomas seized a burning stick from the fire, drove her out, and burned a cross into the wall.

His mother finally gave up, and in **1245**, he was allowed to leave (one story says his sisters helped him escape through a window). He rejoined the Dominicans.

### The dumb ox

The Dominicans sent him to Paris and then to **Cologne**, to study with **Albert the Great**, the most learned man in Europe, a scientist and philosopher who was working to bring all of Aristotle into Christian education.

Thomas was big, heavy and silent. His classmates, mistaking his silence for stupidity, called him **"the dumb ox"**. One classmate, feeling sorry for him, offered to help him with his studies, until he found that Thomas understood the material far better than he did. Albert, according to the traditional story, told the class: "You call him a dumb ox, but one day his bellowing will be heard throughout the world."

### Master in Paris

In **1256**, Thomas became a **master of theology** at the University of Paris, the highest academic position in Europe. He was about thirty-one. It was not an easy time: the older teachers at Paris were trying to drive the friars out of the university, and there were riots. Thomas had to be protected by royal soldiers.

His job had three parts: lecturing on the Bible, preaching, and **disputation**. In a disputation, a question was put forward, for example "Is it lawful to sell a thing for more than it is worth?" Students and teachers offered arguments on both sides. Then the master gave his answer and replied to every objection. This method shaped how Thomas wrote everything.

### The Summa

Over the next fifteen years, in Paris, Naples, Orvieto, Rome and Paris again, Thomas wrote at astonishing speed. He wrote the ***Summa contra Gentiles*** ("Summary against the Unbelievers"), meant to help missionaries explain Christianity to Muslims and Jews using reason that all could share. He wrote commentaries on the Bible and on a dozen of Aristotle's works.

And in **1265** he began his masterpiece, the ***Summa Theologiae*** ("Summary of Theology"). He wrote it, he said, for **beginners**. It runs to well over a million words in more than 500 questions and nearly 2,700 articles, covering God, creation, angels, human nature, happiness, emotions, habits, virtues, law, grace, politics, economics, war, sex, Christ and the sacraments.

His secretaries reported that he could dictate to three or four of them at once, on different subjects, and that sometimes he went on dictating in his sleep. He was so absorbed in thought that at a dinner with **King Louis IX of France**, he suddenly struck the table and said, "That settles the Manichees!" The king, rather than being offended, called a secretary to take down the idea.

### The second Paris battle

From **1268 to 1272**, Thomas was back in Paris, fighting on two fronts at once. On one side were the conservative theologians, mostly Franciscans, who followed Augustine and thought Thomas gave far too much to Aristotle. On the other side were radical teachers in the arts faculty, led by **Siger of Brabant**, who followed Aristotle and his commentator **Ibn Rushd** even where they seemed to contradict the faith. These were the "Latin Averroists".

Thomas wrote against both. In **1270** he published *On the Unity of the Intellect, against the Averroists*. Against the conservatives, he wrote *On the Eternity of the World*, arguing that reason alone cannot prove the world had a beginning; Christians know it only by faith.

### Straw

In **1272**, he returned to Naples to set up a Dominican house of studies. On **6 December 1273**, while saying Mass, something happened to him. Afterwards, he stopped writing. The *Summa Theologiae* was left unfinished. When his friend and secretary **Reginald of Piperno** begged him to continue, Thomas answered:

"I can write no more. All that I have written seems like straw compared to what I have seen."

Some believe he had a mystical experience. Others think he may have suffered a stroke or a breakdown from overwork. Perhaps both.

### Death

Early in **1274**, the Pope called him to a church council at Lyon. On the way, he struck his head on a branch, fell ill, and was taken to the Cistercian abbey of **Fossanova**, south of Rome. He died there on **7 March 1274**, aged about forty-nine.

Three years later, in **1277**, the Bishop of Paris condemned 219 propositions taught at the university, and a few of them were close to Thomas's own views. For a time his reputation was under a cloud. But in **1323** he was declared a saint, and in **1567** a "Doctor of the Church". In **1879**, Pope Leo XIII made his philosophy the foundation of Catholic education worldwide.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Faith and reason are partners

**The idea in one line:** Reason and faith both come from God, so they cannot truly conflict; reason can prove some truths about God, faith reveals others, and each helps the other.

Thomas divided truths about God into two kinds.

**Some can be known by reason alone**, by anyone, of any religion: that God exists, that God is one, that God is good. Aristotle, a pagan, had reached some of these truths. Thomas called them the **"preambles of faith"**: things reason can show, which prepare the way for faith.

**Others can be known only through revelation**: that God is a Trinity, that God became human in Christ. Reason cannot prove these. But it can show that they are not contradictory, and answer objections to them.

Why, then, did God reveal even the truths that reason can reach? Because, Thomas said, only a few people would have the time, ability and training to reach them by reason, it would take a long time, and they would be mixed with errors. Revelation gives everyone the truth they need.

His most famous principle is: **"Grace does not destroy nature but perfects it."** Faith does not cancel reason; it completes it, the way a teacher does not destroy a student's mind but develops it.

And if reason seems to contradict faith? Then, Thomas said, as Ibn Rushd had said before him, someone has made a mistake. Either the argument is faulty, or the faith has been misunderstood.

**Why it matters:** This partnership allowed the Catholic Church, at its best, to support science and universities. Many early scientists, including Copernicus and the Belgian priest **Georges Lemaître**, who first proposed what we now call the Big Bang theory, were churchmen who saw no conflict between their faith and their research.

**A common misunderstanding:** Thomas did not think reason could prove everything in the faith, nor that faith was a substitute for thinking. He gave each its own territory, and insisted they meet in peace.

### Idea 2: Five ways to God

**The idea in one line:** Starting from simple facts about the world, like change and cause, reason can conclude that there must be a first, uncaused source of all things.

Near the beginning of the *Summa Theologiae*, Thomas sets out his famous **Five Ways**: five short arguments for the existence of God. Each starts from something we observe in the world.

1. **Change (motion).** Things change. But nothing changes itself; it is changed by something else. This chain cannot go back forever, so there must be a **first unmoved mover**.
2. **Cause.** Every effect has a cause. The chain of causes cannot go back forever, so there must be a **first cause**.
3. **Contingency.** Things in the world can exist or not exist; they come and go. If everything were like this, at some time nothing would have existed, and nothing could ever have begun. So there must be something that **exists necessarily**.
4. **Degrees.** We find things more or less good, true and noble. This implies a **maximum**, a source of all goodness.
5. **Purpose (governance).** Things without intelligence, like plants, act regularly for an end, as an arrow moves towards a target. Something intelligent must **direct them**, as an archer directs the arrow.

"And this," he ends each argument, "everyone understands to be God."

Think of dominoes falling in a long line. Each falls because the one before it fell. But someone, or something, had to push the first one. Thomas's arguments follow a similar intuition, though his real point is subtler: he is not mainly asking what happened at the beginning of time, but what keeps the whole chain of causes in existence **right now**.

**Why it matters:** The Five Ways are still debated in every introductory philosophy course in the world. Whatever you think of them, they show how to reason from ordinary experience to the deepest questions.

**A common misunderstanding:** Many people think the Five Ways try to prove the universe had a beginning. They don't. Thomas thought reason could not prove that. Even an eternal universe, he argued, would need a first cause to keep it existing, the way a light needs a source as long as it shines.

### Idea 3: Natural law, written in human nature

**The idea in one line:** There is a moral law built into human nature, which every person can know by reason, and which stands above the laws of kings and governments.

Thomas's account of law is perhaps his most influential idea. He defined law as **"an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and promulgated"**. Every law, to be a law, must be reasonable, aim at the good of all, be made by the proper authority, and be made known.

He then described four kinds of law:

1. **Eternal law:** God's plan for the whole universe.
2. **Natural law:** the part of the eternal law that human beings can know by reason, because it is built into our nature.
3. **Human law:** the particular laws made by governments, which apply natural law to specific circumstances.
4. **Divine law:** the law revealed in scripture, to guide us to our final end.

The first principle of natural law is simple: **"Good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided."** But what is good? Thomas says we can read it from our natural inclinations. Like all living things, we seek to **preserve our lives**. Like animals, we seek to **have and raise children**. And as rational beings, we seek to **know the truth** (including about God) and to **live in society** with others. From these follow moral rules: do not kill the innocent; care for your children; do not harm those you live among; seek knowledge; avoid ignorance.

Natural law is the same for everyone, everywhere. A Hindu farmer, a Muslim trader and a Christian priest can all know it, without any scripture, by thinking about what it means to be human.

**Why it matters:** Natural law gave the world a powerful idea: **there is a standard of right and wrong above the state**. When a government passes a law, we can ask not just "Is this the law?" but "Is this law just?" This idea lies behind human rights, international law, the trials of Nazi leaders at Nuremberg, and King's letter from Birmingham.

**A common misunderstanding:** Natural law is not the same as "whatever is natural is good" (disease is natural, after all). It means what is good for human beings given what they are, as discovered by reason.

### Idea 4: An unjust law is no law at all

**The idea in one line:** A human law that contradicts natural law or the common good is not truly a law, and does not bind conscience.

Thomas repeated a saying of **Augustine**: "An unjust law seems to be no law at all." He explained it like this. Human law gets its force from natural law. A human law that contradicts natural law is "no longer a law but a perversion of law".

A law can be unjust in several ways: if it aims at the ruler's private benefit instead of the common good; if the lawmaker goes beyond their authority; or if it places burdens on people unequally. Such laws, Thomas said, "do not bind in conscience".

But Thomas was cautious. Even an unjust law might sometimes be obeyed, he said, "in order to avoid scandal or disturbance". Not every injustice justifies breaking the law, because disorder can cause greater harm. Only laws that command something against God's law, like worshipping idols, must never be obeyed.

**Why it matters:** This idea has inspired resistance to unjust power for centuries. King's *Letter from Birmingham Jail* quoted it directly. When **Gandhi** broke the British salt law in 1930, he made a parallel argument: a law that taxes the poor man's salt is immoral, and civil disobedience against it is a duty. Thomas would have recognised the logic, though Gandhi drew on different sources.

**A common misunderstanding:** Thomas was not a revolutionary. He believed in order, hierarchy and caution. His idea is a limit on power, not a call to break any law you dislike. The question is always whether the law truly violates justice, and whether resisting it will do more good than harm.

### Idea 5: Happiness and the virtues

**The idea in one line:** Every human being seeks happiness; we find it through the virtues, and complete it in knowing God.

Following Aristotle, Thomas asked: what is the final goal of human life? Everyone agrees it is **happiness**. But in what does happiness consist?

Thomas examines and rejects the usual candidates, one by one: **wealth** (it is only a means), **honour** and **fame** (they depend on others), **power** (it can be used badly), **bodily pleasure** and **health** (we share them with animals, and they pass). None can satisfy the human heart, which wants the complete and lasting good. Like Augustine, he concludes that only God can fully satisfy us, and that perfect happiness is the **vision of God**.

But on the way, we can achieve a real, imperfect happiness in this life through the **virtues**: good habits of mind and character. He accepted Aristotle's four **cardinal virtues**:

- **Prudence:** practical wisdom, knowing what to do in a particular situation.
- **Justice:** giving everyone what they are due.
- **Courage (fortitude):** facing danger and difficulty for the sake of the good.
- **Temperance:** self-control in pleasures.

To these he added three **theological virtues**, given by grace: **faith, hope and charity (love)**. Charity, he said, is the "form" of all the virtues: love gives all the others their life.

**Why it matters:** Virtue ethics, the idea that morality is mainly about building good character, not just following rules, has made a strong comeback in modern philosophy, psychology and business ethics. Thomas is one of its great sources.

**A common misunderstanding:** Thomas did not despise earthly happiness. He thought friendship, health, knowledge and good work are real goods. He only denied that any of them could be the final goal.

### Idea 6: Property, need and the just price

**The idea in one line:** Private property is useful, but the goods of the earth are meant for everyone; in extreme need, taking what you need is not theft.

Thomas defended private property for practical reasons: people take better care of what is their own; there is less confusion; and there is more peace when everyone knows what is theirs.

But he insisted that the **use** of property must serve the common good. Whatever we have beyond our needs, he said, is owed to the poor. And he drew a striking conclusion: **"In cases of need all things are common property."** If someone is in urgent, extreme need, and there is no other way to save their life, taking another's property is **not theft**, because need has made it common.

He also wrote about economics. He condemned selling something for more than it is worth, or using a buyer's ignorance to cheat them. He followed the church in condemning **usury** (charging interest on loans), though later thinkers would revise this.

**Why it matters:** These ideas shaped Catholic social teaching, which influenced Christian Democratic parties in Europe, labour movements in Latin America and debates about the duties of the rich. Thomas's principle that "need makes things common" resembles Gandhi's idea of **trusteeship**, that the rich hold their wealth in trust for society.

**A common misunderstanding:** Thomas was not a socialist. He defended private property. But he limited it with a duty to the common good, which makes him uncomfortable for both the free-market right and the socialist left.

### Idea 7: How to argue

**The idea in one line:** Before you answer a question, state the strongest objections to your own view, then answer them one by one.

Every article of the *Summa* follows the same pattern, taken from the university disputation:

1. **The question:** for example, "Whether God exists?"
2. **Objections:** the best arguments **against** the answer Thomas will give. For "Whether God exists?", the first objection is the problem of evil: if God existed, there would be no evil; but there is evil; so God does not exist.
3. **"On the contrary":** a short statement from an authority supporting the other side.
4. **"I answer that":** Thomas's own reasoned answer.
5. **Replies to the objections:** an answer to each objection in turn.

Notice what this means. Thomas begins **every** discussion by stating the opposing case, often more clearly and strongly than his opponents had. He takes every objection seriously and answers it directly.

**Why it matters:** Imagine if every television debate, social media argument or political speech followed this rule: first state your opponent's best argument, fairly, then reply. Much of what passes for public argument today would collapse. Thomas's method is a model of intellectual honesty.

**A common misunderstanding:** The *Summa*'s method can look dry and mechanical. But it reflects a deep moral attitude: the belief that the truth can survive any honest objection, and that your opponent deserves to be heard at their best.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Aristotle: the Philosopher

Thomas called Aristotle simply **"the Philosopher"**, as if there were no other. He wrote commentaries on a dozen of his works, and built his own philosophy on Aristotle's ideas of nature, cause, purpose, the soul as the form of the body, and the virtues. But he corrected Aristotle where he thought faith required it: the world had a beginning, God creates and knows each individual thing, and the soul survives death.

### Augustine: the father

Augustine was Thomas's most quoted Christian authority. Thomas kept Augustine's theology of grace and his teaching that only God satisfies the restless heart. But where Augustine looked inward and upward, distrusting the senses, Thomas, following Aristotle, said all our knowledge begins with the senses, and that nature is good and can be understood.

### Ibn Rushd: the Commentator

Thomas relied heavily on Ibn Rushd's commentaries to read Aristotle, and called him "the Commentator". He shared Ibn Rushd's conviction that faith and reason cannot really conflict. But he wrote fiercely against Ibn Rushd's doctrine that there is one intellect for all humanity. Imagine them meeting:

*Ibn Rushd:* The intellect that grasps universal truths cannot be a bodily power. So it cannot be divided among bodies. There is one intellect for all.

*Thomas:* But it is this man who understands. I do. If the intellect were not mine, my thoughts would not be mine.

*Ibn Rushd:* Your imagination is yours. It feeds the shared intellect.

*Thomas:* Then you cannot say that Socrates thinks, any more than a wall is seeing because someone looks at its colour. And if my intellect is not mine, how can I be responsible for my choices?

### Locke: natural law, made modern

**John Locke** quotes the Anglican theologian **Richard Hooker** again and again in his *Second Treatise of Government*. Hooker's ideas of natural law and government by consent were deeply shaped by Thomas. Through this chain, Thomas's natural law became Locke's natural rights, and from Locke, the American Declaration of Independence.

### Kant: the proofs don't work

**Immanuel Kant** argued that all proofs of God's existence, including arguments from cause and design like the Five Ways, try to use reason beyond the limits of possible experience. Reason, he said, cannot prove that God exists, though it cannot disprove it either. Many modern philosophers agree with Kant; others, called **Thomists**, have defended and reworked Thomas's arguments.

### Later voices

- **Dante**, in the *Divine Comedy*, places Thomas in the heaven of the Sun, among the wise.
- **The School of Salamanca** in sixteenth-century Spain, Dominicans such as **Francisco de Vitoria**, used Thomas's natural law to argue that the native peoples of the Americas had rights that Spanish conquerors must respect. This was one of the beginnings of international law.
- **Martin Luther** attacked the use of Aristotle in theology, and with it, much of the tradition Thomas represented.
- **Jacques Maritain**, a French Thomist, was part of the UNESCO inquiry in 1947 into the philosophical foundations of human rights, as the Universal Declaration of Human Rights was being drafted.
- **Martin Luther King Jr.** quoted Thomas on unjust laws in 1963.
- **G. E. M. Anscombe** and **Alasdair MacIntyre**, twentieth-century philosophers, revived virtue ethics drawing on Aristotle and Thomas.

## See it around you

**Higher law in Indian courts.** In the **Puttaswamy** judgment of **2017**, India's Supreme Court held that privacy is a fundamental right, and several judges described it as a natural right that belongs to human beings simply because they are human, which the Constitution recognises rather than creates. That is natural-law thinking, whether or not the judges were thinking of Thomas.

**The basic structure of the Constitution.** In **Kesavananda Bharati** (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament cannot change the "basic structure" of the Constitution, even by a valid amendment. The idea that some principles stand above ordinary law-making echoes the structure Thomas described.

**Protest against unjust laws.** From the Salt March to farmers' protests to civil rights movements around the world, protesters ask Thomas's question: is this law truly for the common good?

**Just war debates.** When nations argue about whether a war is justified, they use criteria Thomas inherited from Augustine and refined: proper authority, just cause and right intention.

**Hard medical choices.** Thomas's discussion of self-defence gave rise to what philosophers call the **principle of double effect**: an act with a good aim may sometimes be allowed even if it has a foreseen bad side effect, provided the bad effect is not intended and is proportionate. Doctors use it when giving strong painkillers to dying patients, knowing they may shorten life.

**Feeding the hungry.** When people in a famine or a flood take food from a closed shop, Thomas would ask whether need had made things common.

**Catholic schools and colleges in India.** Many of India's best-known schools and colleges are run by Catholic orders. The idea that faith and serious learning belong together, which shaped them, owes a great deal to Thomas.

**Debates and quizzes.** Every good debate competition, where you must anticipate your opponent's case, is a cousin of the medieval disputation.

## Use it

**1. Steelman before you strike.** Before you argue against a view, write down its strongest objections to your own position, in their best form, as Thomas did. Then answer them. If you can't, think again.

**2. Ask whether a rule serves the common good.** At work, in your housing society or in your college, when a rule feels wrong, ask Thomas's four questions: Is it reasonable? Does it serve everyone, or only someone? Was it made by the right authority? Was it made known?

**3. Distinguish what reason can know from what you believe.** In religious or political debates, separate the claims you can support with arguments that anyone can check from the claims that rest on faith or loyalty. Be honest about which is which.

**4. Build habits, not just rules.** Thomas's virtues are habits. Choose one, such as patience, honesty or self-control, and practise it daily in small ways until it becomes second nature.

**5. Practise prudence.** Before a decision, ask: what is the right thing to do here, in this situation, with these people? General rules help, but wisdom is applying them well.

**6. Remember the poor's claim.** Thomas taught that what you have beyond your needs is owed to those in need. Decide what "beyond my needs" means for you, and act on it.

**7. Don't ask finite things for infinite happiness.** Like Augustine, Thomas warns that wealth, fame and power can't be the final goal. Ask what you are really living for.

**8. Know when to stop.** At the end, Thomas called his work straw. Even the greatest achievements are small before the truth. Hold your opinions firmly, but humbly.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Women.** Following Aristotle's biology, Thomas called woman "something defective and misbegotten" in her individual nature, and held that women are naturally subject to men because men have more reason. These views, though common in his time, shaped centuries of exclusion.

**2. Heretics.** Thomas argued that heretics who persisted after warning could be handed over to the state and executed, because corrupting the faith was worse than forging money. This argument was used to justify the Inquisition. It is one of the darkest passages in his work.

**3. Slavery and hierarchy.** He accepted a form of servitude as a consequence of sin and as useful in human society, and assumed a fixed social hierarchy as natural. The natural law he described often looked suspiciously like the customs of medieval Europe.

**4. Jews.** He accepted the church's discriminatory laws towards Jews, although he opposed forced baptism and argued that Jewish children should not be taken from their parents to be baptised.

**5. The proofs.** Philosophers from Hume and Kant onwards have argued that the Five Ways do not succeed: that the chain of causes might not need a first member; that a "first cause" need not be the God of any religion; and that apparent design in nature can be explained by evolution.

**6. "Natural" can hide prejudice.** Critics argue that natural law theory has been used to call things "unnatural" that are simply unfamiliar, especially in matters of sexuality. What counts as "natural" has often reflected the assumptions of those doing the reasoning.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought of medieval thinkers as people who simply accepted what the church told them, and of law as whatever the government says it is. You may have thought religion and reason must always be at war, and that arguing means attacking your opponent's weakest point. You might have known Aquinas only as a saint's name on a college gate.

**Now,** you can see a thinker who trusted reason so much that he built a whole system with it, and who believed that anyone, of any faith, can know the moral law by thinking about what it means to be human. You can see law as something to be judged: an ordinance of reason for the common good, or else a perversion of law. You can see arguments as things to be tested against their strongest objections. And you can see where his assumptions about women, heretics and hierarchy betrayed his own principle of reason.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A new law in the news** makes you ask not just "Is it legal?" but "Is it reasonable, and for whose good?"
- **A heated argument** reminds you to state the other side's best case first.
- **A court judgment about rights** reveals the old question of whether some rights come before the state.
- **A protest against an unjust law** looks like a debate about natural law.
- **Your habits** become the raw material of character.
- **A great achievement** reminds you of a friar who looked at his life's work and called it straw.

A silent student whom his classmates called a dumb ox spent his life building a cathedral of reason, and taught that the laws of kings must answer to a higher law written in human nature. Seven centuries later, a man in a jail cell in Alabama quoted him, and the walls of an unjust law began to fall.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Italian Dominican friar, philosopher and theologian; the greatest system-builder of the Middle Ages.
- **Context:** the rise of universities; the rediscovery of Aristotle through Arabic and Greek translations; the new orders of friars; the conflict of popes and emperors.
- **Life:** born at Roccasecca; Monte Cassino; Naples; joined the Dominicans and was held captive by his family (1244–45); "the dumb ox" under Albert the Great; master in Paris (1256); *Summa contra Gentiles* and *Summa Theologiae*; fought both conservatives and Averroists; "all that I have written seems like straw" (1273); died at Fossanova (1274); saint (1323).
- **Faith and reason:** partners; grace perfects nature; preambles of faith.
- **Five Ways:** change, cause, contingency, degrees, purpose.
- **Natural law:** a moral law in human nature, known by reason; four kinds of law.
- **Unjust law:** no law at all, a perversion of law.
- **Happiness and virtue:** the cardinal and theological virtues; final happiness in God.
- **Property:** private property limited by the common good; in extreme need, all things are common.
- **Method:** objections, "on the contrary", "I answer that", replies.
- **Criticisms:** views on women, heretics, slavery and Jews; the proofs disputed; "natural" can hide prejudice.

**Key terms**

- **Summa Theologiae:** Thomas's unfinished masterpiece, a complete summary of theology.
- **Scholasticism:** the method of medieval university teaching, based on questions, authorities and disputation.
- **Preambles of faith:** truths about God that reason can prove.
- **Five Ways:** Thomas's five arguments for the existence of God.
- **Natural law:** the moral law knowable by reason from human nature.
- **Eternal law:** God's rational plan for the universe.
- **Common good:** the good of the whole community, which law must serve.
- **Cardinal virtues:** prudence, justice, courage and temperance.
- **Double effect:** the principle that a good act with a foreseen but unintended bad effect may be allowed if the bad is proportionate.
- **Thomism:** the philosophical tradition that follows Thomas Aquinas.
