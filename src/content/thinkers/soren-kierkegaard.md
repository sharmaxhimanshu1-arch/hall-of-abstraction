## Start here

You're twenty-two. You've finished your degree. Everyone has an opinion about your life.

Your parents want you to take the government exam: secure, respectable. Your uncle says engineering jobs abroad are the real future. Your friends are all doing MBAs. Your teacher thinks you should do research. Social media is full of people your age who've started companies, travelled the world, got married, bought cars. You can read a thousand articles comparing careers, salaries, cities. You can make spreadsheets.

And yet, late at night, you feel something none of the articles can touch. A kind of dizziness. *What do I actually want? Who am I going to be? What if I choose wrong? What if I waste my one life?*

Nobody can make this choice for you. Not your parents, not the experts, not the crowd. You alone will live the life you choose. And that thought is both exciting and terrifying.

Now here's the question: is that dizzy, anxious feeling a **problem** to be fixed, by more information, more advice, a better plan? Or is it telling you something true and important about what it means to be a human being?

A strange, brilliant Danish writer in the 1840s said: **it's the second.** That anxiety is "the dizziness of freedom". It comes from standing at the edge of possibilities, knowing that you must choose and that the choice will make you who you are. No system, no expert, no crowd can relieve you of this. And the great danger of modern life is that we try to escape it: by hiding in the crowd, following what "everyone" does, living on autopilot, and never really becoming a self at all.

His name was **Søren Kierkegaard**. He is called the "father of existentialism", the philosophy that begins not with abstract questions about the universe, but with the concrete, personal question: *how should I live, as this particular person, in this one life?*

He was also a devout, troubled Christian who attacked the Church of his country for making faith too easy. And he lived a life full of secrets, melancholy, irony and one great broken love.

By the end of this page, you'll understand why his ideas speak so directly to anyone who has ever felt lost in a crowd, paralysed by choice, or afraid of becoming who they really are.

## The world he was born into

### Golden Age Copenhagen

Kierkegaard was born in **1813** in **Copenhagen**, the capital of **Denmark**. Denmark was a small kingdom, recently humbled. In 1807, the British had bombarded Copenhagen. In 1813, the year of Kierkegaard's birth, the Danish state went **bankrupt**. In 1814, Denmark lost **Norway**.

And yet this was also Denmark's "**Golden Age**" of culture: the age of the storyteller **Hans Christian Andersen**, the physicist **H.C. Ørsted** (who discovered electromagnetism), and many poets, painters and theologians. Copenhagen was a small city, about 120,000 people, where everyone who mattered knew everyone else. Kierkegaard could walk through it every day, talking to everyone, and be known by all.

### A state church

Denmark had an official **Lutheran** state church. Almost every Dane was baptised, confirmed and married in it. Being Danish and being Christian were treated as the same thing. Pastors were civil servants paid by the state. Religion was respectable, comfortable and part of everyday social life.

Kierkegaard would come to see this as a great problem. If everyone is automatically a Christian, simply by being born in Denmark, then is anyone really a Christian at all?

Think of how, in many societies, religion becomes mostly a matter of identity and ritual, which festivals you celebrate, which marriage rites you follow, what surname you carry, rather than a matter of deep personal commitment and transformation. That's what Kierkegaard saw around him.

### Hegel's system

Intellectually, Kierkegaard's world was dominated by **Hegel**, the German philosopher who had built a vast "**system**" explaining all of reality, history, religion, art, the state, as the unfolding of reason or "Spirit". Hegel's followers in Denmark, including leading theologians, claimed his system could explain and absorb even Christianity.

Kierkegaard thought this was absurd. A system can explain everything in general, he said, but it forgets the one thing that matters most: the **existing individual**, the person who has to actually live, choose, suffer and die.

## The life, as a story

### The father and the curse

Søren Aabye Kierkegaard was born on **5 May 1813**, the youngest of seven children. His father, **Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard**, was fifty-six when Søren was born.

The father's story is crucial. As a poor boy, he had herded sheep on the bleak heaths of **Jutland**, cold, hungry and lonely. One day, according to the family story, he climbed a hill and **cursed God** for his miserable life. Soon afterwards, he was sent to Copenhagen, became an apprentice to a cloth merchant, and grew very rich. He retired at forty.

But he never forgot the curse. He became deeply religious and deeply **melancholic**, convinced that God would punish him. And there was a second secret: after his first wife died, he married their servant, **Ane Lund**, and their first child was born only a few months after the wedding. He believed he had sinned again.

When five of his seven children died, most before reaching their mid-thirties, as did his second wife, the father became convinced that a curse lay on the family: that none of his children would live beyond the age of Jesus at his death, thirty-three or thirty-four.

Søren absorbed this dark religiosity. His father took him on imaginary walks, holding his hand and pacing up and down the room, describing streets and scenes in such vivid detail that the boy felt he was really travelling. He trained the boy's imagination and his skill in argument. He also loaded him with his own guilt and gloom.

Around **1835**, Søren seems to have discovered something about his father that shattered him. He wrote in his journal of "**the great earthquake**", a "terrible upheaval" which suddenly forced on him a new interpretation of all phenomena. Scholars still argue about exactly what he discovered: the curse, the sexual sin, or something else.

### The young dandy

At the **University of Copenhagen**, Søren studied **theology**, as his father wanted, but took years over it. Instead, he lived the life of a fashionable young man: he went to the theatre and cafés, wore fine clothes, ran up debts, which his father paid, and became famous for his brilliant, biting wit.

Yet in **August 1835**, aged twenty-two, on a summer trip to the fishing village of **Gilleleje** on the coast, he wrote in his journal one of the most famous passages in the history of philosophy:

"The crucial thing is to find a purpose, to see what it really is God wills that I shall do; the crucial thing is to find a truth which is truth for me, to find the idea for which I am willing to live and die."

Notice what he's saying. He doesn't want objective knowledge, facts, theories, systems. He wants a truth that he can **live**, that gives his own life meaning. This is the seed of existentialism.

His father died in **August 1838**. Søren, reconciled with him shortly before, now felt he had to fulfil his father's wishes. He finished his theology degree in 1840.

### Regine

In **1837**, Søren met **Regine Olsen**, a fourteen-year-old girl from a respectable Copenhagen family. He was twenty-four. He waited, and in **September 1840**, when she was eighteen, he proposed. She accepted.

Almost immediately, he regretted it.

Why? He never fully explained. He wrote that he was "a thousand years too old" for her. He felt his melancholy, his secret guilt, his sense of a religious calling made him unfit for marriage. He couldn't share his inner life with her. He feared he would make her miserable.

So he did something cruel in order, he thought, to be kind. He tried to make her **break off** the engagement by behaving coldly, so that she would hate him and be free of him. She didn't. She begged him to stay. In **October 1841**, he returned her ring. Her father begged him to reconsider. He refused. He then fled to Berlin.

He loved her for the rest of his life. He wrote about her constantly, in disguised forms, in his books. He later wrote in his journal, "If I had had faith, I would have stayed with Regine." She married a former tutor, **Johan Frederik Schlegel**, in 1847, who later became governor of the Danish West Indies. When Kierkegaard died, he left everything he owned to her. (She declined most of it.)

### The flood of books

In 1841, Kierkegaard defended his dissertation, ***The Concept of Irony, with Continual Reference to Socrates***. Then he went to **Berlin** to hear lectures by the famous philosopher **Schelling**, who promised to overcome Hegel. Kierkegaard was excited at first, then bored, and came home.

Then came an extraordinary explosion of writing. In about **five years** (1843–1846), he published a stream of books, many of them masterpieces:

- ***Either/Or*** (1843): two volumes, one written by an aesthete, a pleasure-seeker, and one by an ethical judge.
- ***Fear and Trembling*** (1843): on Abraham's willingness to sacrifice his son.
- ***Repetition*** (1843).
- ***Philosophical Fragments*** (1844).
- ***The Concept of Anxiety*** (1844).
- ***Stages on Life's Way*** (1845).
- ***Concluding Unscientific Postscript*** (1846): a huge, mocking attack on Hegelian philosophy.

### The masks

Here's the strange thing: most of these books were published under **pseudonyms**, fake names, each representing a different character with a different point of view. *Either/Or* was "edited" by **Victor Eremita** (Victor the Hermit). *Fear and Trembling* was by **Johannes de silentio** (John of Silence). *The Concept of Anxiety* was by **Vigilius Haufniensis** (the Watchman of Copenhagen). The *Fragments* and *Postscript* were by **Johannes Climacus** (John the Climber). Later, *The Sickness unto Death* was by **Anti-Climacus**.

Why? Kierkegaard said he wanted to practise "**indirect communication**". He didn't want to tell readers what to believe. He wanted to show them different ways of life from the inside, so that they would have to choose for themselves. Like Socrates, who claimed to know nothing and asked questions, he wanted to make readers think, not to hand them answers.

At the same time, under his own name, he published many religious "**Upbuilding Discourses**", sermons of a kind, meant to speak directly to the reader's soul.

Everyone in Copenhagen knew who the real author was. Kierkegaard would show himself in the streets and theatre lobbies every evening, so people would think he was an idle man about town, while he wrote all night.

### The Corsair affair

In **1846**, Kierkegaard picked a fight with ***The Corsair***, a popular satirical magazine that mocked public figures. He challenged it to attack him. It did, for months, with cartoons of him with spindly legs and trousers of unequal length, and jokes about his name (*kierkegaard* means "churchyard" in Danish).

It was a disaster for him. Street boys mocked him; strangers laughed at him. He could no longer enjoy his walks through the city. He felt he had been made the object of the crowd's contempt.

But the experience deepened his thinking about the "**crowd**" and "**the public**", and about what it means to be an individual in a society ruled by gossip and media. His short book ***The Present Age*** (1846) is a prophetic critique of mass society.

### The attack on Christendom

In his later years, Kierkegaard wrote more directly religious works, like ***Works of Love*** (1847) and ***The Sickness unto Death*** (1849). He increasingly saw his task as reminding people of what Christianity really demands.

In **1854**, the head of the Danish church, **Bishop Mynster**, an old friend of his father, died. At his funeral, the theologian **Hans Lassen Martensen**, Mynster's successor, called Mynster a "**witness to the truth**", one of the line of true witnesses going back to the apostles.

Kierkegaard exploded. A "witness to the truth", in the New Testament, was someone who suffered and died for the faith. Mynster had been a comfortable, worldly official of a state church. To call him a witness to the truth was, Kierkegaard said, a lie that summed up everything wrong with "**Christendom**", a society where Christianity had become respectable, easy and meaningless.

He launched a furious public **attack on the Danish Church**, first in newspaper articles, then in his own pamphlet, ***The Moment***. He said that official Christianity had made a fool of God. He urged people to stop taking part in official church services, so as not to be complicit in the deception.

### Death

In **October 1855**, in the middle of this campaign, he collapsed in the street. He was taken to Frederiks Hospital. He had spent almost all his inherited fortune on publishing his books.

His old friend **Emil Boesen**, a pastor, visited him in hospital. Kierkegaard said he would gladly receive communion, but only from a layman, not from a pastor, an official of the state church. He died on **11 November 1855**, aged forty-two. He had outlived the "curse" by several years.

At his funeral, his nephew caused a scene, protesting that the church was burying a man who had attacked it.

For decades he was little known outside Denmark. Then, in the early twentieth century, he was translated into German, and later into English and French, and was rediscovered as one of the most original thinkers of the nineteenth century, the father of existentialism and a deep influence on modern theology, psychology and literature.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Truth as subjectivity

**The idea in one line:** The most important truths are not objective facts to be known, but truths you must live and commit yourself to.

In the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, Kierkegaard (writing as Johannes Climacus) makes a famous claim: "**subjectivity is truth**".

This sounds alarming, as if he were saying that truth is just personal opinion. But that's not what he means.

Think about two kinds of questions:

1. **Objective questions:** What is the boiling point of water? When did the Mughal empire begin? How does a car engine work? Here, it doesn't matter who you are or how you feel. The answer is the same for everyone. You can learn it, check it, look it up.

2. **Existential questions:** What should I do with my life? Should I marry this person? What do I believe about God? How should I face my death? Here, knowing facts isn't enough. Even if you had all the information in the world, you'd still have to decide, commit and live with your choice. The answer must become **yours**.

Kierkegaard's point is that the second kind of question is the one that matters most for living, and that modern philosophy, science and theology were obsessed with the first kind. A professor can lecture brilliantly about ethics or religion, but if his own life is untouched by what he says, he has missed the point.

Kierkegaard said **how** you hold a belief matters as much as **what** you believe. He gave a striking example. Suppose one person, living in a Christian society, goes to church and prays to the true God, but with a false, half-hearted spirit. And suppose another person, in a non-Christian land, prays to an idol, but with the whole passion of infinity. Which one, he asks, has more truth? He suggests: the second one prays in truth to God, though he worships an idol; the first prays in untruth to the true God, and so in truth worships an idol.

Compare this with the **Bhakti** tradition in India. Kabir mocked those who counted beads and performed rituals without inner devotion: what use is turning the rosary if the mind isn't turned? Mirabai's devotion to Krishna was utterly personal and passionate, against the conventions of her royal family. For the Bhakti saints, as for Kierkegaard, the relationship to the divine is not a matter of ritual or doctrine alone, but of the whole inner self.

**Why it matters:** This idea helps explain why people can know a great deal and still live badly, and why knowing "the right answer" doesn't change us. You can know that smoking kills, that anger harms relationships, that exercise is good. Unless that knowledge becomes yours, appropriated, committed, lived, it does nothing.

**A common misunderstanding:** Kierkegaard did not deny objective truth. He didn't think that the boiling point of water depends on your feelings. He said that for the deepest questions of life, the **relationship** of the person to the truth is what matters most, and that this relationship involves passion, commitment and risk.

### Idea 2: The single individual and the crowd

**The idea in one line:** To become a true self, you must stand before your life as a single individual, not lose yourself in the crowd.

Kierkegaard wrote: "**The crowd is untruth.**"

What did he mean? Not that most people are wrong, or that majorities are always mistaken. He meant that being part of a crowd lets each person escape **responsibility**. In a crowd, nobody is individually responsible. "Everyone is doing it." "That's how things are done." "Everyone says so."

Think of a mob that attacks someone. Each individual in the mob might never have done such a thing alone. But in the crowd, responsibility dissolves. Or think of social media, where thousands of people pile on to mock someone, each one adding just one comment, each feeling it doesn't really matter.

Or think of the quieter crowds of daily life: following a career because "everyone in our family does engineering", dressing a certain way because "everyone does", holding political opinions because "people like us" hold them. In each case, you hand your choice over to the crowd, and avoid the anxiety of choosing for yourself.

Kierkegaard said that before God, and before your own life, you stand alone, as "**that single individual**". He even wanted the phrase "That Individual" on his gravestone. Not because he wanted people to be isolated or selfish, but because only an individual can take responsibility, make a commitment, love, believe, become a self.

In ***The Present Age***, he described a society dominated by "**the public**", an abstract, faceless audience created by the press. The public has opinions on everything but takes responsibility for nothing. It "levels" everyone down, mocking anyone who stands out, discouraging passion and commitment. People talk endlessly, reflect endlessly, but never act. He described the age as one of "**reflection**" without passion: everyone discusses, nobody decides.

This was written in 1846. Now think of today: twenty-four-hour news, social media, comment sections, trending outrage, where everyone has opinions about everything, and nobody is responsible for anything. Kierkegaard looks like a prophet.

**Why it matters:** This idea gives us a way to resist the pressure to conform, not by becoming rebels for the sake of it (which is just another crowd), but by asking: *have I actually chosen this, or am I just going along?*

**A common misunderstanding:** Kierkegaard wasn't against community or other people. He wrote a whole book, *Works of Love*, about loving one's neighbour. His point was that true community is made of individuals who each take responsibility, not of a crowd in which individuals dissolve.

### Idea 3: The three stages of life

**The idea in one line:** People can live in three basic ways, the aesthetic (pleasure and interest), the ethical (duty and commitment), and the religious (faith), and moving between them requires a leap.

This is one of Kierkegaard's most famous and useful ideas. He described three "**stages**" or "**spheres**" of existence. They are not just stages of age, like childhood and adulthood, but fundamental ways of living.

**1. The aesthetic stage.** The aesthete lives for **pleasure**, **interest** and **variety**. He avoids boredom at all costs. He wants every moment to be interesting, every experience new. He doesn't commit, because commitment means giving up possibilities.

In *Either/Or*, the aesthete's papers include "**The Rotation Method**", an essay explaining how to avoid boredom: like a farmer rotating crops, keep changing your pleasures; never get too attached to anything; never make promises; don't get married; don't take an official post. Avoid friendship that ties you down. Keep everything light.

The most famous aesthetic text is "**The Seducer's Diary**", in which a man cleverly seduces a young woman and then abandons her, enjoying the game more than the person.

Does this sound familiar? Think of someone endlessly scrolling for the next interesting video, swiping on dating apps without committing, switching jobs, hobbies and friends whenever they get boring, collecting experiences like trophies. Kierkegaard would say this is the aesthetic life.

Its hidden problem is **despair**. The aesthete is never really anyone. He has no continuity, no self, only a series of moods. When the pleasures fade, he's left with emptiness and boredom. Kierkegaard wrote that boredom is "the root of all evil".

**2. The ethical stage.** The second volume of *Either/Or* is written by **Judge William**, a married civil servant, who writes long letters to the aesthete urging him to choose. The ethical person **commits**: to marriage, to a profession, to duties, to a community. He takes responsibility for his life as a whole. He becomes someone with a stable character.

The judge's key point is this: the most important thing is not to choose the right thing, but to **choose** at all, to choose yourself, to take responsibility. "Either/or": you must choose, and in choosing, you become a self.

Think of someone who decides to take their responsibilities seriously: who marries and stays faithful, who becomes a good doctor or teacher, who looks after their parents, who keeps their word. The ethical life has a depth the aesthetic life lacks.

**3. The religious stage.** But even the ethical life has limits. The ethical person may become proud of his own goodness; or may face guilt that his own efforts can't remove; or may encounter situations that duties can't resolve. The religious stage is a personal relationship with God, in which the individual recognises his own limitations, sin and dependence, and commits himself in **faith**. For Kierkegaard, this was Christianity: not as a social custom, but as a terrifying, transforming personal relationship.

How do you move from one stage to another? Not by reasoning, Kierkegaard said. You can't argue someone into commitment or faith. It requires a **leap**, a decision of the whole person. (The phrase "leap of faith" is commonly associated with him, though his own term was simply "the leap".)

Think of the Hindu ashrama system: **brahmacharya** (student), **grihastha** (householder), **vanaprastha** (retreat), **sannyasa** (renunciation). Or of the Gita's paths of karma (action), jnana (knowledge) and bhakti (devotion). These also describe different modes of life. But Kierkegaard's stages are not tied to age, and you don't automatically pass through them. Many people live in the aesthetic stage all their lives.

**Why it matters:** The three stages give us a powerful mirror. Which stage am I mostly living in? Am I just chasing interesting experiences? Have I committed to anything? What would it mean to go deeper?

**A common misunderstanding:** The stages are not simply "bad, better, best". Each contains something valuable. The aesthetic stage contains beauty, imagination and enjoyment; the ethical keeps these but grounds them in commitment. And Kierkegaard didn't think the religious stage abolished ethics; rather, it placed ethics within a deeper relationship.

### Idea 4: Fear and Trembling, the story of Abraham

**The idea in one line:** True faith is not comfortable; it may require a person to go beyond what society and even ethics can understand.

***Fear and Trembling*** is a short, intense, unsettling book about one story: **Abraham and Isaac**, from the Bible (Genesis 22). God commands Abraham to take his beloved son Isaac, for whom he had waited all his life, up a mountain and sacrifice him. Abraham obeys. He takes Isaac, the wood and the knife, and climbs Mount Moriah. At the last moment, an angel stops him, and a ram is sacrificed instead.

Preachers in Kierkegaard's day praised Abraham as the "father of faith" in comfortable Sunday sermons. Kierkegaard (writing as Johannes de silentio) asks: **do you understand what you are praising?** If a man in your congregation went home after the sermon and tried to do what Abraham did, you'd call him a murderer and a madman. Abraham's faith is **terrifying**.

Here's the problem. **Ethically**, Abraham's duty as a father is clear: to love and protect his son. The ethical is the **universal**: rules that apply to everyone and can be explained to everyone. But Abraham's faith demands that he go beyond the ethical, which Kierkegaard calls the "**teleological suspension of the ethical**": setting aside universal ethics for a higher purpose (a "telos", goal).

And Abraham **cannot explain** himself. He cannot tell Sarah, Isaac or anyone why he is doing this. Anyone who heard would think him insane. He is completely alone, in "fear and trembling".

Kierkegaard contrasts two figures:

- The **knight of infinite resignation**, who gives up what he loves most, in pain, accepting that he'll never have it. (Think of a man who loves a princess he can never marry, and resigns himself to that loss, finding peace in eternal love.) This is admirable, and Kierkegaard thought it was within human power.
- The **knight of faith**, who gives up everything, and **at the same time** believes he will get it back, "by virtue of the absurd". Abraham gives up Isaac, and yet believes that God will give Isaac back to him in this life. This double movement, Kierkegaard says, is beyond understanding.

He says the knight of faith might look completely ordinary, like a tax collector, enjoying his dinner and walking home, with nothing outwardly special about him. Faith is inward.

(Many readers think Kierkegaard was also writing about **Regine**: he had given her up, as Abraham gave up Isaac, but he had not had the faith to believe he could still have her.)

**Why it matters:** *Fear and Trembling* raises a question that has haunted modern thought: **can there be anything higher than ethics?** And if so, how do we tell the difference between a true knight of faith and a dangerous fanatic? People who commit terrible acts in the name of God also believe they're obeying a higher command. Kierkegaard knew this danger, which is why the book is full of fear and trembling, not confidence.

Compare **Arjuna** in the Bhagavad Gita. He stands on the battlefield, facing his own relatives and teachers, and refuses to fight: killing family is surely wrong. Krishna tells him that he must act according to a higher duty, without attachment to the fruits of action. Like Abraham, Arjuna faces a terrible conflict between what ordinary ethics demands and what a higher calling requires. Gandhi read the Gita as an allegory of the inner struggle, not as a command to physical violence, which is one way of handling the danger Kierkegaard sensed.

**A common misunderstanding:** Kierkegaard was not recommending that anyone kill their child, or break ethical rules whenever they feel a divine calling. He was showing how terrifying and paradoxical real faith is, and attacking the comfortable religion that praises Abraham without understanding him. The book is meant to disturb, not to provide rules.

### Idea 5: Anxiety, the dizziness of freedom

**The idea in one line:** Anxiety is not just a disorder; it is the feeling of standing before your own freedom and possibilities.

In ***The Concept of Anxiety***, Kierkegaard distinguished **fear** from **anxiety** (in Danish, *Angest*; often translated "dread" or "angst").

**Fear** has an object. You fear a snake, a dog, an exam, a boss.

**Anxiety** has no definite object. It's a vague, unsettling feeling about... nothing in particular. About possibility itself.

He gave an image: a person standing at the edge of a cliff. You look down and feel dizzy. Partly you fear falling. But partly you feel something stranger: the awareness that you **could jump**. Nothing is stopping you. That awareness of possibility is dizzying.

"Anxiety is the dizziness of freedom," he wrote.

Think about your own experience. On the night before a big decision, choosing a college, accepting a job, proposing marriage, moving to a new city, you feel anxious. Not about a specific danger, but about the openness of the future, the fact that you must choose, and that whatever you choose, you'll become someone different. That's Kierkegaardian anxiety.

Kierkegaard said that anxiety, though painful, is also **educational**. A person who has learned to be anxious in the right way has learned the most important thing. Anxiety shows you that you are free and responsible. It can lead you either to despair or to faith, to running away into the crowd or to becoming yourself.

**Why it matters:** This idea transformed psychology and philosophy. Existentialists like **Heidegger** and **Sartre** built on it. Psychologists like **Rollo May** developed "existential psychotherapy", which treats anxiety not only as a symptom to eliminate but as a signal of the deep questions of human life: freedom, death, meaning, responsibility.

Today, anxiety is everywhere: among students facing competitive exams, young professionals facing choices, people overwhelmed by options. Medical anxiety disorders are real and need proper treatment. But Kierkegaard reminds us that some anxiety is part of being human, a sign that we're free, and that it can be a teacher, not just an enemy.

**A common misunderstanding:** Kierkegaard wasn't saying that clinical anxiety disorders are good, or that people suffering from panic attacks should simply "embrace" them. He was describing a basic feature of human existence, the experience of freedom and possibility, which underlies even ordinary decision-making. If your anxiety is overwhelming, seeing a doctor or counsellor is wise; Kierkegaard's point is about the anxiety everyone meets at the edge of choice.

### Idea 6: Despair, the sickness unto death

**The idea in one line:** Despair is the failure to become yourself, and it can be present even in people who feel fine.

In ***The Sickness unto Death*** (written as Anti-Climacus), Kierkegaard analyses **despair**. The title comes from the Gospel of John, where Jesus says that Lazarus's sickness "is not unto death". Despair, Kierkegaard says, is a sickness of the spirit worse than death: the inability to become oneself, and the inability even to die from it.

He gives a complex definition of the **self**: "a relation that relates itself to itself", a synthesis of opposites: the **finite** and the **infinite** (limits and possibilities), the **temporal** and the **eternal**, **necessity** and **freedom**. Despair occurs when these get out of balance.

For example:

- **Despair of infinitude**: living entirely in fantasy and possibility, never acting, losing oneself in dreams. (The person who always plans to write a novel, start a business, change their life, but never does anything.)
- **Despair of finitude**: losing oneself entirely in the everyday, in conventions, in being like everyone else, never imagining anything more. (The person who becomes a "copy, a number, a mass-man".)
- **Despair of possibility**: everything seems possible, but nothing becomes actual.
- **Despair of necessity**: nothing seems possible; everything is fixed and fated.

Kierkegaard's most striking claim is that **most people are in despair without knowing it**. They seem happy and successful. They are busy, they have families and careers. But they have never become a self; they live as the crowd lives, and they've never faced the question of who they are. "The specific character of despair is precisely this: it is unaware of being despair."

He describes two deeper forms:

- **In despair not willing to be oneself**: wanting to be someone else, escaping from oneself.
- **In despair willing to be oneself**: defiantly trying to create oneself entirely by one's own power, refusing any dependence on anything beyond.

The cure, for Kierkegaard, was **faith**: "in relating itself to itself and in willing to be itself, the self rests transparently in the power that established it". For him, that power was God.

**Why it matters:** This analysis anticipates much of modern psychology: the idea that people can be "fine" on the surface while empty inside, the importance of authenticity and self-acceptance, the danger of living others' lives. The phrase "**quiet desperation**" from Henry David Thoreau captures a similar thought.

**A common misunderstanding:** Kierkegaard's "despair" is not just feeling sad or depressed. It's a spiritual condition, a failure to become oneself, that can coexist with feeling quite cheerful.

### Idea 7: Against the System

**The idea in one line:** No philosophical system can capture the reality of an existing human being who must live, choose and die.

Kierkegaard's great intellectual enemy was the **Hegelian system**, which claimed to explain everything: history, religion, art, ethics, the state, all as stages in the self-development of Spirit.

Kierkegaard mocked it mercilessly. He said that the Hegelian philosopher builds an enormous palace of thought, but then lives in a shed beside it, or in the doghouse. That is, the system explains everything except the life of the person who built it. He can explain world history, but he can't tell you how to face your own death.

He also pointed out that a system can only be completed at the end of history, looking backwards. But we're living in the middle of history, forwards. Hence his famous journal entry:

"It is quite true what philosophy says: that life must be understood backwards. But then one forgets the other principle: that it must be lived forwards."

Think about it. When you look back on your life, you can see patterns: why that failure led to that success, why that breakup was necessary. But when you're living it, you don't know. You have to choose without the full picture. No system can remove that uncertainty.

**Why it matters:** This critique of grand systems influenced much of twentieth-century thought: existentialism, which starts from concrete existence; postmodernism, which is suspicious of "grand narratives"; and theology, which emphasises personal encounter over abstract doctrine. It's also a warning to anyone who thinks a single theory, of economics, politics, psychology, can explain everything about human life.

**A common misunderstanding:** Kierkegaard wasn't anti-intellectual. He was a brilliant, learned philosopher who knew Hegel thoroughly. He criticised the pretension of reason to explain everything, not reason itself.

### Idea 8: Christianity versus Christendom

**The idea in one line:** True Christianity is a demanding, personal, often painful commitment, the opposite of comfortable, official, social religion.

In his last years, Kierkegaard said that "**Christendom**", a society where everyone is officially Christian, had made Christianity impossible. If everyone is a Christian by birth, then being a Christian means nothing. Christianity had become a matter of respectability, ritual and identity, not of following Christ in suffering, self-denial and love.

He was savage in his attacks. He compared official Christianity to a play performed in a theatre; to a cookbook that nobody actually cooks from; to a counterfeit coin. He said the pastors, as paid officials, had an interest in keeping Christianity comfortable, because a demanding Christianity would empty the churches and their salaries.

**Why it matters:** This critique applies to every religion. When faith becomes mainly a matter of identity, ritual, politics or social belonging, people may be fiercely loyal to their religion as a **group** while having little relationship with its deepest teachings. Think of how often religious identity is used in elections, riots and social media wars, by people who may not practise the compassion, humility or self-examination their scriptures demand. Kierkegaard asks: is that religion at all?

Indian reformers made similar critiques: the Bhakti saints attacked ritualism and caste within Hinduism; **Guru Nanak** criticised empty rituals in both Hinduism and Islam; **Vivekananda** said religion is "realisation", not talk; **Gandhi** tried to live his faith through daily practice. Kierkegaard belongs with these voices that call religion back from habit to inwardness.

**A common misunderstanding:** Kierkegaard didn't attack Christianity; he attacked what he saw as its counterfeit. He believed deeply in Christ. That's precisely why he was so angry at what he saw as the betrayal of Christ by a comfortable church.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Socrates: the hero

Kierkegaard's great model was **Socrates**. His doctoral thesis was on Socratic **irony**. Like Socrates, he saw himself as a "gadfly", stinging his complacent city into thinking. Like Socrates, he claimed not to have answers but to make people aware of their own ignorance and self-deception. Socrates said "know thyself"; Kierkegaard said "become yourself". He even wrote that his task was a Socratic one: not to make people Christians, but to show them that they were not, so that they might become so.

### Hegel: the palace and the shed

**Hegel** was his great opponent. Hegel saw the individual as a moment within the larger movement of history and Spirit. Kierkegaard insisted that the individual is not a moment in anything; the individual stands alone before God.

Imagine them arguing:

*Hegel:* Your individual is an abstraction. A person only becomes who they are within a family, a society, a state, a history. To understand yourself, you must understand the whole.

*Kierkegaard:* And having understood the whole, Professor, how will you die? Your system explains world history, but when you lie on your deathbed, which part of the system will hold your hand?

*Hegel:* Reason reveals that the real is rational. Even death has its place.

*Kierkegaard:* Exactly. Everything has its place in your system, except the person who must actually live it.

### Kant: the universal and the exception

**Kant** argued that morality consists of universal laws that apply to every rational being, and that the highest duty is to obey them. In *Fear and Trembling*, the "ethical" is very close to this Kantian universal. Abraham's faith seems to place the individual **above** the universal, which Kant would reject: Kant himself wrote that Abraham should have answered God that it was quite certain he should not kill his son, but quite uncertain that the voice was God's. Kierkegaard's book lives right in the middle of that tension.

### Nietzsche: the other prophet

**Nietzsche**, a generation later, never read Kierkegaard (a Danish critic, Georg Brandes, suggested he should, in 1888, just before Nietzsche's collapse). Yet the two are often paired as the twin founders of existentialism. Both attacked the conformity of the "herd" or "crowd"; both mocked comfortable, conventional Christianity; both insisted on the passionate individual. But Kierkegaard wanted people to become **true** Christians; Nietzsche wanted them to move **beyond** Christianity entirely.

### Sartre and Beauvoir: God removed

**Jean-Paul Sartre** took Kierkegaard's ideas of anxiety, choice and individual responsibility, and **removed God**. For Sartre, we are "condemned to be free" with no God to guide us, and must create our own values. **Simone de Beauvoir** drew on Kierkegaard's sense of human ambiguity. Kierkegaard would have seen their atheism as a form of the despair "willing to be oneself", trying to create oneself by one's own power.

### Later voices

- **Martin Heidegger** (1889–1976) developed Kierkegaard's anxiety and the critique of "the public" into his analysis of "**das Man**" (the "they"), the anonymous crowd in which we lose ourselves.
- **Karl Barth** (1886–1968) and other Protestant theologians were inspired by Kierkegaard's emphasis on the radical otherness of God and the need for personal decision.
- **Franz Kafka** read Kierkegaard and felt a deep kinship with him.
- **Ludwig Wittgenstein** called Kierkegaard "by far the most profound thinker of the last century".
- **Martin Luther King Jr.** read Kierkegaard as a student and was influenced by his idea of faith as personal commitment.

## See it around you

**The crowd online.** Whenever a mob forms on social media, attacking someone, everyone adding one more comment, nobody feeling responsible, you're seeing what Kierkegaard called "the crowd is untruth". Every "trending" outrage is a little lesson in how individuals dissolve into the public.

**Choice overload.** Dozens of career options, hundreds of products, thousands of potential partners on apps. Many young people feel paralysed: what if I choose wrong? That's Kierkegaard's "dizziness of freedom" in the age of infinite options. And the habit of keeping every option open, never committing, is his aesthetic stage.

**Quarter-life crisis.** The feeling, in your twenties, that you've done everything "right", got the degree, got the job, and yet feel empty, as if the life you're living isn't yours. That's Kierkegaardian despair: the life of finitude, lived according to others' expectations.

**Religion as identity.** When religion becomes a badge for elections, social media wars and community identity, rather than a path of inner transformation, compassion and humility, Kierkegaard's attack on "Christendom" applies to every faith.

**"Log kya kahenge?"** The constant worry about what others will say is a way of handing your life to the crowd. Kierkegaard would ask: who are these "log"? Have you ever met them? Will they live your life?

**Weddings without marriages, degrees without learning.** Elaborate ceremonies, certificates and rituals that substitute for the real commitment they're supposed to represent. Kierkegaard warned of a society full of the forms of commitment, with little of its substance.

**Therapy and meaning.** When counsellors and psychologists help people face questions of meaning, freedom and death, rather than just treating symptoms, they're often working in a tradition that goes back to Kierkegaard.

## Use it

**1. Ask: "Have I chosen this, or am I just going along?"** Look at the big things in your life: your studies, career, relationships, beliefs. For each, ask whether you have actually chosen it, or whether you drifted into it because others expected it. You might choose the same thing, but now it will be yours.

**2. Name your stage.** Honestly ask: am I mostly living in the aesthetic stage (chasing interesting experiences, avoiding commitment), the ethical (taking on responsibilities), or something deeper? What would the next step look like for me?

**3. Treat anxiety as a signal.** When you feel anxious before a big choice, don't only try to make the feeling go away. Ask what it's telling you. Often, it means that something important is at stake, that you're free, and that the choice matters. Then act. (If anxiety is overwhelming you, talk to a professional; that's a wise choice too.)

**4. Commit to something.** The aesthete keeps every door open and walks through none. Choose something, a person, a craft, a cause, a practice, and commit to it. Commitment closes some possibilities, but it is the only way to become someone.

**5. Live forwards.** You'll only understand your life backwards. Don't wait for certainty before you act; it won't come. Make the best choice you can, and trust that its meaning will become clearer later.

**6. Step out of the crowd once a day.** Before you share, like, or join in, ask: would I say or do this if I were alone, and had to take full responsibility? If not, don't.

**7. Make your beliefs inward.** Whatever your religion or philosophy, ask whether it has actually changed how you live: how you treat people, how you face suffering, what you value. If it hasn't, it's still Christendom, not Christianity, in Kierkegaard's terms.

**8. Read slowly, with yourself in mind.** Kierkegaard wanted readers to read "for themselves", applying every idea to their own lives. Try it with any serious book: ask, at every page, *what does this mean for me?*

## Where he went wrong

**1. Too much individualism?** Kierkegaard's focus on the single individual can neglect the social side of human life: how much our identity, freedom and possibilities depend on others, on society, on material conditions. A poor labourer in a village may be less free to "become himself" than a wealthy Copenhagen gentleman. Marx would say Kierkegaard ignored the conditions that make individual choice possible.

**2. The danger of the "leap".** If faith goes beyond reason and ethics, how do we tell the true knight of faith from a fanatic? People who commit violence in the name of God also believe they've received a higher command. Kierkegaard's emphasis on faith "by virtue of the absurd" can seem to open the door to irrationality.

**3. His treatment of Regine.** Many readers find his treatment of Regine troubling: he deliberately made her suffer, then turned their story into literature for decades. However tortured his reasons, he made her an object in his spiritual drama.

**4. Women.** His writings often treat women stereotypically: as immediate, natural, less reflective than men, valuable mainly as objects of love or as wives. Some passages are frankly misogynistic, reflecting the attitudes of his time.

**5. Withdrawal from politics.** Kierkegaard was largely indifferent or hostile to the democratic and social movements of his time, like the revolutions of 1848 and Denmark's new constitution of 1849. He worried that political movements were just another form of the crowd. Critics say this left his philosophy with little to say about justice and collective action.

**6. Obscurity and irony.** The pseudonyms, ironies and indirect communication make his work fascinating but often bewildering. Readers still argue about what he really thought, and whether the pseudonyms speak for him. His method can feel like a hall of mirrors.

**7. Gloom.** His relentless focus on anxiety, despair, sin and suffering can seem one-sided. Other traditions, from Aristotle's flourishing to the Buddha's equanimity, offer different pictures of a good life, where joy, peace and community play a larger part.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought of anxiety as just a problem, of choices as things to be solved with enough information, and of becoming yourself as something that happens automatically as you grow up. You may have assumed that belonging to a religion, a family or a group gives your life its meaning.

**Now,** you can see that nobody can live your life for you; that anxiety is the dizziness of standing at the edge of your own freedom; that the crowd lets you escape responsibility, and leaves you empty; and that being a self is a task, not a given. You can see the difference between a life spent collecting interesting experiences and a life built on commitment, and between faith as a social badge and faith as a whole-hearted inward relationship. And you can also see the dangers: that an individual cut off from society, or a faith cut off from reason and ethics, can go badly wrong.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A big decision** becomes something you must own, not just calculate.
- **The anxiety before choosing** feels less like an enemy and more like a sign of freedom.
- **A social media pile-on** reveals the crowd at work, and your choice to stay out of it.
- **"Everyone's doing it"** loses its force as a reason.
- **Your endless options** look less like freedom and more like a trap, until you commit.
- **Rituals and ceremonies** make you ask what inner reality they're supposed to express.
- **Looking back on your life**, you understand it a little; looking forward, you still have to live it.

Søren Kierkegaard broke his engagement, wrote under a dozen masks, was laughed at in the streets of his city, and died at forty-two after attacking his own church. He asked the single question that no system, no crowd, and no expert can answer for you: *who will you become?* Only you can answer it, by how you live.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855), Danish philosopher, theologian and writer; father of existentialism.
- **Context:** Golden Age Copenhagen, a comfortable state Lutheran church, the dominance of Hegel's system.
- **Life:** a melancholy father and a family "curse"; the broken engagement to Regine Olsen; a flood of pseudonymous books; mocked by *The Corsair*; a final attack on the Danish Church.
- **Truth as subjectivity:** the deepest truths must be lived and appropriated, not just known.
- **The individual:** "the crowd is untruth"; the public levels everyone down.
- **Three stages:** aesthetic (pleasure), ethical (duty and commitment), religious (faith), connected by a leap.
- ***Fear and Trembling*:** Abraham's terrifying faith; the teleological suspension of the ethical; the knight of faith.
- **Anxiety:** "the dizziness of freedom".
- **Despair:** "the sickness unto death", the failure to become oneself, often unconscious.
- **Against the system:** life is understood backwards but must be lived forwards.
- **Christendom:** official, comfortable religion as the betrayal of real faith.
- **Criticisms:** too individualistic, risk of fanaticism, his treatment of Regine and women, withdrawal from politics, gloom.

**Key terms**

- **Existentialism:** philosophy that begins with the concrete existence, freedom and choices of the individual.
- **Subjectivity is truth:** the claim that the deepest truths depend on how a person relates to them.
- **Aesthetic, ethical, religious stages:** three ways of living, from pleasure to commitment to faith.
- **Leap:** the decisive commitment that moves a person from one stage to another; cannot be achieved by reasoning alone.
- **Teleological suspension of the ethical:** setting aside universal ethics for a higher purpose.
- **Knight of faith / knight of infinite resignation:** the one who gives up everything and believes he'll receive it back / the one who gives it up and accepts the loss.
- **Anxiety (Angest):** the vague dread that comes from awareness of freedom and possibility.
- **Despair:** the spiritual sickness of failing to be oneself.
- **Indirect communication:** Kierkegaard's method of using pseudonyms and characters to make readers think for themselves.
- **Christendom:** Christianity as a comfortable social institution, as opposed to true personal faith.
