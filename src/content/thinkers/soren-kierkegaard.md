## Life

Søren Aabye Kierkegaard was born in Copenhagen in 1813, the youngest of seven children of Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard, a wealthy retired wool merchant who had risen from poverty. The father was intensely pious and burdened by guilt. As a shepherd boy, he believed, he had cursed God, and he raised his children in a stern, melancholy Lutheranism. Five of the seven children died before Søren turned twenty-one, which the father took as divine punishment.

Kierkegaard studied theology at the University of Copenhagen, though for years he lived as a witty, extravagant man about town. In 1840 he became engaged to **Regine Olsen**, but within a year he broke off the engagement, apparently convinced that his melancholy and his sense of religious vocation made marriage impossible. The episode haunted him for the rest of his life, and his books are full of its echoes. His inheritance allowed him to devote himself entirely to writing.

In an extraordinary burst between 1843 and 1846 he published a series of works under elaborate pseudonyms, among them Victor Eremita, Johannes de Silentio and Johannes Climacus. Each represented a different point of view, a method he called "indirect communication", which forced readers to choose for themselves. Alongside them he published "upbuilding discourses" under his own name.

In 1846 the satirical paper *The Corsair* mocked him relentlessly, caricaturing his appearance and uneven trouser legs, and he became a figure of ridicule on Copenhagen's streets. In his last years he launched a ferocious public attack on the Danish state church, arguing that comfortable official "Christendom" had abolished true Christianity. In October 1855 he collapsed in the street, and he died in hospital weeks later, aged forty-two.

## Key ideas

**Against the system.** Hegel claimed to grasp all of reality in a rational system. Kierkegaard replied that a system of existence is impossible for an existing person, who must live forward into an uncertain future and make decisions no system can make for them.

**Truth is subjectivity.** The most important truths, about how to live and what to believe, are not objective facts to be observed from a distance but must be appropriated with passionate inward commitment. "The crucial thing is to find a truth which is truth for me."

**The stages of existence.** Kierkegaard described three ways of life. The **aesthetic** seeks pleasure and interest and flees boredom. The **ethical** commits to duty, choice and universal moral norms, as in marriage. The **religious** stands as an individual before God. One does not reason one's way between them: one leaps.

**The leap of faith.** In *Fear and Trembling* he meditates on Abraham, commanded to sacrifice Isaac. Faith, he argues, is not a comfortable certainty but a paradox: a passionate commitment beyond reason that may even suspend ordinary ethics. The "knight of faith" lives it while looking, outwardly, like an ordinary man.

**Anxiety and despair.** Anxiety is "the dizziness of freedom", the vertigo of facing open possibilities. Despair, the "sickness unto death", is the failure to be oneself: to accept that one is a self grounded in something beyond oneself.

**The individual and the crowd.** "The crowd is untruth." In crowds, of the public, the press or the established church, people evade personal responsibility. Each person must become a "single individual".

## Influence and legacy

Kierkegaard was little known outside Denmark until German translations appeared in the early twentieth century. Then his influence exploded. Karl Jaspers, Martin Heidegger, Jean-Paul Sartre and Albert Camus all drew on his analyses of anxiety, choice and authenticity, and he is widely regarded as the first existentialist. Protestant theologians such as Karl Barth, Rudolf Bultmann, Paul Tillich and Dietrich Bonhoeffer were deeply shaped by him. Ludwig Wittgenstein called him "by far the most profound thinker of the last century".
