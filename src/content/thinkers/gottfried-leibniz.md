## Start here

Every photo on your phone, every song, every WhatsApp message, every video call, is stored and sent as a long string of just two symbols: **0** and **1**. Your face, your voice, this page you are reading: all of it, at the deepest level, is zeros and ones.

The man who first set out this two-digit system of arithmetic in full, and saw that all numbers and perhaps all reasoning could be expressed with it, was a German philosopher who died in 1716, more than two hundred years before the first electronic computer.

The same man also invented, independently of Isaac Newton, the **calculus**: the mathematics of change that lets engineers design bridges, doctors model the spread of disease and economists predict growth. The symbols you see in a calculus class, ∫ and *dx*, are his.

And the same man spent years on a very different question, one that every human being faces sooner or later. A child dies of cancer. An earthquake destroys a city. A flood sweeps away a village. If God is all-good and all-powerful, **why is there so much suffering in the world?**

His answer was famous, and became even more famous when people mocked it: this is **the best of all possible worlds**. God, being perfectly wise and good, considered every possible world and chose the best one. Even the suffering is part of the best possible whole.

Is that comforting, or outrageous? Fifty years later, after a terrible earthquake destroyed Lisbon, the French writer Voltaire ridiculed this idea in a comic novel, *Candide*, whose foolish philosopher keeps insisting that all is for the best while disaster after disaster strikes.

The man was **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz**. He was a lawyer, diplomat, historian, engineer, librarian, mathematician, logician, physicist and philosopher. He wrote to more than a thousand correspondents across Europe, China and Russia. He dreamed of a universal language in which every argument could be settled by calculation. He tried to reunite the Christian churches. He designed one of the first calculating machines that could multiply. He has been called the last person who knew everything.

By the end of this page, you will understand his strange and brilliant picture of reality, made of countless tiny mind-like units he called **monads**. You will see how his questions, why is there something rather than nothing, can a machine think, why do good people suffer, are still alive in science, AI and religion. You will discover a surprising connection between his mathematics and the mathematicians of ancient and medieval India. And you will see why, despite his genius, he died almost forgotten.

## The world he was born into

### A Germany in ruins

Leibniz was born in **Leipzig** in 1646, two years before the end of the **Thirty Years' War**. This war, fought mostly on German soil between Catholic and Protestant powers, was one of the most destructive in European history. Armies marched back and forth for a generation, burning, looting and spreading disease. In some regions, a third or more of the population died.

When the war ended in 1648, "Germany" was not a country but a patchwork of more than three hundred states: kingdoms, duchies, bishoprics, free cities, all loosely joined in the Holy Roman Empire, divided between Catholic, Lutheran and Calvinist rulers. Each had its own court, laws and politics.

Think about what this meant for a brilliant young man. There was no single national university system, no great capital like Paris or London. To make a career, you had to serve a prince. And all around you were the scars of religious war, a reminder of what happens when people cannot agree and resort to violence. Much of Leibniz's life would be devoted to finding **harmony**: between churches, between philosophies, between nations, between science and faith.

### The new science and the new philosophy

Intellectually, Leibniz grew up at the height of the **Scientific Revolution**. Galileo had died in 1642. **Descartes** had published his philosophy. **Spinoza** was a young man in Amsterdam. In England, **Isaac Newton** was born in 1643, three years before Leibniz. Scientific societies were being founded: the Royal Society in London in 1660, the Academy of Sciences in Paris in 1666.

The great question of the age was how to reconcile the new mechanical science, which explained everything in terms of matter in motion, with traditional ideas about God, the soul, freedom and purpose. Descartes had split the world into mind and matter. Spinoza had merged everything into one necessary God-Nature. Leibniz wanted a third way: a world that was fully rational and scientific, but also full of life, purpose, freedom and a good God.

## The life, as a story

### The boy in his father's library

Leibniz's father, Friedrich, was a professor of moral philosophy at the University of Leipzig. He died when Gottfried was six, leaving behind a large library. At first the boy was not allowed in. When he was about eight, he was finally given access, and he devoured it. He taught himself **Latin**, partly by working his way through illustrated books, and then Greek. By his early teens he was reading ancient historians, poets, philosophers and medieval theologians.

He entered the University of Leipzig at fourteen, studying philosophy and law. At twenty he applied for a doctorate in law, but Leipzig refused him, possibly because of his youth. He went instead to the University of **Altdorf**, near Nuremberg, which awarded him a doctorate in 1667. His dissertation so impressed the faculty that they offered him a professorship. He declined. He did not want an academic life. He wanted to be in the world, among princes, diplomats and scientists, where ideas could change things.

### The diplomat with a wild plan

Through a patron, Baron Johann Christian von Boineburg, Leibniz entered the service of the **Elector of Mainz**, one of the most important princes of the Empire. He worked on reforming the legal code and wrote political papers.

In 1672, he was sent to Paris on a remarkable diplomatic mission. King **Louis XIV** of France was the most powerful ruler in Europe and was threatening to attack the Dutch Republic and the German states. Leibniz had drawn up a plan to divert him: persuade Louis to invade **Egypt** instead, to conquer it from the Ottoman Empire and open routes to the East. The plan went nowhere; France attacked the Netherlands that year. (More than a century later, Napoleon did invade Egypt, and some later writers wondered whether he had known of Leibniz's plan.)

### Four years that changed everything

The mission failed, but Leibniz stayed in **Paris** for four years, and they were the most important years of his intellectual life. Paris was the intellectual capital of Europe. Leibniz met leading scholars and scientists, above all the great Dutch physicist **Christiaan Huygens**, who became his mathematics teacher.

Leibniz threw himself into mathematics. By **1675**, he had developed the **differential and integral calculus**, including the notation that is still used: *dx* for an infinitely small difference, and the elongated S, **∫**, for "sum" (Latin *summa*), the symbol for the integral.

He also designed a mechanical calculator, the **"stepped reckoner"**, which could add, subtract, multiply and divide. He showed a model to the Royal Society on a visit to **London** in 1673, and was elected a Fellow.

On his way back to Germany in 1676, he stopped in Amsterdam and The Hague. There he met the microscopist Leeuwenhoek, and spent several days in long conversations with **Spinoza**, whose ideas fascinated and alarmed him.

### Forty years in Hanover

From 1676 until his death, Leibniz served the Dukes of **Brunswick-Lüneburg** at **Hanover**, a middle-sized German court. His official jobs were librarian, legal adviser and later court historian. In practice, he did everything.

He spent years trying to improve the **silver mines** in the Harz mountains, designing windmills and pumps to drain water from the mines. The project failed, partly due to technical problems and partly due to the miners' resistance. He was commissioned to write the **history of the ruling family**, the House of Brunswick, and travelled to archives in Germany, Austria and Italy for years. He never finished it.

He worked tirelessly for the **reunion of the Catholic and Protestant churches**, and later of the Lutheran and Calvinist churches, corresponding with bishops and theologians. It did not happen.

He formed deep intellectual friendships with two remarkable women: **Sophie, Electress of Hanover**, and her daughter **Sophie Charlotte**, who became Queen of Prussia. With Sophie Charlotte's support, he founded the **Berlin Academy of Sciences** in 1700 and became its first president. He also met and advised **Peter the Great** of Russia, proposing scientific institutions and reforms.

### The man who wrote to the world

Leibniz never wrote one great systematic book of philosophy. Instead, his ideas are scattered across short essays, unfinished treatises and an enormous number of **letters**: tens of thousands of letters to around 1,100 correspondents, from Jesuit missionaries in China to mathematicians in Switzerland, from princesses to theologians. The work of publishing all his papers, begun over a century ago, is still not finished.

He was fascinated by **China**. Through Jesuit missionaries, he learned about Chinese philosophy, language and history. In 1697 he published *Novissima Sinica*, "Latest News from China", suggesting that Europe and China should exchange knowledge: Europe's science for China's practical ethics and political wisdom. In 1701, a Jesuit in China, Joachim Bouvet, sent him diagrams of the **hexagrams** of the ancient Chinese *Book of Changes* (*Yijing*). Leibniz was thrilled to see that the patterns of broken and unbroken lines could be read as **binary numbers**. He published his *Explanation of Binary Arithmetic* in 1703, mentioning this connection.

### The bitter quarrel

The last years of Leibniz's life were poisoned by a priority dispute with **Isaac Newton**. Newton had developed his own version of calculus, which he called "fluxions", in the mid-1660s, about ten years before Leibniz, but he did not publish it. Leibniz published his calculus first, in **1684**.

Newton's supporters began accusing Leibniz of having stolen the idea, perhaps after seeing some of Newton's papers during his visit to London. Leibniz appealed to the Royal Society, of which he was a member, to investigate. In 1712, the Society published a report finding in Newton's favour. What Leibniz did not know was that the report had been secretly written by Newton himself, who was President of the Royal Society.

Today historians agree that Newton and Leibniz discovered calculus **independently**. Newton was first; Leibniz published first; and Leibniz's notation was so much clearer that it became the standard used around the world. British mathematicians, out of loyalty to Newton, kept using his clumsier notation for about a century, and fell behind mathematicians on the European continent.

### Left behind

In 1714, Leibniz's employer, Georg Ludwig, Elector of Hanover, became **King George I of Great Britain**. Leibniz hoped to follow him to London. The king refused, partly because of the dispute with Newton, and ordered Leibniz to stay in Hanover and finish the family history.

Leibniz died in Hanover on **14 November 1716**, aged seventy. He had never married. (One story says he proposed to a woman when he was about fifty, but she asked for time to think, and while she thought, he changed his mind.) He had become unpopular at court. According to a contemporary account, his funeral was attended by almost no one except his secretary. The man who had written to half the learned world went to his grave almost alone.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Nothing happens without a reason

**The idea in one line:** For everything that exists or happens, there is a sufficient reason why it is so and not otherwise.

Leibniz called this the **principle of sufficient reason**, and it was one of the two great principles of his thought. Nothing happens by pure chance, without a reason. If something is this way rather than another way, there must be a reason why.

**An example.** A ball is resting on a perfectly flat table, and suddenly it rolls to the left. You would immediately ask: why did it roll left? A breeze? A tilt in the table? Someone pushed it? You would never accept "for no reason at all". That instinct, that there must be a reason, is the principle of sufficient reason.

**The biggest question.** Leibniz pushed this principle to the ultimate question: "**Why is there something rather than nothing?**" For, he says, nothing is simpler and easier than something. And then: why do things exist in this way rather than another?

The universe itself cannot explain why it exists, he argued, because every event in it is explained by previous events, and that chain, even if endless, does not explain why there is a chain at all. So the sufficient reason for the whole universe must lie **outside** it, in a necessary being that carries the reason for its existence within itself. That being, Leibniz says, is **God**.

**Why it matters.** The principle of sufficient reason is the hidden assumption of all science. Scientists look for causes because they believe events have reasons. And the question "why is there something rather than nothing?" is still asked by philosophers and physicists. Modern cosmology can describe the Big Bang in astonishing detail, but the question of why there is any universe at all remains open.

### Idea 2: No two things are exactly alike

**The idea in one line:** If two things were exactly alike in every way, they would be the same thing; so no two different things in nature are perfectly identical.

This is called the **identity of indiscernibles**. Leibniz argued that God would have no reason to create two perfectly identical things and put one here and one there, since there would be no reason for choosing either arrangement. So every individual thing is unique.

He told a story about this in his letters to Samuel Clarke. A great princess, walking in her garden (usually taken to be the Electress Sophie at Herrenhausen), remarked that she did not believe there were two leaves perfectly alike. An ingenious gentleman walking with her thought he could easily find two. He searched for a long time and could not.

**An example.** Every snowflake is said to be unique. Every fingerprint, every face, every voice, every iris. Even "identical" twins have different fingerprints and develop differences as they grow. Leibniz would say: of course. Nature never repeats itself exactly.

**Why it matters.** This idea underlies a deep respect for **individuality**: each person, each creature, each moment is unique and irreplaceable. It also shaped Leibniz's argument against Newton about space, which we will see later.

### Idea 3: Monads, the atoms of reality

**The idea in one line:** The ultimate building blocks of reality are not bits of matter but simple, indivisible, mind-like units called monads, each of which reflects the whole universe from its own point of view.

This is Leibniz's strangest and most famous idea. Let us build it slowly.

**Step 1: matter can always be divided.** Any piece of matter, however small, has parts. You can always, in principle, cut it in half. So matter is made of parts, which are made of parts, and so on. But then, Leibniz asked, what is it ultimately made of? There must be something truly **simple**, without parts, that is the foundation of everything composite. Otherwise there would be nothing real at the bottom, only an endless regress of parts.

**Step 2: the simple things cannot be physical.** If something has no parts, it has no size or shape. So it cannot be a tiny physical atom; physical atoms would still have parts in principle. The true simple units must be non-physical.

**Step 3: they are something like minds.** What non-physical thing do we know? Our own mind. So Leibniz concluded that the simple substances are **mind-like**: each has some form of **perception** (an inner representation of the world) and **appetite** (a drive towards new perceptions). He called these simple substances **monads**, from the Greek *monas*, "unit".

**Step 4: every monad reflects the whole universe.** Each monad perceives, or represents, the entire universe, but from its own **point of view**, and with different degrees of clarity. Most monads have very dim, confused perceptions (those that make up rocks and plants). Animal souls have clearer perceptions with memory. Human minds have the clearest, with reason and self-awareness. God, the supreme monad, perceives everything perfectly.

Leibniz used an image: just as the same city looks different from every side, so the universe is, in a sense, multiplied in all the monads, each a **living mirror** of the universe from its own perspective.

**Step 5: monads have no windows.** And here is the strangest part. Monads cannot affect each other. "The Monads have no windows, through which anything could come in or go out." Nothing outside a monad can change what happens inside it. Each monad's perceptions unfold entirely from its own inner nature, like a programme running.

**An example.** Think of every person at a large wedding. Each person experiences the whole event, the music, the bride and groom, the food, the relatives, but from their own position and perspective. The bride's mother sees it differently from the cook, who sees it differently from a child under the table. Each person's experience is a "view" of the same wedding. Now imagine that there was no actual wedding happening between them, and each person's experience was simply generated from within, perfectly coordinated with everyone else's. That is roughly Leibniz's monadic universe.

**Why it matters.** It sounds bizarre, and many philosophers have found it so. But think about what Leibniz was trying to do. He wanted a universe where **mind** is not an odd extra added to dead matter, as in Descartes, but the basic stuff of reality. Some modern philosophers, faced with the "hard problem" of how consciousness arises from matter, have revived similar views (sometimes called **panpsychism**: the idea that some form of experience is a basic feature of the world). Leibniz was there first.

### Idea 4: The orchestra without a conductor

**The idea in one line:** Mind and body, and all monads, don't actually interact; God set them up at creation so their states correspond perfectly, like clocks keeping the same time.

If monads have no windows, how do we explain the apparent interaction of mind and body? When I decide to raise my arm, my arm rises. When I touch fire, I feel pain.

Leibniz's answer was **pre-established harmony**. God created every monad with its entire future already built in, so perfectly coordinated that the mind's decisions and the body's movements always correspond, without any actual influence between them. Leibniz used the image of **two clocks** that keep perfect time with each other. They might be connected by some mechanism, or be continually adjusted by a clockmaker. Or they might simply be so perfectly made at the start that they agree forever. The third option is the most worthy of God.

Another image: an **orchestra** in which each musician plays from their own score, never hearing the others, yet together they produce perfect harmony, because the composer wrote all the parts to fit.

**Why it matters.** This solution avoided Descartes' problem of how a non-physical mind pushes a physical body. It also kept both mind and body real, unlike Spinoza's single substance. But many critics found it extravagant: a universe of isolated units, coordinated by God like a cosmic programme. It raises a hard question too. If everything in my mind unfolds from my own pre-programmed nature, in what sense am I free? Leibniz worked hard to answer this, arguing that our actions are free because they come from our own nature and reasons, even if God foresaw them.

### Idea 5: The mill, and why a machine can't think

**The idea in one line:** You will never find a thought by looking at the parts of a machine, however complex.

In the *Monadology* (§17), Leibniz gives a famous argument. Imagine a machine built so that it could think, feel and perceive. Now imagine it enlarged to the size of a **mill**, so that you could walk inside it, as you walk into a mill. Inside, you would find only parts pushing and pulling each other: gears, levers, wheels. You would never find anything that explains a **perception**. So perception, he concluded, cannot be explained mechanically.

**An example.** Imagine you could shrink yourself and walk inside a human brain, or inside the servers running an AI system. You would see neurons firing, electrical signals, chemicals moving across gaps, or transistors switching on and off. Where, in all that, is the experience of tasting a mango, or the feeling of grief? You might see the processes that *correspond* to the experience, but not the experience itself.

**Why it matters.** This is one of the earliest statements of what philosophers today call the **hard problem of consciousness**: explaining why physical processes are accompanied by subjective experience. Every modern debate about whether an AI could be conscious, or whether consciousness is "just" brain activity, has Leibniz's mill in the background.

**A common misunderstanding.** Leibniz did not deny that the body works like a machine. He thought it was an extraordinary machine, a "divine machine", made of machines all the way down. His point was that perception itself is something different from mechanism, and must belong to a simple, unified substance, a monad.

### Idea 6: Tiny perceptions we don't notice

**The idea in one line:** Much of what goes on in our minds is too small or faint to be noticed, but it still affects us.

In the preface to his *New Essays*, Leibniz gives a wonderful example. When you stand by the seashore, you hear the roar of the waves. That roar is made up of the sounds of countless individual small waves. You don't hear each tiny wave separately; if you did, you'd be overwhelmed. But you must perceive each one a little, otherwise you could not hear the whole roar, because a hundred thousand nothings cannot make something.

He called these ***petites perceptions***, "little perceptions": perceptions too small for us to notice consciously, which nevertheless shape our experience, our tastes, our feelings and our actions.

**Why it matters.** This was a remarkable anticipation of the idea of the **unconscious mind**. Two centuries before Freud, Leibniz argued that much of mental life happens below awareness. Modern psychology confirms this: we are influenced by things we don't consciously notice, from background music in a shop to subtle facial expressions. Advertisers know this very well.

### Idea 7: The best of all possible worlds

**The idea in one line:** God, being perfectly good, wise and powerful, chose to create the best of all possible worlds, even though it contains evil.

Now we come to Leibniz's most famous and most mocked idea.

**The problem of evil.** If God is all-good, he would want to prevent suffering. If God is all-powerful, he could prevent it. If God is all-knowing, he knows about it. So why does suffering exist? This question is ancient. Leibniz gave a new word to the attempt to answer it: ***theodicy***, from the Greek for "God" and "justice": the justification of God's goodness in the face of evil.

**His answer.** Imagine God, before creation, considering all the infinitely many **possible worlds** that could exist: worlds where things go slightly differently, or completely differently. Being perfectly wise, God knows them all. Being perfectly good, God chooses the best. And being all-powerful, God creates it.

So this world is the best possible. Not perfect, because only God is perfect, and any created world must have limitations. But the **best possible balance** of good over evil, of richness and variety with simplicity of laws.

**Why include any evil at all?** Leibniz gave several reasons:

- **Finite creatures are limited.** Any created world must have imperfections, because only God is unlimited. Leibniz called this "metaphysical evil".
- **Some goods require some evils.** Courage requires danger. Compassion requires suffering. Forgiveness requires wrongdoing. A world with free creatures, who can choose good, must also allow them to choose evil.
- **We see only a small part.** We judge from our tiny corner of the universe. The whole may be beautiful even if parts look ugly, like a painting that seems to be a mess of colour up close, but forms a magnificent picture from a distance. Or like music, where dissonant notes make the harmony richer.

**An example.** A child falls and scrapes her knee while learning to ride a bicycle. The parent could have prevented it by never letting her ride. But then she would never learn, never gain confidence, never experience the joy of riding. A wise parent allows some pain for a greater good. Leibniz thought God's choice is similar, on a cosmic scale.

**The Lisbon earthquake.** On **1 November 1755**, All Saints' Day, a massive earthquake struck **Lisbon**, followed by a tsunami and fires. Tens of thousands of people died, many of them in churches during Mass. The disaster shook Europe's faith in a benevolent order. How could this be part of the best of all possible worlds?

**Voltaire's mockery.** In 1759, Voltaire published ***Candide***, a comic novel in which a naive young man and his tutor, **Dr Pangloss**, a caricature of Leibniz's followers, travel through a world of war, earthquake, slavery, disease and cruelty, while Pangloss keeps repeating that everything is for the best in the best of all possible worlds. The novel was hugely popular and made "Leibnizian optimism" a punchline for centuries.

**The Indian comparison.** Indian traditions have often answered the problem of evil differently: through **karma**. Suffering, in this view, is the result of beings' own past actions across many lives, not the direct choice of a creator. Some Indian schools also spoke of the world as God's ***lila***, divine play, not something created to meet a need. These answers raise their own difficult questions (is a newborn's suffering really "deserved"?), but they place the source of suffering differently from Leibniz.

**Why it matters.** Whatever you think of his answer, Leibniz faced the hardest question in religion head-on, with rigour and honesty. And his framework of "possible worlds" turned out to be extraordinarily useful, as we'll see.

**A common misunderstanding.** Leibniz did not say that everything in the world is good, or that we should accept suffering passively. He thought we should work to reduce evil wherever we can. His claim was about the whole: that God, choosing among all possibilities, chose the best. Voltaire's Pangloss is a caricature, though a funny and influential one.

### Idea 8: Truths of reason and truths of fact

**The idea in one line:** Some truths are necessary, because denying them is a contradiction; others are contingent, because they could have been otherwise.

Leibniz distinguished two kinds of truth:

- **Truths of reason** are necessary. Their opposite is impossible. "Two plus two equals four." "A triangle has three sides." "Every bachelor is unmarried." You can't deny these without contradicting yourself. They are true in every possible world.
- **Truths of fact** are contingent. Their opposite is possible. "Caesar crossed the Rubicon." "It rained in Mumbai yesterday." "Leibniz never married." These happen to be true, but they could have been otherwise. They are true in this world, but not in every possible world.

For truths of reason, the principle of **non-contradiction** is enough. For truths of fact, we need the principle of **sufficient reason**: there must be a reason why this happened rather than something else.

**Why it matters.** This distinction became fundamental in philosophy. And Leibniz's idea of "true in all possible worlds" was revived in the twentieth century by logicians, who developed **possible-worlds semantics**, a powerful tool for understanding necessity, possibility and meaning. When computer scientists, linguists and philosophers today talk about "possible worlds", they are speaking Leibniz's language.

### Idea 9: Calculus, and an Indian surprise

**The idea in one line:** Change and motion can be analysed mathematically by thinking about infinitely small differences and infinite sums.

Calculus is the mathematics of change: how fast something is changing at any instant (differentiation), and how small changes add up to a total (integration). It is used to calculate the orbits of satellites, the spread of a virus, the flow of electricity, the growth of an economy.

Leibniz's genius was not only in discovering calculus but in his **notation**. He wrote *dy/dx* for the rate of change of *y* with respect to *x*, and ∫ for the integral. These symbols make the logic of calculus visible on the page; they almost do the thinking for you. Leibniz believed that good notation is a tool for thought, a theme we'll see again.

**The Indian connection.** Here is something that should make every Indian student proud. One of Leibniz's famous discoveries is an infinite series for π: π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + ..., which he published in 1682. But this series had already been discovered around three centuries earlier by the great Indian mathematician **Madhava of Sangamagrama**, founder of the **Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics**, in the fourteenth century. The Kerala mathematicians developed infinite series for π, sine and cosine, ideas very close to calculus, long before Newton and Leibniz. Today the series is often called the **Madhava–Leibniz series**.

There is no strong evidence that Leibniz knew of the Kerala work; it seems to have been an independent discovery. But it is a powerful reminder that great mathematical ideas have been discovered in many places. And calculus itself, like all of modern mathematics, rests on the **decimal place-value system with zero**, which was developed in India and travelled to Europe through the Arab world.

### Idea 10: Binary, and the dream of a thinking machine

**The idea in one line:** All numbers can be written with just 0 and 1, and perhaps all reasoning can be reduced to calculation.

In his *Explanation of Binary Arithmetic* (1703), Leibniz showed how every number can be written using only two digits: 0 and 1. In our normal decimal system, each place is a power of ten (1, 10, 100...). In binary, each place is a power of two (1, 2, 4, 8, 16...). So 5 is 101 (one 4, no 2, one 1), and 13 is 1101 (one 8, one 4, no 2, one 1).

Leibniz loved this system for both practical and spiritual reasons. Practically, binary arithmetic is very simple: the rules of addition and multiplication are trivial. Spiritually, he saw a symbol of creation: everything made from 1 (God) and 0 (nothing).

He was also delighted by the link with the Chinese *Yijing*, whose hexagrams, made of broken and unbroken lines, could be read as binary numbers.

**Another Indian echo.** Long before Leibniz, the ancient Indian scholar **Pingala**, in his work on Sanskrit poetic metre (*Chandahshastra*), probably composed in the last centuries BCE, described a system for listing all combinations of short and long syllables that has been recognised by modern scholars as a form of binary enumeration. Binary thinking has a deep history in more than one civilisation.

**The dream of calculation.** But Leibniz dreamed of something much bigger. He imagined a **universal language of thought**, the ***characteristica universalis***, in which every concept would be represented by a symbol, so that reasoning could be carried out like arithmetic, using a ***calculus ratiocinator***, a calculus of reasoning. Then, he wrote, when people disagreed, instead of arguing, they would sit down with pens and say to each other: "**Let us calculate!**" (*Calculemus!*), and the answer would follow.

**Why it matters.** Leibniz never achieved this dream. But it inspired the development of **symbolic logic** in the nineteenth century (by George Boole and Gottlob Frege) and, through it, **computer science**. Boolean logic, operating on 0 and 1, true and false, is the language of every computer chip. The dream of mechanising reasoning lives on in artificial intelligence. When we argue today about whether AI can "think", we are standing in the space Leibniz opened, and at the door of the mill he asked us to walk into.

### Idea 11: Space and time are relations, not containers

**The idea in one line:** Space is not an empty box that exists on its own; it is just the order of relations between things. Time is the order of succession of events.

In the last year of his life, Leibniz carried on a famous exchange of letters with **Samuel Clarke**, a defender of Newton (with Newton himself probably helping behind the scenes). One major topic was the nature of space.

Newton held that space is **absolute**: a vast, real, empty container that would exist even if there were nothing in it. Leibniz argued that space is **relational**: it is just the system of relations between things. If there were no things, there would be no space.

His argument used his two principles. Suppose space were an absolute container, and God placed the whole universe in one location. Now imagine God placing it ten metres to the left, everything else the same. These two situations would be **indiscernible**, no one could ever tell the difference. So by the identity of indiscernibles, they are the same situation; and God could have had no sufficient reason to prefer one over the other. So absolute space makes no sense.

**Why it matters.** For two centuries, Newton's absolute space seemed to win. But in the twentieth century, **Einstein's theory of relativity** showed that space and time are not fixed, absolute containers. They are bound up with matter, energy and motion. Many physicists and philosophers see Einstein as vindicating at least part of Leibniz's relational view.

### Idea 12: Love as joy in another's happiness

Leibniz had a beautiful definition of love: **to love is to find pleasure in the happiness of another**. Justice, he said, is the charity of the wise. He believed that the more we understand the order and beauty of the universe, the more we will love God and our fellow creatures, and the happier we will be.

This vision drove his practical work for reconciliation between churches, cooperation between nations, the founding of scientific academies and the exchange of knowledge with China. He believed knowledge should serve the common good, summed up in his motto for the Berlin Academy: *theoria cum praxi*, theory joined with practice.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Aristotle: the ancient he revived

Most modern philosophers of Leibniz's time had abandoned **Aristotle**, whose physics had been overthrown. Leibniz surprised them by defending some of Aristotle's ideas, especially **substantial forms** (the idea that real things have an inner principle of unity and activity) and **final causes** (purposes). He thought the mechanists had thrown out something important: mechanism explains how things happen, but not why, and not what makes a living thing a unified being rather than a heap of parts.

### Descartes: the predecessor he corrected

Leibniz respected **Descartes** but found mistakes everywhere. He showed that Descartes' laws of motion were wrong: Descartes thought the total "quantity of motion" (mass times speed) is conserved; Leibniz argued that what is really conserved is a different quantity, which he called ***vis viva***, "living force" (mass times speed squared), an early form of the concept of energy. He also rejected Descartes' view of matter as mere extension and his account of mind–body interaction.

### Spinoza: the shadow he tried to escape

After meeting **Spinoza**, Leibniz spent years trying to avoid Spinoza's conclusions. Spinoza said there is one substance, God-Nature, from which everything follows necessarily, with no purposes and no free choice. Leibniz said there are infinitely many substances (monads), God freely chooses among possible worlds, and purposes are real. Some scholars think Leibniz's whole system can be read as an answer to Spinoza. Others think he came closer to Spinoza than he liked to admit.

### Locke: the empiricist he answered line by line

When **John Locke** published his *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* in 1689, arguing that the mind is a blank slate and all ideas come from experience, Leibniz wrote a long reply, the ***New Essays on Human Understanding***, following Locke chapter by chapter as a dialogue between two characters. He argued that the mind is not a blank slate but more like a **block of marble with veins** already marking out the shape of a statue, so that some ideas and truths, like those of mathematics and logic, are innate, though we need experience to bring them out. He also introduced his "little perceptions" in this work. Locke died in 1704, and Leibniz decided not to publish a reply to a dead man. The *New Essays* appeared only in 1765.

### Newton: the rival

The relationship with **Newton** was the great rivalry of Leibniz's life, over calculus, over space and time, and over gravity. Leibniz suspected that Newton's gravity, acting at a distance through empty space with no mechanism, was a return to "occult qualities". Newton's camp thought Leibniz's God, who set up the world perfectly at the start and never needed to intervene, made God a distant clockmaker. The debate shaped physics for centuries.

### Confucius and China

**Leibniz** was one of the first major European philosophers to take Chinese thought seriously. He admired **Confucian** practical ethics and the Chinese system of government by educated officials. He argued that the Chinese had a natural theology compatible with Christianity, and he proposed that China and Europe should learn from each other as equals. In an age of growing European arrogance, this was remarkably open-minded.

### Voltaire and the optimism debate

**Voltaire**'s *Candide* turned Leibniz into a figure of fun, and the image of Dr Pangloss stuck. This was not entirely fair: Leibniz's argument was more careful than Pangloss's slogan, and Leibniz himself worked hard to improve the world. But Voltaire raised a real question: does it help, or harm, to tell suffering people that their pain is part of the best possible world?

### Kant: from student to critic

**Immanuel Kant** grew up in a philosophical world shaped by Leibniz and his follower **Christian Wolff**, whose systematic version of Leibniz's ideas dominated German universities. Kant's early work was Leibnizian. Then, as he said, reading Hume woke him from his "dogmatic slumber", and he came to criticise the whole rationalist claim that pure reason can know the ultimate nature of reality, God and the soul. Kant's critique of Leibniz's rationalism shaped modern philosophy.

### Russell, Frege and computing

In 1900, **Bertrand Russell** published a book on Leibniz's philosophy, arguing that Leibniz's logic was the key to his whole system. Russell and **Gottlob Frege** were among the founders of modern logic, and both recognised Leibniz as a forerunner. The dream of the *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator* led, through Boole, Frege, Turing and others, to the computer. Norbert Wiener, a founder of cybernetics, called Leibniz a patron saint of the field.

## See it around you

**In every digital device.** Every computer, phone and smartwatch runs on binary: 0s and 1s, the system Leibniz described.

**In every calculus class.** The ∫ and *dx* on your exam papers are his.

**In AI debates.** Can machines think? Leibniz's mill argument and his dream of mechanised reasoning sit on either side of this debate.

**In problems of suffering.** When a disaster strikes, floods, earthquakes, pandemics, people ask how a good God could allow it, and theologians, priests and ordinary believers reach for answers that Leibniz systematised. Others, like Voltaire, find the answers inadequate.

**In "everything happens for a reason".** People often say this to comfort someone after a loss. It is a popular version of Leibniz's optimism, and it carries the same risk Voltaire pointed out: it can sound dismissive of real pain.

**In science.** Every time a scientist assumes that an event has a cause and goes looking for it, the principle of sufficient reason is at work.

**In individuality.** Fingerprints, DNA, the uniqueness of every person: the identity of indiscernibles turns up in forensic science and in our respect for individuals.

**In the unconscious.** Advertising, background music, subtle design choices, nudges in apps: all exploit the *petites perceptions* that shape us below awareness.

**In open, global science.** Leibniz's network of correspondents across countries and his belief that knowledge should be shared for the common good foreshadow international scientific collaboration today, and the idea that East and West can learn from each other.

## Use it

**1. Ask for the sufficient reason.** When something puzzles you, a rule at work, a family habit, a news event, don't accept "it just is". Ask what reason there is for it being this way rather than another.

**2. Remember the leaves.** No two people, situations or days are exactly alike. Before applying a general rule to someone, ask what makes this case different.

**3. Notice the little perceptions.** Pay attention to subtle influences on your mood and decisions: the music in a shop, the colour of a room, the tone of a message. You can't notice everything, but noticing some makes you harder to manipulate.

**4. Look at the bigger picture, carefully.** When something bad happens, it can help to step back and see how it fits into a larger whole. But be careful not to tell others their suffering is "for the best". That may comfort you more than them.

**5. Calculate where you can.** When a disagreement is about facts, numbers or logic, try Leibniz's approach: set out the reasoning clearly, step by step, and check it together. Many arguments disappear when the calculation is done.

**6. Use good notation.** Leibniz showed that the right symbols make thinking easier. In your own work, find clear ways to write things down: diagrams, tables, structured notes. Good notation is half the solution.

**7. Learn from other civilisations.** Leibniz looked to China with respect, not condescension. Look for what you can learn from cultures and traditions different from your own.

**8. Love as joy in another's happiness.** Try Leibniz's definition in practice: notice when you feel genuine pleasure at someone else's happiness, and cultivate it.

## Where he went wrong

**1. The best of all possible worlds?** Many people find this claim impossible to accept in the face of genocides, child abuse, painful diseases and natural disasters. Even if some suffering produces greater goods, the sheer amount and senselessness of much suffering seems to count against it. Voltaire's mockery, and the Lisbon earthquake, made this objection unforgettable.

**2. Windowless monads are hard to believe.** A universe of isolated mind-like units that never actually interact, coordinated by God's pre-programming, strikes most people, including most philosophers, as fantastically complicated and unnecessary.

**3. Freedom is under pressure.** If everything about each monad's future is built into it from creation, and God foresaw and chose it all, it is hard to see how we are truly free. Leibniz's attempts to reconcile freedom with his system are subtle, but many find them unconvincing.

**4. Too much faith in pure reason.** Leibniz believed reason could prove God's existence and the structure of reality. Kant and many others argued that reason cannot reach that far beyond experience.

**5. The dream of calculating all arguments was too optimistic.** Gödel's incompleteness theorems in the twentieth century showed that even mathematics cannot be fully captured in a single formal system. And many human disagreements, about values, meanings and priorities, cannot simply be calculated.

**6. Scattered and unfinished.** Leibniz started countless projects and finished few. His philosophy is scattered across letters and short essays, sometimes inconsistent, making it hard to know exactly what he believed.

**7. Serving power.** Leibniz spent his life serving princes, flattering patrons and pursuing their projects, from mine engineering to genealogies. Critics say his genius was too often spent on courtly tasks, and that his philosophy of harmony and optimism suited a man who needed to keep the powerful happy.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought of philosophy and mathematics as separate worlds, of computers as a twentieth-century invention, and of the question "why does God allow suffering?" as something only priests and believers worry about.

**Now,** you can see one mind connecting calculus, binary code, logic, physics, theology and the nature of consciousness. You can see that the digital world runs on an idea described over three centuries ago, and that some of those ideas were discovered even earlier in India and China. And you can see that the problem of evil is a question that every thinking person eventually faces, whatever they believe.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **Your phone** becomes a machine full of Leibniz's zeros and ones.
- **A calculus problem** carries symbols chosen by a German diplomat in Paris in 1675, and an echo of a Kerala mathematician three centuries before him.
- **"Everything happens for a reason"** becomes a big philosophical claim, not a casual comfort.
- **An argument at home or work** raises a hope: could we just set out the reasoning and calculate?
- **The roar of the sea, or a crowd** reminds you of the countless small perceptions you don't notice.
- **Every person you meet** is a unique mirror of the universe, seen from a point of view no one else has.
- **An AI chatbot** sends you back to the mill: is there anyone inside, or only parts pushing parts?

Leibniz wanted to understand everything and reconcile everyone. He died almost alone, his history unfinished, his philosophy scattered, his reputation damaged by a quarrel. Three centuries later, his symbols are on every exam paper and his zeros and ones are in every pocket. The best of all possible legacies, perhaps.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716), German philosopher, mathematician, diplomat and librarian in Hanover; founder of the Berlin Academy of Sciences.
- **Mathematics:** co-invented calculus independently of Newton, with the notation still used (∫, *dx*); described binary arithmetic; built a calculating machine.
- **Principles:** sufficient reason (nothing without a reason); identity of indiscernibles (no two things exactly alike); "why is there something rather than nothing?"
- **Monads:** simple, mind-like substances that mirror the universe from their own point of view; they have no windows.
- **Pre-established harmony:** mind and body (and all monads) don't interact but are perfectly coordinated by God, like clocks.
- **Mind:** the mill argument against mechanical explanations of perception; *petites perceptions* anticipate the unconscious.
- **Theodicy:** this is the best of all possible worlds; mocked by Voltaire's *Candide* after the Lisbon earthquake.
- **Logic and language:** truths of reason vs truths of fact; possible worlds; the dream of a universal calculus of reasoning ("Let us calculate!").
- **Space and time:** relational, not absolute, against Newton.
- **Indian connections:** the Madhava–Leibniz series for π; Pingala's binary-like enumeration; India's decimal system with zero.
- **Limits:** optimism hard to accept; monads hard to believe; freedom under pressure; too much faith in reason.

**Key terms**

- **Principle of sufficient reason:** nothing exists or happens without a reason why it is so and not otherwise.
- **Identity of indiscernibles:** two things identical in every respect would be one thing.
- **Monad:** a simple, indivisible, mind-like substance.
- **Pre-established harmony:** the God-ordained correspondence between all monads, including mind and body.
- **Petites perceptions:** perceptions too small to be noticed consciously.
- **Theodicy:** the attempt to justify God's goodness given the existence of evil.
- **Best of all possible worlds:** Leibniz's claim that God chose the best possible world.
- **Truths of reason / truths of fact:** necessary truths versus contingent truths.
- **Characteristica universalis:** Leibniz's dream of a universal symbolic language for reasoning.
- **Vis viva:** "living force", an early concept of kinetic energy.
