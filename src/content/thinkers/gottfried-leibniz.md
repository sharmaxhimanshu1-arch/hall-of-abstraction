## Life

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz was born in Leipzig in 1646, two years before the end of the Thirty Years' War. His father, a professor of moral philosophy, died when he was six, leaving a large library that the boy devoured, teaching himself Latin in the process. He entered the University of Leipzig at fourteen and received a doctorate in law from Altdorf at twenty, but declined an academic post. He preferred a life of practical affairs.

He entered the service of the Elector of Mainz and in 1672 was sent to Paris on a diplomatic mission. The four years he spent there were transformative. He met Europe's leading scientists, including Christiaan Huygens, who tutored him in mathematics, and by 1675 he had developed the **differential and integral calculus**. He also designed a mechanical calculator that could multiply and divide. On his way home in 1676 he visited Spinoza in The Hague.

For the last forty years of his life Leibniz served the Dukes of Brunswick-Lüneburg in Hanover as librarian, legal adviser, engineer and court historian, writing a vast history of the ducal family that he never finished. He worked on draining the Harz silver mines, on reuniting the Catholic and Protestant churches, and on founding scientific academies. He became the first president of the Berlin Academy of Sciences in 1700. He wrote tens of thousands of letters to some 1,100 correspondents.

His final years were clouded by a bitter dispute with Isaac Newton's supporters over who had invented calculus first. A Royal Society report of 1712, secretly drafted by Newton himself, accused Leibniz of plagiarism. Historians now agree they discovered it independently. When his patron became King George I of Britain, Leibniz was left behind in Hanover. He died in 1716, and his funeral was attended, it is said, only by his secretary.

## Key ideas

Most of Leibniz's philosophy was set out in letters and short essays rather than great treatises. *Theodicy* (1710) was the only book he published in his lifetime.

**Monads.** The ultimate constituents of reality are not atoms of matter but **monads**: simple, indivisible, non-physical substances, each a kind of soul or centre of perception. Every monad reflects the entire universe from its own point of view, more or less clearly. Monads "have no windows": they do not causally interact.

**Pre-established harmony.** Then why do mind and body, or any two things, seem to interact? Because God created all monads so that their internal states unfold in perfect coordination, like clocks set to keep the same time.

**The best of all possible worlds.** God, being perfectly good, wise and powerful, must have chosen to create the best of all possible worlds. Evil exists because the best world, the one richest in variety yet simplest in its laws, necessarily includes some imperfection.

**The principle of sufficient reason.** Nothing happens without a reason why it is so and not otherwise. This principle led Leibniz to the fundamental question: "Why is there something rather than nothing?" His answer was God.

**Truths of reason and truths of fact.** Necessary truths, like those of mathematics, cannot be denied without contradiction. Contingent truths, like "Caesar crossed the Rubicon", could have been otherwise. The idea anticipates modern talk of truth "in all possible worlds".

**A universal language of thought.** Leibniz dreamed of a *characteristica universalis*, a symbolic language in which all reasoning could be carried out by calculation, so that disputes could be settled by saying "Let us calculate."

## Influence and legacy

Leibniz's calculus notation, including the integral sign ∫ and *dx*, is the one mathematicians still use. His binary arithmetic, which he connected to the Chinese *Yijing*, became the language of digital computers, and his dream of mechanised reasoning anticipated symbolic logic and artificial intelligence.

Voltaire's satire *Candide* (1759), with its foolish optimist Dr Pangloss, made "the best of all possible worlds" a punchline. Yet Leibniz's metaphysics shaped Kant and German philosophy, his relational view of space influenced debates in physics, and his ideas about possible worlds became central to modern logic and philosophy of language.
