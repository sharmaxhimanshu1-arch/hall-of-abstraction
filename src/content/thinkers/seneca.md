## Start here

Open your phone's settings and look at the screen-time report for last week.

Go on, I'll wait.

Most people are a little shocked. Four hours a day. Five. Sometimes more. Multiply that by seven, then by fifty-two. That is more than two months of waking life every year, gone into a small glowing rectangle. And here is the strange part. If someone stole two months' salary from you, you would go to the police. If someone took a piece of your land, you would go to court. But when hours of your life disappear, nobody files a complaint. Not even you.

Now listen to a man who noticed this exact problem two thousand years ago, without any phones at all:

"It is not that we have a short space of time, but that we waste much of it."

He wrote that people are tight-fisted with their money and property, but when it comes to wasting time, the one thing where it would be right to be a miser, they are shockingly generous. They let anyone take it. A neighbour drops in, a client calls, an invitation arrives, and they hand over hours without a second thought, as if they had an endless supply.

The man was **Lucius Annaeus Seneca**. He was one of the richest men in Rome, a senator, a famous writer of plays and essays, and for years the chief adviser to one of the most notorious emperors in history: **Nero**. He lived surrounded by power, wealth, flattery, plots and murder. And in the middle of all that, he wrote some of the most practical, human and beautiful advice about how to live that has ever been written.

He is also one of the most *contradictory* thinkers you will ever meet. He praised poverty while owning a gigantic fortune. He wrote about freedom while serving a tyrant. He taught calm while living in constant danger. Critics have called him a hypocrite for two thousand years.

By the end of this page, you will understand why Seneca still matters, and why his contradictions might make him *more* useful to you, not less. Because you, too, live in a world full of distraction, ambition, fear and anger. And you, too, will never be a perfect sage. Seneca's question is: how does an ordinary, imperfect person, caught up in a busy, compromised life, still become a little wiser every day?

## The world he was born into

To understand Seneca, you need two pictures in your mind: the Roman Empire, and a Greek philosophy called Stoicism.

### Rome under the emperors

Seneca was born around 4 BCE. Just a few decades earlier, Rome had been a **republic**, governed by elected officials and a Senate. But a century of civil wars had ended that. Julius Caesar seized power; after his assassination in 44 BCE, more wars followed, until his adopted son Octavian defeated all rivals and became **Augustus**, the first emperor.

On paper, the Senate still existed. In reality, one man held power. And power in imperial Rome was terrifying. During Seneca's lifetime, Rome was ruled by Augustus, then Tiberius (who became paranoid and cruel in his later years), then Caligula (famously unstable and murderous), then Claudius, then Nero. Senators could be executed on the word of an informer. Fortunes could be seized overnight. A clever remark at dinner could cost you your life.

At the same time, Rome was rich beyond imagination. Wealth flowed in from conquered provinces. The elite lived in luxurious villas, held lavish banquets and competed in display. Think of the most extravagant celebrity weddings you have heard of, and multiply.

So picture the life of an ambitious Roman: surrounded by luxury, chasing status, flattering the powerful, and always one rumour away from ruin. Anxiety was built into the system.

### Stoicism: a philosophy for hard times

Into this world came a Greek philosophy called **Stoicism**. It had been founded in Athens around 300 BCE by a merchant named **Zeno of Citium**, who, according to legend, lost everything in a shipwreck, wandered into a bookshop in Athens, read about Socrates, and decided to become a philosopher. He taught in a public colonnade called the *Stoa Poikile*, the "Painted Porch". His followers were named after the porch: the **Stoics**.

The core Stoic idea is simple to state and very hard to live:

- The only truly good thing is **virtue**: wisdom, justice, courage and self-control.
- Everything else, money, health, reputation, even life, is "indifferent". Some indifferents are preferable (health is better than sickness), but none can make you truly good or truly happy.
- We cannot control what happens to us. We can control how we judge it and respond to it.
- The universe is ordered by a divine reason (*logos*), and wisdom means living in harmony with it.

Can you see why this philosophy appealed to Romans? If an emperor can take your money, your position and even your life at any moment, then a philosophy that says *these things do not define your worth* is not just comforting. It is a kind of armour.

Seneca is the bridge between Greek Stoicism and its Roman flowering. He took the technical school philosophy of the Greeks and turned it into vivid, practical Latin advice for people living real lives.

## The life, as a story

### A boy from Spain with weak lungs

Seneca was born in **Corduba** (today's Córdoba, in southern Spain), then a prosperous Roman province. His family was wealthy and educated. His father, also named Seneca and now known as **Seneca the Elder**, was a famous expert on rhetoric, the art of public speaking. Seneca had two brothers. The elder, **Gallio**, later became governor of the province of Achaia in Greece; he appears in the New Testament (Acts 18), where the Jews of Corinth bring the apostle Paul before him and he refuses to hear the case. The younger brother was the father of the poet **Lucan**.

Seneca was brought to Rome as a small child. He was sickly. He suffered all his life from a chest illness that left him gasping for breath, probably asthma. In one letter he says that doctors called it "rehearsing death". As a young man he was so ill and depressed that he thought of ending his life, but, he later wrote, he held back because he thought of how his old father would grieve. Sometimes, he wrote, even to go on living is an act of courage.

He studied philosophy with enthusiasm. One teacher, the Stoic **Attalus**, impressed him so much that he gave up oysters, mushrooms, perfumes and soft mattresses, at least for a while. Another, Sotion, convinced him to become vegetarian for a year, until his father, worried that it looked like a suspicious foreign religion, asked him to stop. He also spent time in Egypt with an aunt, recovering his health.

### Rise, danger and exile

Back in Rome, Seneca entered politics and became famous as a brilliant speaker. Too brilliant, perhaps. According to the historian Cassius Dio, the emperor **Caligula** was so jealous of Seneca's eloquence that he ordered his death, and let him live only because someone pointed out that Seneca was so ill he would soon die anyway.

Under the next emperor, **Claudius**, disaster struck. In 41 CE, Seneca was accused of adultery with Julia Livilla, the emperor's niece. The charge was probably political. He was sentenced to death, then the sentence was reduced to **exile on the island of Corsica**.

He spent eight years there. Imagine going from the centre of the world's greatest city to a rocky island with few comforts and no friends. From Corsica he wrote a *Consolation to Helvia*, a letter to his mother, comforting *her* for *his* exile. It is a remarkable piece. He tells her that place does not matter to a wise person, that the mind is free anywhere, that the same sky is above us wherever we go. He also wrote a much less admirable piece, a consolation to Claudius' powerful freedman Polybius, which is full of flattery of the emperor, plainly written in the hope of being recalled. Here already you see both Senecas: the philosopher and the politician.

### Tutor to Nero

In 49 CE, the emperor's new wife, **Agrippina**, had Seneca recalled from exile. She had plans. Her son from an earlier marriage, a twelve-year-old boy named Lucius Domitius, was being lined up to become emperor, and she wanted the most famous intellectual in Rome to educate him. That boy became **Nero**.

When Claudius died in 54 CE (very probably poisoned by Agrippina), Nero became emperor at sixteen. Seneca, together with the praetorian prefect **Burrus**, effectively ran the empire for the next several years. Ancient historians generally remembered these early years of Nero's reign as a time of good government.

Seneca wrote speeches for Nero. He wrote a treatise, *On Clemency*, addressed to the young emperor, urging him to rule with mercy. And he also wrote, around this time, a vicious comic satire on the dead Claudius, mocking him as he arrives in heaven and is rejected by the gods. Its title is a pun often translated as *The Pumpkinification of Claudius*: instead of becoming a god (deification), he becomes a pumpkin.

### The compromises pile up

Then things grew darker. Nero had his step-brother Britannicus, a rival claimant, poisoned in 55 CE. In 59 CE he had his own mother, Agrippina, murdered. According to the historian Tacitus, Seneca drafted the letter Nero sent to the Senate justifying the killing, claiming Agrippina had plotted against him.

Meanwhile Seneca became fabulously rich. Ancient sources put his fortune at around 300 million sesterces, an enormous sum. He owned estates, vineyards and villas, and lent money at interest. One hostile historian, Cassius Dio, even claimed that Seneca's sudden recall of large loans in Britain contributed to the great revolt of Queen Boudica. That claim is disputed, but the accusation that Seneca was a hypocrite, preaching simplicity while growing rich at the emperor's side, was made in his own lifetime. A senator named Suillius publicly asked by what philosophy Seneca had acquired 300 million sesterces in four years.

When Burrus died in 62 CE, Seneca's protection was gone. He asked Nero to let him retire and offered to give back his wealth. Nero refused, with warm words. Seneca withdrew from public life anyway, as far as he could, and devoted himself to writing. These last three years produced his greatest work: the 124 **Moral Letters to Lucilius**, written to a friend, Lucilius, who was a civil servant in Sicily.

### The death

In 65 CE, a conspiracy to assassinate Nero, led by a senator named Piso, was discovered. Seneca's nephew Lucan was involved. Whether Seneca himself was involved is unclear. Nero did not care. He sent a centurion to Seneca's villa with an order: he must take his own life.

Tacitus describes the scene in detail. Seneca, denied the right to make a will, turned to his friends and said that since he could not leave them his property, he would leave them the one thing he still had, and the finest: the **pattern of his life**. When they wept, he gently scolded them: where was their philosophy now? Did anyone not know Nero's cruelty? After killing his mother and brother, what else was left but to kill his teacher?

His wife, **Paulina**, insisted on dying with him. They cut the veins of their arms together. Nero, not wanting to seem cruel, ordered Paulina saved; her wounds were bound, and she survived, pale, for some years. Seneca's death, though, was slow. His old, thin body bled slowly. He took poison, hemlock, as Socrates had, but it did not work. Finally he was placed in a hot bath and suffocated by the steam. He had consciously modelled his death on Socrates', dictating to his secretaries to the end.

Whatever his compromises in life, Rome remembered how he died.

## The big ideas, one by one

A quick note about how Seneca writes. He is not a system-builder. He does not give you a complete theory of the universe, as Plato or Aristotle did. He gives you **medicine**: short, sharp, memorable advice for specific problems, written in a style full of punchy one-liners. Reading Seneca is like having a wise, slightly sarcastic older friend who knows exactly what you are struggling with because he struggles with it too.

He also says openly that he is not a sage. "I am not a wise man," he writes, and he never will be. He is, as he puts it, a patient in the same hospital, discussing the common disease with his fellow patients. That honesty is part of his charm.

### Idea 1: Time is your only real possession

**The idea in one line:** Life is long enough if you know how to use it; what makes it short is how much of it we waste.

Seneca's first letter to Lucilius begins with this idea, and so does one of his most famous essays, *On the Shortness of Life*.

**An example.** Think of a typical Indian household conversation. "Where did the day go?" "I don't know, I was busy all day." Busy doing what? Replying to messages, attending a meeting that could have been an email, standing in a queue, watching a debate that made you angry, scrolling. At the end of the day, you are tired but you cannot name what you did with your time.

Seneca calls such people the **occupied** (*occupati*): always busy, never living. He describes them in ways that sound uncomfortably modern. People who rush from one engagement to another, who chase approval from the powerful, who plan their lives for "retirement", only to arrive at old age and discover they never actually lived. "You will hear many men saying," he writes (in Basore's translation), "'After my fiftieth year I shall retire into leisure, my sixtieth year shall release me from public duties.' And what guarantee, pray, have you that your life will last longer?"

He has a cruel but brilliant image. When you see an old man with many years behind him, do not assume he has lived long. He has only *existed* long. As a ship tossed about in a storm is not on a long voyage, just a long tossing about.

**The Indian echo.** The poet Bhartrihari, in his *Vairagya Shataka*, wrote a famous verse that Seneca would have loved. Roughly: *we did not enjoy pleasures; pleasures consumed us. We did not perform austerities; we were burned. Time did not pass; we passed. Craving did not grow old; we grew old.* And Kabir's famous line is pure Seneca: *kaal kare so aaj kar, aaj kare so ab*. What you plan for tomorrow, do today; what you plan for today, do now.

**Why it matters.** Seneca turns time into a moral issue. Wasting time is not just inefficient; it is a failure to live. And he insists that the only time we actually own is **now**. The past is gone; the future is uncertain. "Nothing, Lucilius, is ours, except time," he writes. Everything else, money, houses, even our bodies, can be taken. Time is the only thing nature has given us, and we let it slip away.

**A common misunderstanding.** Seneca is not a productivity guru telling you to fill every hour with work. Quite the opposite. Busy-ness is the problem. The people he admires most are those who spend time on **philosophy** in the broad sense: on understanding life, on cultivating their minds, on friendship and reflection. Rest, done well, is not wasted time. Mindless busy-ness is.

### Idea 2: We suffer more in imagination than in reality

**The idea in one line:** Most of our fear is about things that have not happened and may never happen.

**An example.** Think about the night before an important exam result, a job interview or a medical test. Your mind runs through every terrible possibility. You fail. You get rejected. The test shows something serious. You imagine the conversations, the disappointment, the shame. By morning you have lived through the disaster ten times.

And then, often, the result is fine. Or it is bad, but not as bad as you imagined, and you cope.

Seneca writes to Lucilius: "There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality." Some of our fears are about things that will never happen. Some are exaggerated. Some are about things that will happen, but we torture ourselves in advance, suffering twice.

His advice: examine your fear. Ask whether there is clear evidence that the bad thing will happen. Ask whether you are exaggerating it. Ask whether worrying now will help. And do not rush to meet your troubles before they arrive. Why, he asks, should we summon troubles that will arrive soon enough on their own?

**Why it matters.** This is one of the most useful pieces of practical psychology ever written. Modern **cognitive behavioural therapy**, one of the most effective treatments for anxiety, works on exactly this principle: identifying distorted, catastrophic thoughts and testing them against reality. Seneca was doing therapy two thousand years before the word existed.

### Idea 3: Rehearse misfortune, and practise poverty

**The idea in one line:** Imagine losing what you have, and occasionally live as if you had already lost it; then misfortune loses its power to shock you.

This sounds like the opposite of the last idea. Didn't Seneca just say not to imagine disasters? But there is a difference. Anxious worry is uncontrolled and repetitive. Seneca's exercise is **deliberate and calm**: you choose to think about possible losses, accept that they could happen, and prepare yourself.

The Stoics called this ***premeditatio malorum***, the "pre-meditation of evils". In one letter, Seneca writes about a great fire that destroyed the city of Lugdunum (modern Lyon) in a single night. A great city, he says, was wiped out between one day and the next. If that can happen to a city, what can't happen to us? So we should keep in mind that anything can happen, and not be taken by surprise.

He also recommends something more radical. In Letter 18, he advises his friend to set aside a few days every so often during which he lives on the plainest food and wears the roughest clothes, and asks himself: "Is this the condition that I feared?" You will find, he says, that poverty is not so terrible. And once you know you can live with little, you lose the fear of losing what you have. You become free.

**An example.** Imagine a young professional who has never faced hardship. Their deepest fear is losing their job, because they cannot imagine life without their salary, flat and comforts. That fear makes them say yes to everything their boss demands, even unethical things. Now imagine the same person after a month of living simply, cooking basic meals, travelling by bus, finding that they were still fine, even happy. They are more free. They can say no.

Many Indian traditions know this practice well. Fasting, simple living during certain festivals, pilgrimages on foot, periods of *vrat* and restraint: whatever their religious meaning, they also train the mind to know that comfort is not necessary for life. Gandhi made voluntary simplicity a political and moral strength.

**Why it matters.** The fear of loss is one of the main ways we are controlled, by employers, by advertisers, by our own anxieties. Rehearsing loss is a way of disarming that fear. It also makes you **grateful** for what you have now, because you are aware that it is not guaranteed.

**A common misunderstanding.** This is not about becoming gloomy or expecting the worst all the time. It is about being mentally prepared, so that when something bad does happen, you respond calmly instead of collapsing. Ironically, people who practise this often enjoy life *more*, because they no longer take it for granted.

### Idea 4: Real poverty is craving

**The idea in one line:** You are not poor because you have little; you are poor because you want more.

"It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor." Seneca borrowed versions of this idea freely from the Epicureans, a rival school, because he believed good ideas belong to everyone. "I am wont," he says, "to cross over even into the enemy's camp, not as a deserter, but as a scout."

**An example.** You get a salary increase. You are happy for about a month. Then you upgrade your phone, your car, your house, your lifestyle. Your expenses rise to match your income. A year later you feel exactly as "short of money" as before. Psychologists call this the **hedonic treadmill**: we adapt to every gain and keep wanting more.

Now think of a person with a modest income who is content: they have enough to eat, a roof, people they love, time to think. Who is richer?

Seneca's point is that wealth is a relationship between what you have and what you want. You can increase wealth in two ways: get more, or want less. The second is always available, and much more reliable.

**The Buddhist parallel.** The Buddha taught that suffering arises from ***tanha***, craving or thirst. Not from lack itself, but from the restless wanting that is never satisfied. Seneca, without knowing anything of the Buddha, diagnosed the same disease. The cures differ: the Buddha's path leads to the extinction of craving through insight and meditation; Seneca's leads to living by reason and virtue, treating wealth as something to use without being used by it.

**A common misunderstanding.** Seneca does not say wealth is evil. Stoics considered it a "preferred indifferent": better to have than not, as long as you are not attached to it. The wise man, he wrote in *On the Happy Life*, does not love riches, but he would rather have them than not. The question is not whether you have money, but whether money has you. Of course, critics have pointed out that this is a very convenient philosophy for one of the richest men in Rome. We will come back to that.

### Idea 5: Anger is a brief madness

**The idea in one line:** Anger is never useful; it is a mistaken judgement that grows into a temporary insanity, and it can be prevented and cured.

Seneca wrote a three-book treatise *On Anger*, one of the most detailed studies of a single emotion in ancient literature. He opens by noting that some wise men have called anger a short madness: it is just as uncontrolled, forgets decency, is deaf to reason, and is roused by trivial causes.

**An example.** You are driving. Someone cuts in front of you. Instantly your blood boils. You honk, shout, maybe chase them to "teach them a lesson". Ten minutes later you are still angry, your heart is racing, you have nearly caused an accident, and the other driver has forgotten you exist. Who was harmed?

Seneca's analysis is subtle. He says anger is not a reflex. There is a first jolt, a physical shock, that we cannot help (the Stoics called these "first movements"). But anger proper begins only when the mind **agrees** with a judgement like "I have been wronged, and they must be punished". That agreement can be stopped.

His remedies:

- **Delay.** "The greatest remedy for anger is delay." Wait before acting. Most anger fades in time.
- **Remember your own faults.** The person who angered you is human, as you are. Have you never done something thoughtless?
- **Consider the source.** If a child or a sick person insults you, do you get angry? Many people who wrong us are, in a sense, ignorant or unwell.
- **Notice how ugly anger looks.** He even suggests that an angry person would calm down if they could see themselves in a mirror.
- **Don't take on too much.** People who are overloaded, tired and hungry are more irritable. Arrange your life to avoid constant provocation.

**Why it matters.** Anger damages relationships, careers, health and societies. Many families have a story of a relationship broken forever by words said in a moment of rage. Many communal riots begin with a rumour that turns anger into violence. Seneca's analysis, that anger depends on a judgement we can learn to question, is the foundation of modern anger-management therapy.

**A common misunderstanding.** Aristotle disagreed with Seneca here. Aristotle thought anger, at the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, is a virtue, and that a person who never feels anger at injustice is lacking something. Seneca thought anger is always dangerous: once let in, it does not obey limits. Which is right? Perhaps the honest answer is that moral outrage at injustice can be a powerful motivator, but it is safest when it is transformed into calm, determined action rather than rage. Gandhi's movement is a good example: deeply angry at injustice, but disciplined into non-violence.

### Idea 6: Philosophy is daily practice, not theory

**The idea in one line:** Philosophy is not for show or argument; it is a set of daily exercises to make your mind healthier.

Seneca is scathing about people who use philosophy for clever debates. He mocks logic-chopping puzzles that make no one better. Philosophy, he says, teaches us to *act*, not to talk.

Some of his practical exercises:

**The evening review.** He describes how his teacher's teacher, Sextius, would ask himself every night before sleep: "What bad habit have you cured today? What fault have you resisted? In what respect are you better?" Seneca does the same. When the lamp is taken away and his wife, knowing his habit, falls silent, he examines his whole day, hiding nothing from himself. "Why should I fear any of my mistakes," he asks, "when I can say, 'See that you never do that again; this time I pardon you'?"

**Read deeply, not widely.** In Letter 2, Seneca warns Lucilius against restlessly reading many authors. "Everywhere means nowhere," he says. A person who travels constantly has many acquaintances but no friends. A person who reads everything superficially gains nothing. Pick a few great minds and absorb them deeply. Every day, take away one thought to digest.

**Avoid crowds.** In Letter 7 he says that whenever he goes among crowds, at the games, the gladiator shows, he comes home "more greedy, more ambitious, more voluptuous, and even more cruel and inhuman", because he has been among human beings. Crowds infect us with their vices. Choose your company carefully.

**Learn by teaching.** In the same letter he says "men learn while they teach" (*homines dum docent discunt*). Explaining something to someone else is the best way to understand it.

**Why it matters.** Seneca's practical exercises are the ancestors of modern journaling, reflection, digital minimalism and deliberate reading. And his warning about crowds has become terrifyingly relevant. Replace "the gladiator games" with social media timelines, where we watch outrage and cruelty for entertainment, and you have the same effect: we come away more angry, more envious, more cruel.

### Idea 7: Death, and the freedom that comes from accepting it

**The idea in one line:** We are dying every day, and once you stop fearing death, nothing else can make you a slave.

Seneca writes about death constantly, but not morbidly. He wants to strip it of its terror.

He points out that we think of death as something ahead of us, but much of it is already behind us. Every hour that has passed belongs to death. "We die every day."

And he makes an argument the Stoics loved: before you were born, you did not exist for countless ages, and it did not trouble you. Death returns you to the same state. Why fear it?

Most powerfully, he connects death to freedom. "He who has learned to die has unlearned slavery." Think about it. Almost every form of control over us, by tyrants, by bullies, by our own anxieties, rests on the fear of loss, and ultimately on the fear of death. A person who has made peace with death cannot be threatened into betraying their principles.

That is exactly what Seneca's own death showed. Nero could order him to die. He could not make him beg.

**The Indian echo.** The Bhagavad Gita's teaching that the self is not born and does not die, and that the wise do not grieve for the living or the dead, comes from a very different metaphysics. But its practical message to Arjuna, that the fear of death should not paralyse one's duty, is close to Seneca's.

**A careful note.** The Stoics, Seneca included, also held that a person may choose to end their own life in certain situations, such as unbearable illness or to avoid being forced into a shameful act. They called this the "open door". This is very different from how we approach these questions today, and it should not be read as advice. If you, or someone you know, is struggling with thoughts of ending their life, please reach out to someone you trust or to a mental health helpline. Seneca himself, in his youth, chose to live for the sake of someone who loved him, and called that choice courage.

### Idea 8: We are all members of one body

**The idea in one line:** Every human being, including those society looks down on, shares the same nature and deserves to be treated as a fellow human.

In his famous **Letter 47**, Seneca writes about how Romans treated their slaves: forcing them to stand silently all night at banquets, beating them for a cough or a sneeze. He urges Lucilius to treat his slaves kindly, to eat with them, to talk to them. "Kindly remember," he writes, "that he whom you call your slave sprang from the same stock, is smiled upon by the same skies, and on equal terms with yourself breathes, lives, and dies."

And then a sharp turn. "'He is a slave.' His soul, however, may be that of a freeman. 'He is a slave.' But shall that stand in his way? Show me a man who is not a slave; one is a slave to lust, another to greed, another to ambition, and all men are slaves to hope or fear."

In another letter, he says all that we see, divine and human, is one: we are the parts of one great body, born related to one another, and nature has implanted in us a mutual love. "Let us possess things in common," he writes, "for birth is ours in common."

**Why it matters.** In a society built on slavery, this was a significant moral advance. And the Stoic idea that every human being shares in reason, and therefore belongs to one human community, the **cosmopolis**, later influenced ideas of natural law and universal human rights.

**A common misunderstanding.** Seneca did *not* call for the abolition of slavery. He urged kinder treatment within the system. That is a real limitation, which we will discuss later. But the seed he planted, that a slave's humanity is equal to a master's, grew into something larger than he could see.

### Idea 9: Fortune can take everything except your mind

**The idea in one line:** Luck, which Seneca calls Fortune, can take your wealth, your position and your health, but not your judgement, your character or your virtue.

Seneca's life was a constant lesson in Fortune's power: favour, exile, wealth, danger, death. His philosophy was a way to stand firm in the middle of that storm.

In his *Consolation to Helvia*, written from exile, he says Fortune can take only what she gave. Wealth, office, home, country: all were lent. What is truly ours, our mind and our virtue, she cannot touch. Wherever he goes, he will carry with him the most important things: nature, which is everywhere, and his own virtue.

He uses a striking image: Fortune's gifts should be held loosely, like things borrowed, ready to be returned without complaint when the lender asks for them back.

**Why it matters.** In a world of layoffs, market crashes, illness and political upheaval, this attitude is a source of resilience. It does not mean you stop caring about your job or your health. It means your sense of who you are does not collapse when they are threatened.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Socrates: the model

For Seneca, as for all the Stoics, **Socrates** was the great example of a philosopher who lived what he taught. Seneca mentions him often: his poverty, his calm in the face of his difficult wife and the mocking crowd, and above all his death. When Seneca's own death came, he deliberately followed Socrates' pattern: conversation with friends, gentle rebuke of their tears, the cup of hemlock. He wanted to prove, at the end, that his philosophy was more than words.

### Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius: the Stoic family

Seneca, **Epictetus** and **Marcus Aurelius** are the three great Roman Stoics whose writings survive. They make a fascinating trio: one was a millionaire senator (Seneca), one was a former slave (Epictetus), and one was the emperor of Rome (Marcus). The philosophy spoke to every level of society.

Compared with Seneca, Epictetus is sharper, more severe, more single-minded: everything comes back to the distinction between what is in our power and what is not. Marcus is more inward and melancholy, writing private notes to himself. Seneca is the most literary, the most worldly, the most willing to admit his own weaknesses. If Epictetus is a strict coach and Marcus a thoughtful diary, Seneca is a wise friend writing letters.

### The Buddha: same diagnosis, different cure

We have already seen the parallel on craving. There are others. The Buddha taught **impermanence** (*anicca*): everything we cling to will change and pass. Seneca's constant reminders that Fortune's gifts are borrowed, that cities can burn in a night, that we die a little every day, express the same insight. Both recommend daily practice: the Buddha, meditation and mindfulness; Seneca, reflection, evening review and rehearsing loss.

The deep difference is in the goal. For the Buddha, liberation means escaping the cycle of craving and rebirth altogether. For Seneca, the goal is to live as a good person *within* the world, fulfilling one's duties in society, guided by reason. The Buddha left the palace. Seneca stayed in the palace, which is both his strength and his problem.

### Epicurus: the rival he quoted

Seneca's school, the Stoics, and the rival Epicureans disagreed sharply. Epicurus taught that pleasure, understood as the absence of pain and anxiety, is the goal of life; the Stoics said virtue is. Yet the first thirty of Seneca's letters to Lucilius almost all end with a quotation from Epicurus! Seneca was not a narrow party man. Good ideas, he said, are common property. This openness is a good model for anyone who studies ideas: learn from opponents, not just allies.

### Nietzsche: "the toreador of virtue"

The German philosopher **Friedrich Nietzsche** had a famously sharp tongue. In *Twilight of the Idols*, listing people he found "impossible", he called Seneca "**the toreador of virtue**", a bullfighter of morality: someone who performed virtue with grand gestures and theatrical flourish. Nietzsche suspected that Seneca's fine words concealed a comfortable, compromised life. He is pointing to the same problem the senator Suillius raised: the gap between Seneca's teaching and his living.

### The Church fathers, Montaigne and the Renaissance

Early Christian writers admired Seneca so much that a forged correspondence between him and St Paul circulated for centuries, and some writers treated him almost as a Christian. In the Renaissance, the French essayist **Montaigne** drew on Seneca constantly; the essay as a form, personal, reflective and conversational, owes a great deal to Seneca's letters. Seneca's bloody tragedies, full of ghosts and revenge, shaped Elizabethan drama, including Shakespeare's early plays.

### Modern psychology

Albert Ellis, who founded rational emotive behaviour therapy in the 1950s, and Aaron Beck, who developed cognitive therapy, both drew on Stoic ideas, especially Epictetus. Seneca's analysis of anger and fear, as judgements that can be examined and changed, is at the heart of this approach. When a therapist asks you "what is the evidence for that thought?", Seneca is in the room.

## See it around you

**Screen time and the attention economy.** Entire industries are built on capturing your time. Apps are designed so that you scroll longer. Seneca's warning that we give away our time to anyone who asks has never been more relevant. The difference is that now the "asker" is often an algorithm designed by very clever people.

**The "after retirement" trap.** How many people in your family say, "Once the children are settled, once the loan is paid off, once I retire, then I'll travel, read, rest, spend time with family"? Seneca's question stings: what guarantee do you have that you will live that long, or be well enough when you do?

**Hustle culture and burnout.** Being busy has become a status symbol. "So busy, yaar!" is said with a kind of pride. Seneca would say: busy-ness is not a sign of a full life; it may be a sign of an empty one.

**Anxiety before results.** Board exams, entrance tests, job interviews, medical reports: modern life is full of waiting. Seneca's line, that we suffer more in imagination than in reality, can be a lifeline for a student lying awake the night before results.

**Road rage and online outrage.** Anger is everywhere: in traffic, in comment sections, on television debates. Seneca's remedies, delay, remembering your own faults, refusing to feed the fire, are as practical today as ever.

**Consumerism and EMIs.** Advertising works by creating new cravings. EMI schemes make it easy to buy what you do not need. Seneca's reminder that the poor person is the one who craves more is a direct challenge to a consumer economy.

**The kindness test.** How do people in your society treat domestic workers, drivers, delivery workers, cleaners? Seneca's Letter 47 is a test we can apply today. He says: judge a person by how they treat those who cannot repay them.

**The hypocrisy question.** Every time a wealthy leader preaches simplicity, or a celebrity promotes "mindfulness" while selling luxury goods, Seneca's contradiction is replayed. His life raises a question you can ask of anyone, including yourself: is it possible to live by high principles while holding power and wealth, or does power always corrupt the preacher?

## Use it

**1. Track your time like your money.** For one week, write down roughly how you spend your waking hours. Then ask Seneca's question: if someone tried to take this much money from me, would I allow it? Decide what you will reclaim.

**2. The night review.** Every night, spend five minutes asking Sextius' questions: What weakness did I resist today? Where did I fall short? What will I do better tomorrow? Be honest but kind to yourself, as Seneca was: "This time I pardon you."

**3. Test your fears.** When anxiety rises, write the fear down. Then ask: Is it actually happening? What's the evidence? If it happened, how would I cope? Is worrying now helping at all?

**4. Practise a little poverty.** Once a month, spend a day or a weekend living very simply: basic food, no shopping, no luxuries. Notice that you are fine. Feel your fear of loss shrink.

**5. Delay your anger.** Make a rule: when angry, wait. Don't send the message, don't reply to the comment, don't say the sentence, for at least an hour. Most of the time, you will be glad you waited.

**6. Read one book deeply.** Instead of skimming fifty articles, choose one great book and read it slowly. Each day, write down one sentence to carry with you.

**7. Choose your crowds.** Notice which places, online or offline, leave you more envious, angry or anxious. Spend less time there. Notice which people leave you better. Spend more time with them.

**8. Treat everyone as one body.** Learn the names of the people who serve you: the security guard, the cleaner, the delivery worker. Speak to them as equals. It is a small thing, and it is exactly what Seneca asked of a Roman slave-owner.

**9. Remember: now.** When you catch yourself planning to live "later", ask what you could do today, even in a small way.

## Where he went wrong

**1. The hypocrisy problem.** Seneca preached poverty and owned a fortune. He praised freedom and served a murderer. He wrote about mercy while drafting justifications for Nero's crimes. His defenders say he was trying to restrain Nero from within, and that leaving might have made things worse. His critics say he was enjoying power and wealth and dressing it in fine words. The truth is probably mixed. Either way, the gap between his words and his life is real, and he partly admitted it.

**2. Accommodation with slavery.** Seneca urged kindness to slaves but never questioned the institution of slavery itself. He also owned many slaves. His argument that "everyone is a slave to something" can even be used to make actual slavery seem less important, which is a dangerous move.

**3. Too much detachment?** The Stoic ideal of treating loved ones, health and life as "indifferent" can feel cold. In his letters of consolation to grieving people, Seneca sometimes urges them to limit their mourning in a way that can seem harsh. Grief, many would say today, is not a mistake to be corrected but a natural expression of love that needs time and expression.

**4. Anger can be useful.** As Aristotle argued, and as many modern thinkers agree, anger at injustice can motivate necessary change. Seneca's total rejection of anger may ask too much, and may even suit those in power, who prefer the oppressed to stay calm.

**5. A philosophy for the elite.** Seneca's advice on time, leisure and reading assumes that you have the freedom to arrange your days. For a labourer working fourteen hours to feed a family, "use your time wisely" can sound like a privileged luxury. Seneca had little to say about the structures that steal people's time, as opposed to the choices that waste it.

**6. Flattery when it suited him.** His consolation to Polybius, written to get himself recalled from exile, is full of praise for the emperor Claudius. After Claudius died, Seneca mocked him savagely. A man whose words change with his interests is hard to trust completely.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you probably thought of time as something you *have* plenty of, and of "being busy" as a sign of a full life. Wealth was about how much you earn. Fear and anger were things that simply *happen* to you.

**Now,** you can see time as the one thing you truly own, and the thing you most easily give away. You can see that wealth depends as much on what you crave as on what you have. And you can see that much of your fear and anger come from judgements you can examine, delay and change.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **Your screen-time report** stops being a statistic. It becomes a record of a part of your life you gave away.
- **"I'll do it after..."** stops sounding reasonable. It starts to sound like a gamble with time you may not have.
- **The fear the night before a result** becomes something you can question: is this real, or am I suffering in imagination?
- **A salary hike** stops being a reason to raise your spending. It becomes a chance to buy something rarer: freedom.
- **The person who cuts you off in traffic** becomes a test you can pass, by waiting, breathing, and letting it go.
- **The delivery worker at your door** becomes, in Seneca's phrase, someone born of the same stock, under the same sky.
- **Your own contradictions** stop being a reason to give up on being good. Seneca never became a sage either. He just kept trying, every day, and wrote it down.

Seneca's last gift to his friends was the pattern of his life, flaws included. His gift to you is simpler: the reminder that the day you are living right now is the only one you can be sure of.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Lucius Annaeus Seneca (c. 4 BCE – 65 CE), born in Spain; senator, writer, exile, tutor and adviser to Nero, and one of the richest men in Rome.
- **Philosophy:** Stoicism, made practical and personal; only virtue is truly good, and our judgements, not events, are in our power.
- **Big ideas:** time is our only true possession; we suffer more in imagination than in reality; rehearse loss to become free of fear; real poverty is craving; anger is a brief madness; philosophy is daily practice; accepting death frees us from slavery; all humans are one body.
- **Life and death:** recalled from exile to educate Nero, compromised by the court, ordered to kill himself in 65 CE, and died following Socrates' example.
- **Legacy:** shaped Christian moral writing, Montaigne's essays, Shakespeare's drama and modern cognitive therapy.
- **Criticism:** the gap between his preaching and his wealth and service to Nero; acceptance of slavery; a sometimes cold view of grief and anger.

**Key terms**

- **Stoicism:** a philosophy holding that virtue is the only true good and that we control our judgements, not events.
- **Preferred indifferents:** things like health and wealth that are reasonable to prefer but not truly good in themselves.
- **Premeditatio malorum:** deliberately imagining possible misfortunes to prepare the mind.
- **Occupati:** Seneca's word for the "busy" people who never truly live.
- **Fortune:** luck or chance, which can give and take all external goods.
- **Cosmopolis:** the Stoic idea that all humans are citizens of one world-community.
- **Evening review:** the nightly practice of examining one's day.
