## Life

Lucius Annaeus Seneca was born around 4 BCE in Corduba (modern Córdoba) in southern Spain, the son of a wealthy equestrian family. His father, Seneca the Elder, was a celebrated writer on rhetoric. Brought to Rome as a child, the younger Seneca studied rhetoric and philosophy, attending the lectures of the Stoic Attalus and the Sextian school, which taught a vegetarian, self-examining asceticism. Despite lifelong ill health, possibly asthma, he rose quickly as an orator and entered the Senate.

His brilliance made him enemies. In 41 CE the emperor Claudius exiled him to Corsica on a charge of adultery with the emperor's niece Julia Livilla. Eight years later Claudius' new wife, Agrippina, recalled him to tutor her son, the twelve-year-old **Nero**. When Nero became emperor in 54, Seneca and the praetorian prefect Burrus effectively guided the government, and the first years of the reign were remembered as a time of good administration.

The arrangement soured. Seneca was implicated, at least in the cover-up, in Nero's murder of Agrippina in 59, and he amassed one of the largest fortunes in Rome, which critics then and since have found hard to square with his praise of simplicity. After Burrus died in 62 he withdrew from public life and devoted himself to writing. In 65 CE Nero accused him of complicity in the Pisonian conspiracy and ordered him to take his own life. According to the historian Tacitus, he did so with Stoic composure, consoling his weeping friends and dictating to his secretaries to the end.

## Key ideas

Seneca was not an original system-builder but a gifted teacher and stylist. He applied Stoic principles to the problems of ordinary life in a vivid, epigrammatic Latin, and he was willing to borrow good ideas even from the rival Epicureans.

**Time is our only true possession.** "It is not that we have a short space of time, but that we waste much of it." We guard our money jealously but give away our hours to anyone who asks. A life is long if we know how to use it.

**Practising philosophy daily.** Philosophy is medicine for the soul. Seneca recommends evening self-examination, reading a little each day and digesting it, and imagining misfortunes in advance so that they lose their power to shock: "he robs present ills of their power who has perceived their coming beforehand."

**Anger as temporary madness.** In *On Anger* he argues that anger is never useful. It is a judgement gone wrong, and can be checked by delay, by understanding the wrongdoer, and by remembering our own faults.

**Death and freedom.** Seneca writes about death constantly, not morbidly but to strip it of terror. "He who has learned to die has unlearned slavery."

**Common humanity.** In his famous 47th letter he urges kind treatment of slaves: the person you call a slave "sprang from the same stock" and "is smiled upon by the same skies" as you.

## Influence and legacy

Early Christians admired Seneca so much that a forged correspondence between him and St Paul circulated for centuries. His tragedies, with their ghosts, revenge and rhetoric, profoundly shaped Renaissance drama, including Shakespeare and Elizabethan revenge tragedy. Montaigne drew on him constantly, and the essay form owes him a great deal. Today his letters are central texts of the modern Stoic revival, and the tension between his ideals and his life makes him one of antiquity's most human philosophers.
