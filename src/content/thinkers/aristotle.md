## Life

Aristotle was born in 384 BCE in Stagira, a small town in northern Greece. His father, Nicomachus, was court physician to the Macedonian king Amyntas III, and medicine's habit of careful observation may have shaped the son's temperament. At seventeen he went to Athens and joined Plato's **Academy**, where he remained for about twenty years, first as a student and then as a teacher, until Plato's death in 347 BCE.

He then spent several years around the Aegean. At Assos he married Pythias, niece of the local ruler Hermias. On the island of Lesbos, with his colleague Theophrastus, he carried out pioneering studies of marine life. In 343 BCE Philip II of Macedon invited him to tutor his thirteen-year-old son, the future **Alexander the Great**. How much the philosopher shaped the conqueror is unknown.

In 335 BCE Aristotle returned to Athens and founded his own school, the **Lyceum**. His followers became known as Peripatetics, perhaps from the covered walkway (*peripatos*) where they met. There he lectured, built a great library, and directed collaborative research, including a survey of the constitutions of 158 Greek cities. When Alexander died in 323 BCE, a wave of anti-Macedonian feeling swept Athens and Aristotle was charged with impiety. He withdrew to Chalcis on Euboea, reportedly saying he would not let the Athenians "sin twice against philosophy", and died there the following year.

## Key ideas

Aristotle's polished dialogues, praised in antiquity for their style, are lost. What survives, about a third of his output, consists largely of lecture notes: dense, technical, and astonishing in range.

**Logic.** Aristotle invented formal logic. His theory of the **syllogism** ("All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal") identified valid patterns of inference and remained the core of logic for over two thousand years.

**Substance, form and matter.** Against Plato's separate Forms, Aristotle held that the primary realities are individual things: this horse, this person. Each is a compound of **matter** and **form**, the organising structure that makes it the kind of thing it is. The soul is the form of a living body, not a separate prisoner within it.

**The four causes.** To understand anything we must know its material cause (what it is made of), formal cause (its structure), efficient cause (what produced it) and final cause (its purpose or end). Nature, for Aristotle, is thoroughly purposive, or **teleological**: things develop from potentiality to actuality.

**Virtue and happiness.** The highest human good is ***eudaimonia***, flourishing or living well, which consists in activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life. Moral virtues such as courage and generosity are stable dispositions acquired by habit, and each lies in a **mean** between excess and deficiency: courage lies between rashness and cowardice. Applying them well requires ***phronesis***, practical wisdom.

**Politics.** Humans are "political animals" who achieve their good only in a community. Aristotle classified constitutions by whether one, few or many rule, and whether in the common interest or their own, and he favoured a mixed "polity" anchored by a strong middle class. He also defended slavery as natural for some people and held women to be naturally subordinate, views long criticised.

## Influence and legacy

Aristotle's influence is unmatched in breadth. Preserved and commented on by Islamic philosophers such as Avicenna and Averroes, who was known simply as "the Commentator", his works returned to Latin Europe in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Thomas Aquinas fused them with Christian theology and called Aristotle simply "the Philosopher", and Dante called him "the master of those who know".

The Scientific Revolution overturned his physics and cosmology, but his biology impressed Darwin, who wrote that his own heroes were "mere school-boys to old Aristotle". In the twentieth century philosophers such as Elizabeth Anscombe and Alasdair MacIntyre revived **virtue ethics**, and his *Poetics* still shapes how we think about tragedy, plot and catharsis.
