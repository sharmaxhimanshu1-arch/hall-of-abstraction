## Life

Edmund Burke was born in Dublin in 1729 to a Protestant father, an attorney, and a Catholic mother, an inheritance that made him lifelong sensitive to the plight of Ireland's Catholic majority. He was educated at a Quaker school and at Trinity College Dublin, then went to London in 1750 to study law at the Middle Temple. He soon abandoned law for letters. His *Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful* (1757) became one of the most influential works of eighteenth-century aesthetics, and he founded and edited the *Annual Register*, a yearly review of world affairs.

In 1765 Burke became private secretary to the Marquess of Rockingham, leader of a Whig faction, and entered the House of Commons, where he sat for nearly thirty years. He was among the greatest parliamentary orators of his age, though his speeches were often too long and learned for his audience; he was nicknamed "the dinner bell" because members left to eat when he rose. He defended the role of political parties, urged conciliation with the American colonists rather than war, campaigned for relief for Irish Catholics, and spent years leading the impeachment of Warren Hastings, governor-general of India, for corruption and cruelty. Hastings was eventually acquitted.

When the French Revolution broke out in 1789, many British Whigs welcomed it. Burke did not. His *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790) predicted, years before the Terror, that the Revolution would end in bloodshed and military dictatorship. The book sold widely, provoked famous replies from Thomas Paine and Mary Wollstonecraft, and cost Burke his friendship with the Whig leader Charles James Fox. Burke retired from Parliament in 1794. His only son, Richard, died the same year. He died at his estate near Beaconsfield in 1797.

## Key ideas

**Against abstract rights.** The French revolutionaries sought to rebuild society from first principles, from abstract "rights of man". Burke argued that real rights are concrete inheritances, like the "rights of Englishmen" secured by centuries of law, and that metaphysical rights applied to complex societies produce chaos.

**Tradition and "prejudice".** Inherited customs and institutions contain a wisdom accumulated over generations that exceeds what any single generation can work out for itself. Burke defended "just prejudice", habits of feeling and judgement that let people act rightly without endless calculation.

**Society across generations.** Society is a contract, but "a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born." No single generation has the right to tear it up.

**Reform to preserve.** Burke was no reactionary. He supported the American colonists and the Glorious Revolution of 1688, which he saw as a restoration of ancient liberties. "A state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation." Change should be gradual and organic.

**The "little platoon".** Our public affections begin with "the little platoon we belong to in society": family, neighbourhood, local community.

**Representation.** In his speech to the electors of Bristol, Burke argued that an elected member should use his own judgement for the good of the whole nation, not simply follow voters' instructions.

## Influence and legacy

Burke is regarded as the founder of modern Anglo-American conservatism, and his *Reflections* is still its foundational text. His influence runs through nineteenth-century thinkers such as Coleridge and Disraeli and twentieth-century writers such as Michael Oakeshott and Russell Kirk. Liberals have also admired his defence of constitutional limits, his attacks on imperial abuses in India and Ireland, and his scepticism of utopian schemes.

The line most often attributed to him, "All that is necessary for the triumph of evil is that good men do nothing", does not appear in his writings, although his genuine warning that "when bad men combine, the good must associate" expresses a similar thought.
