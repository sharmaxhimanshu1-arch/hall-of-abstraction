## Start here

There is an old house in your family. Your grandfather built it. It has strange things about it: a staircase in an awkward place, a small room nobody uses, a wall that seems to serve no purpose, a courtyard that wastes space.

You've just started earning, and you've been reading about modern architecture. You have a plan. Knock it all down. Build a clean, rational, modern flat, with every room designed for maximum efficiency. Your grandmother looks at you and says quietly, "Beta, before you break that wall, find out why your grandfather built it."

You laugh. But then, out of curiosity, you ask. And you find out: the wall holds up the roof. The awkward staircase is where it is because the ground underneath is soft. The courtyard keeps the house cool in May without a single air conditioner. The "useless" room is where the family gathers when someone dies, and it's been used that way for fifty years.

Now you're not so sure.

This is the whole of **Edmund Burke**'s philosophy in one small story. Burke was an eighteenth-century Irish-born politician and writer who looked at the French Revolution, a movement to knock down an old, unjust society and rebuild it according to pure reason, and predicted, years before it happened, that it would end in terror, chaos and military dictatorship. He was right.

From his warnings came a whole way of thinking about politics called **conservatism**: the belief that society is not a machine you can redesign on paper, but a living thing, grown over centuries, full of hidden wisdom; that change should be gradual and careful; and that people who promise to remake the world from scratch usually end up destroying it.

But don't jump to conclusions. Burke was no simple defender of the powerful. This same man spent fourteen years trying to bring the British governor of India to justice for crimes against Indians. He defended the American colonists against London. He fought for Irish Catholics against discrimination. He attacked slavery.

So who was he: a reactionary or a reformer? A defender of privilege or a voice of conscience? By the end of this page, you'll see why the answer is "both, in a way", and why Burke's grandmother-like question, "do you know why this was built?", is one of the most important questions anyone can ask before changing anything, from a family tradition to a nation.

## The world he was born into

### Ireland: a divided island

Burke was born in **Dublin** in **1729**. Ireland was then ruled by Britain, and its society was cut through by religion. The majority of Irish people were **Catholic**, but power, land and political rights were held by a small **Protestant** minority, known as the "**Protestant Ascendancy**".

Under the so-called **Penal Laws**, Catholics were barred from voting, from Parliament, from most professions, from buying land on equal terms, from bearing arms, and from running schools. Think of it as legalised discrimination against a majority by a minority, enforced by the power of a neighbouring empire.

Burke's family sat right on this fault line. His father was a Protestant lawyer (who may have converted from Catholicism to keep his profession); his **mother was Catholic**, and he married a Catholic woman. He knew, from inside his own family, what religious discrimination meant.

### Britain: a mixed constitution

When Burke moved to London, he entered a Britain proud of its "**mixed constitution**": power divided between the **King**, the **House of Lords** (the aristocracy) and the **House of Commons**. Britons believed this balance, settled after the "**Glorious Revolution**" of **1688**, when Parliament replaced King James II with William and Mary, made them the freest people in Europe.

But the system was far from democratic. Only a small fraction of men could vote. Many seats in the Commons were controlled by rich patrons. The King still had great power through patronage, giving out jobs and pensions to loyal MPs.

### An expanding empire

Britain was becoming a world empire. In Burke's lifetime it won Canada and much of India, and lost its thirteen American colonies. The **East India Company**, a private trading company, became the ruler of **Bengal** after the Battle of **Plassey** (1757) and the grant of the **diwani** (revenue rights) in **1765**. Its officials, known in Britain as "**nabobs**", returned home fabulously rich, buying estates and seats in Parliament.

### France: the volcano

And across the Channel was **France**, the largest and most powerful kingdom in Europe, ruled by an absolute monarch, with a privileged aristocracy and church, a burdened peasantry, a bankrupt treasury, and a brilliant intellectual class, the **philosophes** of the Enlightenment, like Voltaire and Rousseau, who criticised everything in the name of reason. In **1789**, the volcano erupted.

## The life, as a story

### A Dublin boyhood

Edmund Burke was born in Dublin on **12 January 1729**. As a boy he spent some years in the countryside of County Cork among his mother's Catholic relatives, and attended a Quaker school at **Ballitore** run by a kind schoolmaster, Abraham Shackleton. He formed a lifelong friendship with the schoolmaster's son.

He then studied at **Trinity College Dublin**, where he founded a debating club, and threw himself into literature, history and philosophy.

### A struggling writer in London

In **1750**, he went to London to study law at the **Middle Temple**, as his father wanted. Like Bentham after him, he soon abandoned law. Unlike Bentham, he did so to become a writer, which disappointed his father deeply, and he had little money for years.

In **1757**, he published ***A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful***, a study of why some things strike us as beautiful (small, smooth, delicate) and others as **sublime** (vast, dark, powerful, terrifying, like a storm at sea or a towering mountain). It was widely read and influenced later thinkers, including Kant. In the same year he married **Jane Nugent**, the Catholic daughter of an Irish doctor who had treated him. Their marriage was happy and lasted all his life.

He also began editing the ***Annual Register***, a yearly survey of world events, and moved in a brilliant circle of friends, including the writer **Samuel Johnson**, the painter **Joshua Reynolds**, and the actor **David Garrick**. Johnson, not easily impressed, said that if you met Burke under a shed while sheltering from the rain for five minutes, you would say, "this is an extraordinary man".

### Into politics

In **1765**, Burke became private secretary to the **Marquess of Rockingham**, the leader of a group of Whigs (one of Britain's two political factions), who briefly became Prime Minister. The same year, Burke entered the **House of Commons**. He would sit there for nearly thirty years.

Burke never became a minister of great power. He was an Irishman of modest birth in a Parliament dominated by English aristocrats. Some mocked his accent and suspected his Catholic connections. His power came from his **ideas** and his **words**: he was one of the greatest orators and political writers in the history of the English language, though his speeches were often so long and dense that MPs went off to dinner. He was nicknamed "**the dinner bell**".

### Defending party and Parliament

In ***Thoughts on the Cause of the Present Discontents*** (1770), Burke defended the idea of **political parties**, then widely considered to be selfish factions. A party, he wrote, is "a body of men united, for promoting by their joint endeavours the national interest, upon some particular principle in which they are all agreed". Good people must organise to resist bad ones: "When bad men combine, the good must associate".

(By the way, you may have seen the famous quote, "The only thing necessary for the triumph of evil is for good men to do nothing", attributed to Burke. Scholars have never found it in his writings. The real line, about good men "associating", is close in spirit, but the famous version seems to be a later invention.)

### Bristol and the duty of a representative

In **1774**, Burke was elected MP for **Bristol**, then Britain's second city. After the election, he gave a famous speech explaining his idea of what an elected representative should be. He was not a delegate who simply did what voters told him:

"Your representative owes you, not his industry only, but his judgment; and he betrays, instead of serving you, if he sacrifices it to your opinion."

He then did exactly what he said. He supported freer trade for Ireland and relief for Catholics, both unpopular with Bristol's merchants. In **1780**, he lost their support and stood down from Bristol. He found another seat, at **Malton**.

### America: conciliation, not force

As the American colonies moved toward revolt, Burke argued that Britain should **conciliate** them rather than force them. In his great ***Speech on Conciliation with America*** (1775), he argued that the Americans had inherited the English love of liberty; that you cannot indict a whole people; and that the attempt to coerce them would fail and destroy the empire. "A great empire and little minds go ill together," he warned. He was ignored, and he was right.

### India: the great cause

But the cause that consumed Burke most, for **fourteen years**, was **India**.

As he studied the East India Company's rule in Bengal, he became convinced it was a system of plunder. Young Englishmen went out to India, made fortunes, and returned, while Indians suffered famine, extortion and the destruction of their institutions. In his ***Speech on Fox's East India Bill*** (1783) he described the young Company officials: "animated with all the avarice of age, and all the impetuosity of youth", they came "wave after wave", with nothing before the eyes of the natives "but an endless, hopeless prospect of new flights of birds of prey and passage".

He focused his anger on **Warren Hastings**, Governor-General of Bengal from 1773 to 1785. Burke accused him of extortion, bribery, the plundering of the Begums of Awadh, the oppression of the Raja of Benares, and the judicial murder of **Maharaja Nandakumar**, who had accused Hastings of corruption and was then hanged for forgery.

Burke led the campaign to **impeach** Hastings, to put him on trial before the House of Lords. The trial opened in **February 1788** in Westminster Hall, one of the great spectacles of the age. Burke's opening speech lasted **four days**. He ended:

"I impeach him in the name of the people of India, whose laws, rights, and liberties he has subverted..."

His central argument was revolutionary for its time. Hastings's defenders said that Asia had always been ruled by despots, so a British governor could rule Indians despotically too. Burke rejected this with fury. There is, he said, one moral law for all humanity. Indians had their own ancient laws, rights and civilisation. A British governor in India was bound by the same justice as one in England. "Geographical morality", one standard for Europe and another for Asia, was a lie.

The trial dragged on for **seven years**. In **1795**, the Lords **acquitted** Hastings. Burke had lost. But he had put the morality of empire on trial before the British public, and the principle that colonial rulers are accountable to justice remained.

### The French Revolution

Then came **1789**. In France, the Estates-General met, the **Bastille** fell, the Declaration of the **Rights of Man and of the Citizen** was proclaimed, feudal privileges were abolished, and Church lands were seized. In Britain, many liberals and radicals were delighted. Burke's own friend and Whig ally, **Charles James Fox**, called the fall of the Bastille "the greatest event that ever happened in the world, and how much the best".

Burke was horrified, almost from the start.

In November 1789, a dissenting minister, **Richard Price**, preached a sermon in London praising the Revolution and suggesting Britain should follow France's principles. Burke responded with a book in the form of a long letter to a young Frenchman: ***Reflections on the Revolution in France***, published in **November 1790**.

It was a sensation. It sold some 30,000 copies within a couple of years, an enormous number for the time. It provoked dozens of replies, the most famous being **Thomas Paine's** *Rights of Man* (1791) and **Mary Wollstonecraft's** *A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790).

What made the *Reflections* so remarkable was its prophecy. In 1790, the Revolution still looked moderate: the King was alive, the constitution was being written, and the bloodshed seemed limited. Burke predicted that the Revolution, having destroyed all traditional sources of authority, would descend into violence, confiscation, persecution and war, and would end with a popular **general** seizing power. Over the next decade came the **execution of the King** (1793), the **Reign of Terror** (1793–94) with tens of thousands killed, wars across Europe, and in **1799** the rise of **Napoleon Bonaparte**.

### The break with Fox

The Revolution split the Whigs. In a dramatic Commons debate in **May 1791**, Burke declared his friendship with Fox at an end. Fox wept. They never reconciled. Burke spent his last years writing against the Revolution and urging war against it, in works like the *Letters on a Regicide Peace*.

### Last years and grief

In **1794**, Burke's only surviving son, **Richard**, on whom he had pinned his hopes, died of tuberculosis, aged thirty-six. Burke was crushed. He wrote that he lived "in an inverted order": those who should have succeeded him had gone before him.

He retired to his estate at **Beaconsfield**, where he ran a school for the sons of French émigrés. When a duke attacked his government pension, Burke replied with the ferocious ***Letter to a Noble Lord*** (1796), one of the great pieces of English prose, pointing out that the duke's wealth came from royal grants and confiscated monastery land, while his own pension was earned by work.

He died on **9 July 1797**, aged sixty-eight. He asked to be buried quietly, and secretly, fearing that if the French revolutionaries ever invaded Britain they might dig up his body.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Society is a partnership across generations

**The idea in one line:** Society is not a deal between people alive today, but a partnership between the dead, the living and the unborn.

Many Enlightenment thinkers, like **Locke** and **Rousseau**, explained government as a "**social contract**": an agreement made by individuals who come together to form a society for their own benefit. If the contract no longer serves them, they can tear it up and make a new one.

Burke took this image and transformed it:

"Society is indeed a contract... it becomes a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born."

Think about what this means. You were born into a language you did not invent. A religion or culture you did not design. Laws, institutions, roads, songs, festivals, scientific knowledge, all built by people who are now dead. You are the temporary trustee of an inheritance. You did not create it, and you have a duty to pass it on, improved but not destroyed, to people not yet born.

So, Burke says, you are not free to tear it all down simply because you, today, have a clever idea. The dead have a vote, through the institutions they built. The unborn have a stake, because they will inherit what you leave.

Think of a forest. You might cut it down in a day for a quick profit. But it took centuries to grow, and your grandchildren will live in the desert you leave. That's how Burke saw society.

**Why it matters:** This idea gives us a way to think about **intergenerational** responsibility: climate change, debt, heritage, ecology. When environmentalists say "we have not inherited the earth from our ancestors, we have borrowed it from our children", they echo Burke. Indian tradition also has a version of this: the idea of "**rin**", debts owed to ancestors (pitr rin), teachers (rishi rin) and the gods (dev rin), which a person must repay through their life.

**A common misunderstanding:** Burke didn't mean that the past must rule the present, or that nothing can change. He meant that change should respect what has been inherited, and should be made by people who understand they are **trustees**, not owners.

### Idea 2: Against abstract reason in politics

**The idea in one line:** Politics cannot be run by abstract theories; it requires practical wisdom about real circumstances.

The French revolutionaries believed they could design a perfect society using reason. They wrote a Declaration of universal **Rights of Man**. They abolished old provinces and replaced them with geometrical "departments". They introduced a new calendar, starting from Year One of the Republic, with ten-day weeks. They even tried to replace Christianity with a "Cult of Reason". Everything old was suspect; everything would be rebuilt on rational principles.

Burke thought this was madness. Not because reason is bad, but because **politics is not geometry**. Real societies are enormously complex. They contain millions of people, with different needs, histories, habits and loyalties, connected in ways no single mind can understand. A theory that looks perfect on paper ignores most of this complexity.

He wrote that the "**metaphysic rights**" of the revolutionaries, entering into common life, are like rays of light which pierce into a dense medium and are "refracted from their straight line". Real human rights are always bent by circumstances.

What politics needs instead is **prudence**: the practical wisdom of the experienced statesman who knows how things actually work, weighs circumstances, and acts cautiously. Burke called prudence "the god of this lower world". It is the same virtue Aristotle called *phronesis*.

Think of medicine. A medical student knows all the theory. But an experienced doctor knows that each patient is different, that textbook symptoms don't always appear, that the strongest drug may kill the patient. Good doctors are cautious, attentive and practical. So, Burke says, should statesmen be.

**Why it matters:** The twentieth century gave terrible proof of the danger Burke saw. **Soviet** collectivisation in the 1930s, which forced peasants onto collective farms according to Marxist theory, caused famines that killed millions. China's **Great Leap Forward** (1958–62), which tried to transform the economy overnight, led to one of the deadliest famines in history. Cambodia's **Khmer Rouge** declared "Year Zero" and tried to abolish money, cities and families, killing perhaps a quarter of the population. In each case, rulers certain of their theory crushed the complex reality of human society.

The political scientist **James C. Scott** wrote a famous book, *Seeing Like a State* (1998), about how grand state schemes fail because they ignore local, practical knowledge, the kind of knowledge farmers, craftspeople and communities have built up over generations. It's a very Burkean book.

**A common misunderstanding:** Burke was not against reason or thinking. He was against **abstract** reason that ignores experience and circumstances. He thought the wisdom of many generations, tested by experience, is usually more reliable than the reasoning of a few clever individuals.

### Idea 3: The wisdom of prejudice

**The idea in one line:** Inherited habits and instincts, which Burke called "prejudices", often contain more wisdom than any individual's reasoning.

This is one of Burke's most provocative ideas. The word "prejudice" today means unfair bias, like racial or caste prejudice. Burke used it more broadly: **pre-judgements**, beliefs and habits we hold without reasoning them out each time, because they were handed down to us.

He wrote that the English, instead of casting away their old prejudices, cherished them. Why? Because:

"We are afraid to put men to live and trade each on his own private stock of reason; because we suspect that this stock in each man is small, and that the individuals would do better to avail themselves of the general bank and capital of nations, and of ages."

In other words: each of us is not very wise. But humanity, over centuries, has accumulated a vast store of wisdom, stored in customs, habits, manners, institutions, religion. Instead of trying to work everything out from scratch, we'd do better to draw on that "bank".

And prejudice has a practical advantage:

"Prejudice renders a man's virtue his habit; and not a series of unconnected acts."

Think about it. A person who has been raised to be honest doesn't calculate, every time they find a wallet, whether returning it will maximise happiness. They simply return it. Their honesty is a **habit**, part of their character. That makes them reliable, especially in moments of temptation or crisis when there's no time to reason. A person who must reason everything out from scratch may reason themselves into anything.

Consider customs like touching elders' feet, sharing food with guests, or the joint family looking after aged parents. A clever young person might say, "These are irrational traditions." Burke would ask: do you understand what they do? They build respect, hospitality, security in old age, bonds between generations. Remove them, and you may find that nothing replaces their function.

**Why it matters:** There's a famous principle called "**Chesterton's fence**", from the English writer G.K. Chesterton. Imagine you find a fence across a road, and you can't see why it's there. The reformer says, "I don't see the use of this; let's clear it away." The wiser person says, "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. When you can tell me why it was put there, I may allow you to destroy it." That is Burke's idea in a sentence: **understand before you destroy**.

**A common misunderstanding:** This idea can be, and has been, used to defend real injustice. Caste, untouchability, the oppression of women: all were defended as ancient "wisdom". Burke himself fought against some inherited injustices (the Penal Laws in Ireland, the plunder of India, slavery). The real test he implies is whether an institution, tested by long experience, serves human well-being, not merely whether it is old. But critics are right that his respect for tradition gives too much benefit of the doubt to customs that serve the powerful. Ambedkar's answer to tradition-worship was sharp: when the "wisdom of the ages" has kept millions in degradation, it is not wisdom.

### Idea 4: Reform, not revolution

**The idea in one line:** Change is necessary, but it should be gradual, careful and built on what already exists.

Burke is often misunderstood as being against all change. He wasn't. He wrote:

"A state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation."

A society that cannot change will break. But change should follow a certain method. Burke praised the British approach of improving the constitution slowly, preserving its continuity, "**reforming**" rather than "**innovating**".

He contrasted two models:

- **Revolution**: tear everything down and build anew according to a plan.
- **Reform**: repair, adjust and improve the existing structure, keeping what works, changing what doesn't, step by step.

He compared good political change to the way nature works: gradually, keeping the overall body while renewing its parts, the way an old tree grows new leaves each spring while its trunk stays. He even described the ideal: "a disposition to preserve, and an ability to improve, taken together, would be my standard of a statesman."

Think of it like repairing a ship at sea. You can't put the ship in dry dock and rebuild it, because everyone on board would drown. You must replace planks one by one, while the ship keeps sailing. That's how Burke thought societies must be changed.

**Why it matters:** This contrast, reform versus revolution, runs through modern history. Britain avoided violent revolution in the nineteenth century through a series of gradual reforms (extending the vote step by step in 1832, 1867, 1884, 1918 and 1928). France had revolution after revolution.

Look at India. Gandhi's movement, in some ways, was deeply Burkean: rooted in tradition, suspicious of modern industrial civilisation (read *Hind Swaraj*), cautious about violence. Yet Gandhi also aimed at profound change, like the abolition of untouchability. And the Indian Constitution itself, while transformative, kept much of the colonial administrative structure, the Government of India Act 1935, the civil services, the common law, adapting rather than replacing. The Constitution also provides for its own **amendment**, and the Supreme Court's "**basic structure**" doctrine (from *Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973) holds that Parliament can amend but not destroy the Constitution's essential features: a remarkably Burkean balance between change and continuity.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Gradual change" can be used as an excuse for no change at all. People benefiting from injustice always say "not yet, slowly, the time isn't right". Martin Luther King Jr. answered this in his *Letter from Birmingham Jail* (1963): for those suffering injustice, "wait" has almost always meant "never". Burke's method is wise when the existing system is basically sound; it's much harder to defend when the system is fundamentally unjust.

### Idea 5: Real rights versus "metaphysical" rights

**The idea in one line:** People's real rights are those secured by inherited law and institutions, not abstract declarations of universal rights.

The French Declaration proclaimed universal "**rights of man**". Burke didn't deny that people have rights. He said that the **real rights** of men are the concrete benefits of living in a civil society: the right to justice, to the fruits of their labour, to inherit from their parents, to instruction in life and consolation in death.

The English, he argued, didn't claim their liberties as abstract "rights of man" but as "**an entailed inheritance**", passed down from their ancestors, like an estate, through documents like **Magna Carta** (1215) and the **Bill of Rights** (1689). This made their liberties concrete, secure and rooted.

Abstract rights, by contrast, are dangerously open-ended. If every person has an abstract "right" to equality, to liberty, to happiness, then every existing institution can be denounced as a violation, and every revolutionary can claim to be fulfilling the rights of man while doing terrible things.

**Why it matters:** This debate continues today, for example between universal **human rights** (like the UN's Universal Declaration of 1948) and arguments that rights must be rooted in particular cultures and traditions. Critics of universal rights sometimes use Burkean arguments, saying rights must fit local circumstances; defenders reply that such arguments can excuse oppression. The Indian Constitution blends both: universal Fundamental Rights, alongside respect for religious freedom, cultural rights of minorities and community institutions.

**A common misunderstanding:** Burke's critique of abstract rights doesn't mean he didn't care about justice. His prosecution of Hastings was based on a universal principle: that there is one moral law for all people, whether in England or India. He objected to abstract rights used as **revolutionary weapons**, not to the idea that all humans deserve justice.

### Idea 6: The little platoon

**The idea in one line:** Love of the nation and humanity grows out of love for the small communities we belong to.

Burke wrote:

"To be attached to the subdivision, to love the little platoon we belong to in society, is the first principle (the germ as it were) of public affections. It is the first link in the series by which we proceed towards a love to our country, and to mankind."

A "platoon" is a small unit of soldiers. Burke means our families, neighbourhoods, villages, churches, guilds, schools, clubs. We first learn to love and care within these small circles. Out of that experience grows love for the larger community, the nation, and finally humanity.

The French revolutionaries, by contrast, tried to dissolve all intermediate groups, abolishing guilds, the Church's role, provincial identities, so that there would be only the individual citizen and the State. Burke thought this would leave individuals isolated and powerless before the State.

Think of a resident welfare association, a temple or gurdwara committee, a women's self-help group, a cricket club, an alumni association. These "little platoons" teach cooperation, responsibility and loyalty. They also stand between the individual and the state, giving people support and a voice.

**Why it matters:** Sociologists today talk about "**social capital**": the networks of trust and cooperation that make societies work. The American political scientist **Robert Putnam**, in *Bowling Alone* (2000), argued that the decline of clubs and associations in America weakened democracy. Burke saw this two centuries earlier. India's tremendous web of community organisations, from langar kitchens to cooperative societies, are its little platoons.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Little platoons" can also be exclusive and oppressive: caste panchayats that punish inter-caste marriage, communities that ostracise dissenters. Love of one's own group can turn into hatred of others. Burke's idea works best when the little platoons are open, humane, and connected to a wider sense of justice.

### Idea 7: The representative's judgement

**The idea in one line:** An elected representative should use his own judgement for the good of the whole nation, not just obey voters' instructions.

Recall Burke's speech to the electors of Bristol: "Your representative owes you, not his industry only, but his judgment; and he betrays, instead of serving you, if he sacrifices it to your opinion."

He added that Parliament is "a deliberative assembly of one nation, with one interest, that of the whole", not "a congress of ambassadors from different and hostile interests". A member is chosen by Bristol, but once chosen, "he is not member of Bristol, but he is a member of Parliament".

This is called the "**trustee**" model of representation, as opposed to the "**delegate**" model, where representatives simply do what their constituents want.

**Why it matters:** Think of an MP whose constituents want something that harms the country as a whole, or a minority. Should she follow their wishes or her conscience? India's **anti-defection law** (the Tenth Schedule, added in 1985) adds a further layer: MPs must generally follow their **party's** whip, not their own judgement or their voters'. Critics argue this has reduced Parliament to a counting of party votes and destroyed independent deliberation. Burke would have had strong views.

**A common misunderstanding:** The trustee model can become an excuse for representatives ignoring their voters entirely. Burke himself paid the price: Bristol voted him out. The question is one of balance: representatives should listen, explain, and be accountable, while not merely following every passing opinion.

### Idea 8: Empire must answer to justice

**The idea in one line:** Power over other peoples is a trust, and those who abuse it must be held accountable.

This idea, which drove Burke's India campaign, is perhaps his most important for Indian readers. Burke argued that the East India Company's power over India was a **trust**: held for the benefit of the people of India, not for the profit of the Company. When that trust is abused, it must be taken back.

His attack on "**geographical morality**" was central. Hastings's defence argued that India had always been ruled by despots, so arbitrary power was simply the "custom of the country". Burke replied that the laws of morality are the same everywhere, and pointed out that India had its own ancient legal traditions, including laws restraining rulers. Asian peoples were not a lesser breed, fit only for despotism.

This was rare in eighteenth-century Britain. Burke admired Indian civilisation and saw Indians as people with their own laws, religions, nobility and dignity. He described the princes and people of India as being of an ancient and cultivated civilisation, at a time when most of his countrymen saw only "natives" to be exploited.

**Why it matters:** Burke's campaign was an early argument that colonial power must be accountable. Indian nationalists later found in his speeches powerful material against British rule. Even when Hastings was acquitted, the trial established in British political culture the idea that rulers of an empire answer to moral law. In a sense, Burke's moral argument against the Company was part of the long road to its abolition in 1858.

**A common misunderstanding:** Burke did not oppose the British Empire as such. He wanted it to be just and accountable, not abolished. And his admiration for Indian tradition partly reflected his conservative respect for established hierarchies, including Indian princes and priests. He was a reformer of empire, not an anti-imperialist.

### Idea 9: The sublime and the beautiful

**The idea in one line:** Beauty attracts us through pleasure; the sublime overwhelms us through awe mixed with fear.

Before politics, Burke wrote about **aesthetics**, the philosophy of art and beauty. He distinguished:

- The **beautiful**: small, smooth, gentle, delicate, gradual. Think of a flower, a gentle river, a child's face. It produces love and pleasure.
- The **sublime**: vast, dark, powerful, infinite, obscure, terrifying. Think of the Himalayas, a storm at sea, a thunderstorm, a vast desert, a dark temple interior. It produces **astonishment**, a feeling of awe mixed with fear, as long as we are safe enough not to be actually destroyed.

The sublime, he said, is the strongest emotion the mind is capable of feeling.

**Why it matters:** Burke's theory influenced **Romantic** art and poetry, Kant's aesthetics, and even modern ideas about why people seek out horror films, roller coasters and extreme mountaineering. It also connects to his politics: Burke saw society itself as something vast and awe-inspiring, not a small machine to be taken apart. And he thought political authority needed a certain mystery and grandeur to command loyalty. The revolutionaries, he felt, had stripped away all the "pleasing illusions" that made power gentle and obedience noble.

Think about why governments build grand monuments, hold parades and preserve ceremonies. They are drawing on the sublime.

**A common misunderstanding:** The sublime isn't just "very beautiful". It's a different feeling entirely, one that includes fear and smallness. Standing at the foot of a great mountain or before the sea, you feel tiny. That smallness is part of the experience.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Aristotle and Montesquieu: the masters

Burke's emphasis on **prudence** and practical wisdom comes from **Aristotle**, who taught that ethics and politics are not exact sciences but require judgement about particular circumstances. From **Montesquieu**, whom he called the greatest genius of his age, Burke learned that laws must fit a people's history, climate, religion and customs, and that the balance of powers protects liberty. Like Montesquieu, Burke admired the British constitution precisely because it had grown rather than been designed.

### Locke: the 1688 inheritance

**John Locke** defended the Glorious Revolution of 1688 by appealing to natural rights and a social contract. Burke also defended 1688, but in a completely different way: not as an exercise of the people's right to choose their rulers, but as a careful **restoration** of the ancient constitution, changing as little as possible. Richard Price, in the sermon that provoked the *Reflections*, claimed that 1688 gave the British people the right to "cashier" their governors for misconduct and frame a government for themselves. Burke denied this flatly. The same historical event, two opposite lessons.

### Smith: friends in the market

Burke and **Adam Smith** were friends who admired each other. Both distrusted grand schemes imposed from above, and both believed in the spontaneous order of society and markets. Burke wrote on economics too, notably *Thoughts and Details on Scarcity* (1795), arguing against government regulation of wages and grain prices. There is a well-known story that Smith told Burke that he was the only man who, without communication, thought on economic subjects exactly as he did, though we have this only second-hand.

### Paine and Wollstonecraft: the great replies

**Thomas Paine** responded to the *Reflections* with ***Rights of Man*** (1791), which sold even more copies. Paine argued that no generation has the right to bind future generations: "the vanity and presumption of governing beyond the grave is the most ridiculous and insolent of all tyrannies." He mocked Burke's grief for the French Queen while ignoring the suffering of the poor: "He pities the plumage, but forgets the dying bird."

**Mary Wollstonecraft**, in *A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790), attacked Burke for defending inherited property and privilege that kept the poor in misery, and for his sentimental view of women. Two years later she wrote *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, a founding text of feminism.

Picture the debate:

*Paine:* The living have the right to govern themselves. The dead have no rights.

*Burke:* The living are the trustees of what the dead built. You can't just dissolve the partnership.

*Paine:* Your "partnership" is a deal made by aristocrats to keep their privileges forever.

*Burke:* And your "rights of man" will end in the guillotine.

Both, in a sense, were proved right. The French Revolution did turn bloody. But the principles Paine defended, democracy, equal rights, the end of hereditary privilege, gradually spread across the world.

### Bentham: strange bedfellow

**Jeremy Bentham**, the radical utilitarian, also attacked the French Declaration of Rights, calling natural rights "nonsense upon stilts". So Burke and Bentham agreed that abstract rights were dangerous. But Bentham wanted to sweep away the old English institutions Burke revered, and rebuild law on the principle of utility. Burke would have seen Bentham's scheme as another version of the same rationalist arrogance.

### Marx: the sycophant

**Karl Marx** despised Burke. In *Capital* he called him a "sycophant" who, "in the pay of the English oligarchy", played the romantic praiser of times past against the French Revolution, while also being a vulgar bourgeois in his economics. For Marx, Burke's reverence for tradition was simply a defence of the property and power of the ruling class. Burke's "wisdom of ages" was, for Marx, the "ideas of the ruling class".

### Later voices

- **Hegel** shared Burke's sense that society is an organic, historical whole.
- **Alexis de Tocqueville** reflected, like Burke, on how the French Revolution centralised power.
- **Michael Oakeshott** (1901–1990) wrote that being conservative is "to prefer the familiar to the unknown, to prefer the tried to the untried".
- **Friedrich Hayek** (1899–1992), though he insisted he was not a conservative, developed the Burkean idea that society's order emerges from countless individual actions and traditions that no planner can understand.
- In India, figures like **C. Rajagopalachari** in his later years voiced Burkean caution about rapid state-led transformation, while others found in Burke a defender of Indian civilisation against colonial contempt.

## See it around you

**"Why was this fence built?"** In every office, school or family, someone wants to scrap an old rule that seems pointless. Sometimes they're right. Sometimes the rule was protecting something nobody remembers. A company that scraps a "useless" safety procedure and then suffers an accident has learned Chesterton's fence the hard way.

**Debates about social reform.** India's history is full of Burke-versus-reformer debates. When the **Hindu Code Bills** were proposed in the late 1940s and 1950s to reform marriage, divorce and inheritance for Hindus, many opponents argued that ancient customs could not be overturned by legislation. Ambedkar resigned from the cabinet in 1951 partly in frustration at the delay. The bills were eventually passed in parts (1955–56). Was the gradual approach wisdom or cowardice? People still debate it.

**Sudden policy shocks.** When governments introduce sweeping changes overnight, the debate often follows Burkean lines: supporters praise bold action against an old, corrupt system; critics warn that complex systems react in unexpected ways, and that people at the bottom pay the price. Whatever your view of any particular policy, the Burkean question is worth asking: did the planners understand how the existing system actually worked?

**Regime change abroad.** In **2003**, the United States invaded **Iraq**, removed Saddam Hussein, and dismantled the army and ruling party, hoping to build a democracy from scratch. Instead came years of insurgency and civil war. Similar stories played out in Libya and Afghanistan. Many commentators, from left and right, invoked Burke: you cannot simply destroy a society's institutions and expect a new order to grow overnight.

**Heritage and development.** When an old neighbourhood, temple, market or forest is cleared for a new project, the conflict between "progress" and "inheritance" is Burke's conflict. Heritage conservation laws, debates about redeveloping historic city centres, and movements to save sacred groves all have a Burkean dimension.

**Joint family to nuclear family.** Many Indian families are going through a quiet revolution, from joint to nuclear households, from arranged to self-chosen marriages, from village to city. Burke would ask: as we change, what functions did the old forms serve (care of the elderly, childcare, support in crisis), and how will we replace them? Ignoring that question leaves lonely old people and overstretched young parents.

**Representatives and the party whip.** Every time an MP votes against their conscience because of the anti-defection law, or against their constituents' wishes on a matter of principle, the trustee-versus-delegate debate Burke began comes alive.

## Use it

**1. Ask Chesterton's question first.** Before changing any rule, system or tradition, at work, at home, in your society, ask: why was this built? What problem was it solving? What will fill its role if I remove it? Only after you can answer should you decide.

**2. Change in steps, and watch.** When you want to change something big, your study routine, your company's processes, a family arrangement, try small, reversible steps first. See what happens. Adjust. Big-bang changes leave no room to learn from mistakes.

**3. Respect inherited wisdom, but test it.** When an elder gives advice that seems old-fashioned, don't dismiss it immediately; there may be experience behind it. But then test it against reason and justice. Burke gives you the first half of the habit; reformers like Ambedkar give you the second.

**4. Be suspicious of perfect plans.** When someone, a politician, a guru, a business consultant, a revolutionary, promises that a single grand plan will fix everything, ask what they're ignoring. Complex things rarely respond to simple fixes.

**5. Invest in your little platoons.** Join, build and care for the small communities around you: family, neighbours, colleagues, clubs, volunteer groups. They give meaning, support and a voice. A society of isolated individuals is fragile.

**6. Think like a trustee.** Whatever you've inherited, a family business, a home, a culture, a planet, you're holding it for those who come after. Ask what state you want to pass it on in.

**7. Use your judgement when it counts.** In a team or organisation, you'll often be asked to follow the crowd. Burke's Bristol speech reminds you that sometimes the duty of a leader is to say "I understand what you want, and I believe it would be wrong", and to accept the consequences.

**8. Hold power to the same moral standard everywhere.** Burke's attack on "geographical morality" applies to how we treat people in other places, in other castes, in other classes. A wrong done to a domestic worker, a migrant labourer, or someone far away is as wrong as one done to your neighbour.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Too gentle with privilege.** Burke's respect for tradition often meant defending the aristocracy, inherited wealth and a hierarchical social order. He famously wrote that learning would be "trodden down under the hoofs of a swinish multitude", a phrase radicals never let him forget. He was far more moved by the fall of the French Queen than by the centuries of misery of French peasants. Paine's jibe, "he pities the plumage, but forgets the dying bird", hits home.

**2. Tradition can be oppression.** The "wisdom of ages" has defended slavery, caste, the subordination of women, untouchability, colonialism. Ambedkar, Phule and Periyar fought traditions that had been "tested by time" precisely because time had made the suffering normal. A purely Burkean approach makes it very hard to challenge injustices that are deeply rooted.

**3. Who speaks for the dead?** Burke's partnership of the dead, the living and the unborn sounds noble. But the "dead" never speak; living people speak for them, usually those who benefit from existing arrangements. Paine asked why the living should be ruled by the wishes of people long gone.

**4. The French Revolution wasn't only terror.** Burke saw the Revolution's dangers clearly, but missed its achievements: the end of feudal privileges, legal equality, religious toleration for Protestants and Jews, the Napoleonic Code, the spread of the idea that government should serve citizens. Much of modern Europe, and the modern world, rests on these.

**5. Empire reformed, not ended.** Burke's campaign against Hastings was brave, but he wanted a better empire, not freedom for India. He accepted that Britain had a right to rule India, provided it ruled justly. Indian nationalists later had to go far beyond him.

**6. Gradualism can mean "never".** For those suffering oppression, the call for slow, careful change often means waiting forever. Some changes, like the abolition of untouchability or of slavery, needed decisive breaks, not gentle reforms.

**7. Idealising the past.** Burke's picture of the old French monarchy and English constitution was rosier than the reality. The "ancient constitution" he praised gave power to a tiny elite; the "chivalry" he mourned had been accompanied by poverty and arbitrary power.

**8. Fear can justify repression.** In his last years, Burke urged war against France and supported harsh measures against radicals at home. Fear of revolution can easily become an excuse for suppressing legitimate demands for change.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought of tradition as either sacred or stupid, and of change as simply good or bad. You may have thought of "conservatives" as people who resist progress out of selfishness, or of "reformers" as wise people who see what others don't.

**Now,** you can see society as something more like a living tree or an old house than a machine: grown over generations, full of hidden supports, easy to damage and slow to repair. You can see that every reform must ask what it is replacing, and that clever plans on paper often fail in reality. And you can see the other side too: that inherited institutions often protect the powerful, that the dead don't speak for themselves, and that "slowly, carefully" can be a way of saying "never".

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A rule that seems pointless** makes you ask why it was made, before you break it.
- **A grand plan** promising to fix everything at once makes you look for what it's ignoring.
- **Your family's customs** become something to understand before you accept or reject them.
- **Your neighbourhood association or club** looks like a small school of citizenship.
- **The environment, the Constitution, your language** feel like inheritances you hold in trust for the unborn.
- **A demand to "wait for gradual change"** makes you ask who is being asked to wait, and at what cost.
- **A wrong done far away**, to people of another place or class, feels just as wrong, because there is no geographical morality.

Edmund Burke was the man who warned Britain against tearing France apart, and also the man who demanded justice for Bengal. Learning to hold both, the respect for what we've inherited and the courage to judge it by justice, is the beginning of political wisdom.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Edmund Burke (1729–1797), Irish-born British statesman, orator and writer; MP for nearly thirty years; founder of modern conservatism.
- **Context:** discrimination against Irish Catholics, Britain's "mixed constitution" of 1688, the East India Company's rule in Bengal, the American and French Revolutions.
- **Causes:** conciliation with America, relief for Irish Catholics, the impeachment of Warren Hastings for crimes against the people of India.
- ***Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790):** predicted terror and military dictatorship.
- **Society:** a partnership of the dead, the living and the unborn.
- **Against abstraction:** prudence and practical wisdom over theory; "prejudice" as stored wisdom.
- **Change:** "a state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation"; reform, not revolution.
- **Little platoons:** love of community as the root of public affection.
- **Representation:** the representative owes you his judgement (the trustee model).
- **Critics:** Paine, Wollstonecraft, Marx; accused of defending privilege, romanticising the past and ignoring the suffering of the poor.

**Key terms**

- **Conservatism:** a political outlook that values tradition, continuity and gradual change.
- **Prudence:** practical wisdom about how to act in specific circumstances.
- **Prejudice (Burke's sense):** inherited habits and judgements that carry accumulated wisdom.
- **Little platoon:** the small community a person belongs to, the first school of public affection.
- **Trustee model:** the idea that representatives should use their own judgement for the common good.
- **Geographical morality:** the view, which Burke rejected, that moral standards differ by region.
- **Impeachment:** a trial of a public official by Parliament for serious misconduct.
- **Sublime:** an experience of awe mixed with fear, produced by vastness and power.
- **Chesterton's fence:** the principle that you should understand why something exists before removing it.
- **Glorious Revolution:** the 1688 replacement of James II by William and Mary, the basis of Britain's constitutional monarchy.
