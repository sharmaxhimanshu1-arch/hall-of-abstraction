## Start here

Imagine you are an ordinary person, with no army, no money, no weapons, and you are facing the most powerful empire the world has ever seen. It has guns, ships, police, prisons and the law on its side. What can you do?

You could take up arms, but you would almost certainly lose, and many people would die. You could beg, through petitions and polite speeches, but the empire can ignore you. You could give up.

Or you could do something strange. You could break an unjust law **openly**, tell the authorities in advance exactly what you are going to do, refuse to hit back when they beat you, and fill their prisons until they run out of room. You could make your suffering so visible, and so dignified, that the whole world, including the people who support your rulers, begins to feel ashamed.

In 1930, a thin, bald, sixty-year-old man in a loincloth did exactly this. He walked about 390 kilometres to the sea with a band of followers, picked up a handful of salt, and broke the British law that made salt a government monopoly. Within weeks, millions of Indians were breaking the salt laws, and some sixty thousand were in jail. Pictures and reports of unarmed marchers being beaten went around the world.

His name was **Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi**. India calls him **Bapu** (father) and the **Mahatma** (great soul). He called his method **satyagraha**, "holding firmly to truth", or "truth-force".

But Gandhi is much more complicated than the face on our currency notes. He was a lawyer and a saint, a politician and a crank, a reformer who fought untouchability and a Hindu who defended the idea of varna for much of his life. He was loved by millions and criticised, sharply, by **Tagore** and by **Ambedkar**. He was shot by a Hindu nationalist who blamed him for being too soft on Muslims.

By the end of this page you will understand how this man thought, why his ideas still shake the world, from Martin Luther King to Nelson Mandela to every peaceful protest you see on the news, and where his critics were right.

## The world he was born into

### A princely state in Gujarat

Gandhi was born in **1869** in **Porbandar**, a small coastal princely state in **Kathiawar**, Gujarat. India was then under the British Crown, but about a third of it was made up of princely states ruled by Indian kings under British supervision.

His family belonged to the **Modh Bania** caste of merchants, but his father and grandfather had served as **diwans** (chief ministers) of small princely states.

### A religious mosaic

Gujarat was a meeting place of religions. Gandhi's mother was a devout **Vaishnava** Hindu, but she also belonged to the **Pranami** sect, which drew on both Hindu and Islamic texts. The family had friends among **Jains**, **Muslims** and **Parsis**. Jain monks visited the house. From Jainism especially, the young Gandhi absorbed ideals of **ahimsa** (non-violence to all living things), fasting, vows and self-discipline.

### Empire at its height

When Gandhi was a young man, the British Empire was at its peak, covering a quarter of the world. Indians were taught, in schools and courts, that British rule was a blessing that had brought railways, law and order. Many educated Indians believed it. Indian nationalism was still a matter of the small, polite **Indian National Congress** (founded in 1885), which passed resolutions and sent petitions.

### Indians abroad

Indians had also been carried across the empire as **indentured labourers**, to Fiji, the Caribbean, Mauritius and **South Africa**, where they worked on sugar plantations under harsh contracts. Indian traders followed. In South Africa, they faced racist laws and daily humiliation. That is where Gandhi would discover himself.

## The life, as a story

### A shy, frightened boy

Mohandas was born on **2 October 1869**, the youngest child of **Karamchand Gandhi** and his fourth wife, **Putlibai**. Putlibai was deeply religious, fasting often and keeping difficult vows, and her example of self-discipline stayed with him all his life.

He was, by his own account in his autobiography, a shy, mediocre student, afraid of ghosts, thieves, snakes and the dark. At **thirteen**, he was married to **Kasturba**, also thirteen, in an arranged child marriage. He later condemned child marriage bitterly.

As a teenager he went through a rebellious phase. Under the influence of a friend, he secretly ate meat (to become strong like the British, his friend said), smoked, and once stole a bit of gold to pay a debt. Overcome by guilt, he wrote a confession to his father, who was ill in bed. His father read it, wept silently and tore it up. Gandhi said those tears cleansed him, and taught him the power of **ahimsa**: his father's love and silent suffering changed him more than punishment could have.

### London: the vegetarian lawyer

In **1888**, at eighteen, he sailed to **London** to study law, after promising his mother he would avoid wine, women and meat. He tried for a while to become an English gentleman, buying fine clothes and taking dancing and violin lessons, then gave it up.

In London he found the **Vegetarian Society** and joined its committee. Through vegetarian friends and **Theosophists**, he read the **Bhagavad Gita** for the first time, in Edwin Arnold's English translation, *The Song Celestial*. He also read the Bible, and the **Sermon on the Mount**, "resist not evil... whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also", went straight to his heart.

He was called to the bar in **1891** and returned to India, where he failed as a lawyer: at his first case in a Bombay court, he was so nervous that he couldn't speak, sat down, and returned the fee.

### South Africa: the train at Pietermaritzburg

In **1893**, he took a job with an Indian trading firm in **South Africa**. A week after arriving, travelling by train from Durban to Pretoria with a first-class ticket, he was ordered by a white passenger and an official to move to the third-class van, because Indians ("coolies") were not allowed in first class. He refused. At **Pietermaritzburg** station, on a cold winter night, he was thrown off the train with his luggage.

He sat shivering in the waiting room all night, thinking: should he go back to India? Or stay and fight? He decided to stay. He later called this the most creative experience of his life.

### Twenty-one years of struggle

He stayed in South Africa, on and off, for **twenty-one years**. He became a successful lawyer and the leader of the Indian community. He founded the **Natal Indian Congress** in **1894**, wrote petitions, organised the community, and started a newspaper, *Indian Opinion*.

In **1904**, on a train journey, he read **John Ruskin's** *Unto This Last*, a critique of industrial capitalism, and was so moved that he decided to change his life overnight. He founded the **Phoenix Settlement** near Durban, a farm where everyone did manual labour and lived simply. (He later translated Ruskin's book into Gujarati as *Sarvodaya*, "the welfare of all".)

In **1906**, he took a vow of **brahmacharya** (celibacy and self-control), which he kept for the rest of his life.

That same year, the government of the Transvaal passed a law requiring all Indians to register and be fingerprinted, the so-called "**Black Act**". At a mass meeting in Johannesburg's Empire Theatre on **11 September 1906**, Indians took a solemn vow to defy it, whatever the consequences. This was the birth of **satyagraha**. Gandhi needed a name for this new kind of struggle; he held a competition in *Indian Opinion*, and from a suggestion, "sadagraha", he coined the word **satyagraha**.

For the next eight years, thousands of Indians in South Africa, including women and indentured labourers, defied unjust laws, burned their registration certificates, crossed borders illegally, went on strike and went to jail. In **1913**, Gandhi led a great march of striking miners. In **1914**, the government made significant concessions.

He also corresponded with the aged Russian novelist **Leo Tolstoy**, whose writings on non-violent resistance he admired, and named a second community **Tolstoy Farm** (1910).

On a ship back from London in **1909**, he wrote, in ten days, his manifesto ***Hind Swaraj*** (*Indian Home Rule*), a fierce critique of modern civilisation and a vision of true self-rule.

### Return to India

Gandhi returned to India in **January 1915**, aged forty-five, already famous. On the advice of his political mentor, **Gopal Krishna Gokhale**, he spent a year travelling around India by third-class train, observing, listening, silent. He founded an **ashram** at **Ahmedabad**, on the banks of the Sabarmati.

His first campaigns in India were local and practical:

- **Champaran** (Bihar, 1917): he investigated the oppression of indigo farmers forced to grow indigo for British planters. When ordered to leave the district, he refused and said he would accept the penalty. The government backed down, and an inquiry led to reforms.
- **Kheda** (Gujarat, 1918): he supported peasants refusing to pay land tax after crop failure.
- **Ahmedabad** (1918): he supported mill workers striking for higher wages, and went on his first public fast to keep the strike non-violent.

### Non-cooperation

In **1919**, the British passed the **Rowlatt Acts**, allowing imprisonment without trial. Gandhi called a nationwide **hartal** (strike). Then came the massacre at **Jallianwala Bagh** in April. Gandhi, who had until then believed in the British Empire's fairness, turned against it.

In **1920**, he launched the **Non-Cooperation Movement**: Indians were asked to boycott British courts, schools, titles, legislatures and cloth. Lawyers left their practices, students left colleges, foreign cloth was burned, and Indians spun their own **khadi**. The movement joined with the **Khilafat** campaign of Indian Muslims (protesting the treatment of the Ottoman Caliph), bringing Hindus and Muslims together in mass struggle.

In **February 1922**, at **Chauri Chaura** in Uttar Pradesh, a crowd of protesters set fire to a police station, killing twenty-two policemen. Gandhi, horrified, **called off the entire movement**. Many of his colleagues were furious. He was arrested soon after and sentenced to six years in prison. At his trial, he pleaded guilty, and asked the judge either to resign or to give him the heaviest penalty. He was released early, in 1924, after an operation.

### The Salt March

On **12 March 1930**, Gandhi set out from Sabarmati Ashram with seventy-eight followers on a march to **Dandi**, on the coast, about 390 kilometres away. Salt, needed by every Indian, rich or poor, was taxed by the British and its production was a government monopoly. On **6 April**, at Dandi, Gandhi picked up a lump of natural salt from the shore, breaking the law.

The act was simple, symbolic and brilliant. Across India, people made salt illegally, boycotted foreign cloth and liquor shops, and courted arrest. At **Dharasana** salt works, unarmed volunteers marched in line toward police who beat them with steel-tipped lathis; they fell, and the next line came forward. The American journalist **Webb Miller** reported the scene to the world.

The **Civil Disobedience Movement** forced the British to negotiate. In **1931**, Gandhi went to London for the **Second Round Table Conference**, wearing his loincloth and shawl to meet the King.

### The Poona Pact

In **1932**, the British government announced the **Communal Award**, granting **separate electorates** to the "Depressed Classes" (Dalits), as **B.R. Ambedkar** had demanded: Dalit voters would elect their own Dalit representatives. Gandhi, then in Yerawada jail, believed this would split Hindu society permanently, and began a **fast unto death**.

Under enormous pressure, with Gandhi's life at stake and fears of violence against Dalits if he died, Ambedkar agreed to the **Poona Pact** (September 1932): instead of separate electorates, there would be **reserved seats** for the Depressed Classes within general constituencies, with more seats than the Award had given. Gandhi ended his fast. Ambedkar later described the pact bitterly, as something forced on him.

After the fast, Gandhi devoted himself to a campaign against untouchability. He founded the **Harijan Sevak Sangh**, started a weekly called ***Harijan*** ("children of God", his name for Dalits, which many Dalits later rejected as patronising), and toured India campaigning for temple entry and the end of untouchability.

### Quit India

In **August 1942**, in the middle of the Second World War, Gandhi launched the **Quit India Movement**, telling Indians: "**Do or Die**." The British arrested him and the entire Congress leadership within hours. The movement turned violent in places and was crushed. Gandhi's wife **Kasturba** and his long-time secretary **Mahadev Desai** both died while imprisoned with him at the Aga Khan Palace in Pune.

### Partition and the last fasts

As independence approached, Hindu-Muslim relations collapsed into terrible violence. Gandhi opposed the **Partition** of India but could not prevent it. While Delhi celebrated independence on **15 August 1947**, Gandhi was in **Calcutta**, fasting and praying to stop riots. In **Noakhali** (in present-day Bangladesh), he had walked barefoot from village to village, trying to restore peace.

In **January 1948**, he fasted again in Delhi, to stop the killing and to press the Indian government to pay Pakistan its share of assets. Many Hindus who had suffered in the Partition violence were furious at him.

On **30 January 1948**, on his way to evening prayers at **Birla House** in Delhi, he was shot three times at close range by **Nathuram Godse**, a Hindu nationalist who blamed him for appeasing Muslims and for the Partition. He died, according to witnesses, with the name of God, "He Ram", on his lips (some historians question this detail).

Nehru told the nation: "The light has gone out of our lives, and there is darkness everywhere."

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Truth is God

**The idea in one line:** The search for truth is the highest aim of life; truth is God itself.

Gandhi called his autobiography ***The Story of My Experiments with Truth***. For most of his life he said "God is Truth". Later, around **1931**, he reversed it: "**Truth is God**." Why the change? Because, he said, some people deny God, but no one can deny truth. Even an atheist seeks truth. So making truth the highest value opened his religion to everyone.

What did truth mean for Gandhi? Several things at once: telling the truth, living truthfully (consistency between what you say and what you do), and seeking the ultimate truth of existence. He admitted that he saw only relative truth, and must hold it humbly, ready to be corrected. That is why he called his life a series of **experiments**.

Think about this. Most of us want to be right. Gandhi wanted to find the truth, even if it proved him wrong. He changed his mind often and publicly, and admitted his mistakes. In 1919, after calling a mass protest before people were ready for non-violence, he publicly called it a "**Himalayan miscalculation**".

**Why it matters:** This approach makes political life a moral search rather than a game of winning. If you treat your opponent's view as possibly containing truth, you argue differently.

**A common misunderstanding:** Gandhi was not saying that he had the truth. He insisted that no one has the whole truth, which is precisely why violence against others is wrong: you might be killing someone who is right.

### Idea 2: Ahimsa, non-violence as a force

**The idea in one line:** Non-violence is not weakness or passivity; it is the active, courageous refusal to harm, and it is stronger than violence.

**Ahimsa** means non-harming. Gandhi took it from Jainism, Buddhism and Hinduism, and from the Sermon on the Mount, and gave it a political form.

For Gandhi, ahimsa was not just not hitting back. It included not hating your opponent, not wishing him harm, not even speaking of him with contempt. And it was active: it required courage, sacrifice, and a willingness to suffer.

He wrote:

"Non-violence is the law of our species as violence is the law of the brute."

And he distinguished non-violence from cowardice. He said that if the only choice were between cowardice and violence, he would advise violence. But non-violence, he said, is infinitely superior to both: it is the courage of one who could hit back but chooses not to.

Think about the Dharasana salt works. Volunteers walked toward armed police, knowing they would be beaten, without raising their hands. That is not weakness; it is a kind of courage few soldiers have.

**Why it matters:** Gandhi showed, on a vast scale, that organised non-violence could challenge a great empire. His methods inspired the **American civil rights movement** (Martin Luther King Jr. called Gandhi's method "the only morally and practically sound method open to oppressed people"), the anti-apartheid struggle, the fall of communist regimes in Eastern Europe, and countless movements today.

**A common misunderstanding:** The quote "An eye for an eye makes the whole world blind" is often attributed to Gandhi, but there's no reliable evidence he said it. Neither did he say "Be the change you wish to see in the world" in those words; he did say something close in spirit: that if we could change ourselves, the tendencies in the world would also change.

### Idea 3: Satyagraha, truth-force

**The idea in one line:** Satyagraha is a method of struggle that uses truth and self-suffering, not violence, to convert the opponent and change an unjust situation.

**Satyagraha** combines *satya* (truth) and *agraha* (firmness, holding on). It means holding firmly to truth.

How does it work? The satyagrahi:

1. **Identifies an injustice** and tries all reasonable means to resolve it: negotiation, petitions.
2. **Announces openly** what he or she will do. No secrecy, no surprise.
3. **Breaks the unjust law or refuses cooperation**, non-violently.
4. **Accepts the penalty** willingly, without running away or hitting back.
5. **Seeks to convert, not defeat**, the opponent: to touch their conscience, so that they change their mind, not just give in.
6. **Is always ready to negotiate and compromise** on non-essentials.

The key idea is **self-suffering**. By willingly suffering for a cause, the satyagrahi shows how serious it is, exposes the injustice to the world, and appeals to the humanity of the opponent and of onlookers.

Gandhi often stressed that satyagraha required **discipline**. That is why he called off the Non-Cooperation Movement after Chauri Chaura: if the people were not ready for non-violence, the movement had to stop.

**Why it matters:** Satyagraha is a practical toolkit for the powerless: boycotts, strikes, marches, sit-ins, fasts, civil disobedience. Look at any modern protest movement and you'll see its methods.

**A common misunderstanding:** Satyagraha isn't just "protesting peacefully". It is a demanding discipline with ethical rules: openness, willingness to suffer, no hatred, readiness to negotiate. Protest that seeks to humiliate opponents or that turns violent is not satyagraha in Gandhi's sense.

### Idea 4: Means and ends

**The idea in one line:** Good ends cannot be reached by bad means; the means are the end in the making.

Many revolutionaries believe that the end justifies the means: violence, lies and cruelty are acceptable if they lead to a just society. Gandhi rejected this completely. In ***Hind Swaraj***, he wrote:

"The means may be likened to a seed, the end to a tree; and there is just the same inviolable connection between the means and the end as there is between the seed and the tree."

If you plant a neem seed, you will not get a mango tree. If you use hatred and violence to win freedom, you will get a society of hatred and violence. If you use lies, you will build a society of lies.

Think about history. Revolutions that used terror to build a perfect society, like the French Terror or Stalin's Soviet Union, often produced new tyrannies. Gandhi saw this logic clearly.

**Why it matters:** This principle applies to politics, business and personal life. Can you build a good company through dishonest practices? A good family through fear? A good country through hate? Gandhi says no.

**A common misunderstanding:** Gandhi didn't say that only means matter and results don't. He cared intensely about results: freedom, justice, an end to untouchability. He said that the only reliable way to good results is through good means.

### Idea 5: Swaraj, self-rule

**The idea in one line:** True freedom is not just political independence but self-rule: rule over oneself, and self-governance by communities.

What is freedom? In *Hind Swaraj*, Gandhi's reader (the book is a dialogue between an Editor and a Reader) wants the British out and a strong, modern, independent India with its own army and factories. The Editor (Gandhi) replies that this would be "English rule without the Englishman": India would have the tiger's nature but not the tiger.

**Swaraj**, for Gandhi, had several layers:

- **Self-control**: the individual's rule over his own desires, anger and fear. "It is swaraj when we learn to rule ourselves."
- **Village self-rule**: self-sufficient, self-governing village communities.
- **National independence**: freedom from British rule.

Without the first two, he believed, the third would be hollow. A people who are slaves to their own greed, fear and hatred cannot be truly free, whoever rules them.

**Why it matters:** Gandhi's swaraj challenges us to ask what freedom actually means. Is a country free if its people are trapped in poverty, caste and consumerism? Is a person free if she is ruled by her desires and fears? The Indian Constitution's provisions for **panchayati raj** (village self-government, strengthened in 1992) reflect part of Gandhi's vision.

**A common misunderstanding:** Gandhi's swaraj was not about national power or glory. He did not want India to become a great military or industrial power on the Western model. Many nationalists, including Nehru, wanted exactly that, and independent India largely followed Nehru, not Gandhi, in this.

### Idea 6: Swadeshi, khadi and the critique of modern civilisation

**The idea in one line:** Real independence requires economic self-reliance, simple living and local production, not imitation of industrial civilisation.

**Swadeshi** means "of one's own country". For Gandhi, it meant using goods made close to home, by your neighbours, rather than imported or mass-produced goods. The **charkha** (spinning wheel) and **khadi** (hand-spun, hand-woven cloth) became its symbols.

Why the spinning wheel? British rule had destroyed India's handloom industry; machine-made cloth from Manchester flooded India, and millions of spinners and weavers lost their livelihood. Spinning, Gandhi said, would give every idle hand in the villages work, bring back self-reliance, and unite rich and poor in a common task. Spinning was also a kind of meditation.

In *Hind Swaraj*, he went further, attacking **modern civilisation** itself: its railways, doctors, lawyers and machines. He argued that it multiplied wants endlessly and made people slaves of their bodies and comforts. A civilisation, he said, should help people do their duty and control their desires, not satisfy every craving.

**Why it matters:** Gandhi was one of the first great critics of consumerism and industrial excess. Today, with climate change and ecological crisis, his idea that "the earth provides enough to satisfy every man's needs, but not every man's greed" (a saying attributed to him by his followers) sounds prophetic. Movements for local production, sustainable living and "vocal for local" echo swadeshi.

**A common misunderstanding:** Gandhi was not against all machines. He said he objected to the craze for machinery that threw people out of work and concentrated wealth in a few hands. He accepted machines like the sewing machine that saved labour without destroying livelihoods. Even so, many of his contemporaries, including Tagore, Nehru and Ambedkar, found his rejection of modern industry and medicine unrealistic.

### Idea 7: Sarvodaya and trusteeship

**The idea in one line:** A just economy aims at the welfare of all, and the rich should hold their wealth as trustees for society.

From Ruskin, Gandhi took the idea of **sarvodaya**, "the rise of all", including the last and the least. The good of the individual is contained in the good of all; the work of a barber has the same value as that of a lawyer; a life of labour is the life worth living.

Instead of class struggle, which Marx proposed, Gandhi proposed **trusteeship**: the rich should not be violently dispossessed, but should regard their wealth as held in trust for the welfare of society, keeping only what they need. If they refused, he said, non-violent non-cooperation could be used against them.

And he gave the famous "**talisman**" for testing every decision:

"Recall the face of the poorest and the weakest man whom you may have seen, and ask yourself, if the step you contemplate is going to be of any use to him."

**Why it matters:** The talisman is printed in Indian school textbooks and has been cited in policy debates. Corporate social responsibility, philanthropy by the very rich, and cooperative movements draw, loosely, on the idea of trusteeship.

**A common misunderstanding:** Critics, including Ambedkar and the socialists, argued that trusteeship was naive: the rich rarely give up their wealth voluntarily, and the idea could become an excuse to leave inequality untouched. Gandhi's defenders say he combined it with the threat of non-cooperation.

### Idea 8: Untouchability, caste and varna

**The idea in one line:** Untouchability is a sin and a crime against humanity that Hindus must end; but for much of his life Gandhi defended the idea of varna as duty, not hierarchy.

Gandhi attacked **untouchability** fiercely, more fiercely than almost any Hindu leader before him. He called it a "crime against God and man" and a blot on Hinduism. He cleaned latrines himself (the work forced on Dalits) and made his ashram residents do the same. He adopted a Dalit girl, Lakshmi, as his daughter. He campaigned for temple entry and wanted Hindus to accept untouchables as equals. In the 1930s, after the Poona Pact, he made this his central cause.

But for much of his life, he also **defended varnashrama dharma**, the ideal of four varnas, each following its hereditary occupation, as a division of duties without superiority. He opposed **inter-caste dining and marriage** in his early years, though he later changed his mind, and by the **1940s** said he would only bless marriages between a caste Hindu and a Dalit.

This is the heart of his famous clash with **Ambedkar**, who argued that varna and caste could not be separated, and that Gandhi's reformism left the system intact while making Dalits dependent on the goodwill of caste Hindus. Gandhi's word "**Harijan**" (children of God) for Dalits was rejected by many Dalits as patronising, and is now considered offensive.

**Why it matters:** This debate, between reforming Hinduism from within and rejecting caste at its root, remains at the centre of Indian politics and society.

**A common misunderstanding:** Gandhi's views on caste changed significantly over his life. Quoting his early writings as his final view is unfair; ignoring them is also unfair. The honest view is that he was a sincere enemy of untouchability who came only slowly to question the varna system that underlay it.

### Idea 9: Religious harmony and the equal respect of religions

**The idea in one line:** All religions are true, all have errors, and all deserve equal respect; Hindus and Muslims must live as brothers.

Gandhi called himself a devout **Sanatani Hindu**, but his prayer meetings included readings from the Gita, the Quran, the Bible and Buddhist texts. He said all religions were like branches of the same tree, all true, all imperfect because they were interpreted by imperfect human beings. He called this **sarva dharma samabhava**: equal respect for all religions.

He gave his life to Hindu-Muslim unity, from the Khilafat movement in 1920 to his last fasts in 1947–48. He was killed because a Hindu nationalist believed he was too sympathetic to Muslims.

**Why it matters:** Gandhi's idea of religious harmony shaped Indian secularism, which, unlike the strict separation of church and state in some countries, seeks equal respect for all faiths. Whether India lives up to it is one of the defining questions of its present.

**A common misunderstanding:** Gandhi's harmony was not a vague "all religions are the same". He took religion very seriously, argued with people of other faiths, and opposed conversion by inducement. Equal respect, for him, meant humility about one's own faith, not indifference.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Socrates: the soldier of truth

In South Africa, Gandhi translated **Plato's *Apology***, Socrates' defence speech at his trial, into Gujarati, under a title that meant "the story of a soldier of truth". Socrates refused to stop questioning even under threat of death, and accepted his execution rather than flee. Gandhi saw in him a model satyagrahi: someone who obeys conscience over the state, but accepts the state's punishment openly rather than running away.

### Vivekananda: service of the poor

From **Swami Vivekananda**, Gandhi took the ideal of serving "Daridra Narayan", God in the form of the poor, and made it central to a mass movement. But where Vivekananda called for "muscles of iron" and national strength, Gandhi insisted on non-violence and simplicity.

### Tagore: the public argument

**Rabindranath Tagore** called Gandhi "Mahatma" and Gandhi called him "Gurudev". But Tagore criticised non-cooperation as negative and isolating, the burning of foreign cloth as wasteful, the charkha as a mechanical duty that required no thought, and Gandhi's claim that the 1934 Bihar earthquake was divine punishment for untouchability as superstition. Gandhi replied respectfully, but did not change course. Their debate shows that admiration and disagreement can live together.

### Ambedkar: the deepest challenge

**B.R. Ambedkar** was Gandhi's sharpest critic on caste. He argued that Gandhi's opposition to separate electorates in 1932 was a betrayal of Dalits, and that Gandhi's fast forced him into the Poona Pact. In ***Annihilation of Caste*** (1936), Ambedkar argued that caste could not be abolished without destroying the authority of the scriptures that sanctioned it. Gandhi replied in *Harijan*, defending varna as he understood it. Ambedkar responded again.

Imagine them meeting at Yerawada jail during the fast:

*Gandhi:* Separate electorates will divide Hindu society forever. I want the untouchables to be one with the Hindus.

*Ambedkar:* One with the Hindus who will not let them drink from the same well? You want unity on the terms of those who oppressed us.

*Gandhi:* I will give my life to end untouchability.

*Ambedkar:* You have the power to end it within your own followers today, Mahatma. But you are asking us to give up the only political weapon we have, and trust in the change of heart of our oppressors.

History has not settled who was right; it has shown that both were sincere, and that the debate continues.

### Marx: trusteeship versus class war

Gandhi rejected **Karl Marx**'s idea that history is driven by class struggle and that the oppressed must overthrow the property-owning class by force. He proposed trusteeship and non-violent non-cooperation instead. Indian Marxists, in turn, criticised Gandhi for protecting the interests of landlords and industrialists, some of whom (like G.D. Birla) were his close supporters.

### Later voices

- **Martin Luther King Jr.** studied Gandhi and visited India in 1959, calling himself a "pilgrim".
- **Nelson Mandela** honoured Gandhi, while adopting armed struggle when non-violence was crushed in South Africa.
- **Albert Einstein** wrote that generations to come would scarce believe that such a one as this ever in flesh and blood walked upon this earth.
- **George Orwell** wrote a sceptical essay, "Reflections on Gandhi" (1949), admiring his courage while doubting that non-violence could work against a totalitarian regime.
- **Jawaharlal Nehru**, Gandhi's chosen heir, shared his values but led India toward the modern industrial state Gandhi had warned against.

## See it around you

**Peaceful protests.** Candle marches, sit-ins, hunger strikes, boycotts, refusing to pay unjust fees: these are Gandhian methods. The **Chipko** movement, the farmers' protests of 2020–21, Anna Hazare's anti-corruption fast in 2011, and movements across the world use his toolkit.

**Khadi and "vocal for local".** Khadi stores, handloom revival campaigns and pushes for local products echo swadeshi.

**The talisman in policy.** "Will this help the poorest person I have seen?" is a test that can be applied to any budget, scheme or business decision.

**Caste debates.** Every argument over reservations, temple entry, inter-caste marriage, manual scavenging or the word "Harijan" is part of the long Gandhi–Ambedkar debate.

**Religious tension.** When communal violence breaks out and people call for peace marches and fasts, Gandhi's example is invoked; when he is attacked or his assassin glorified, the old divisions resurface.

**Consumerism and climate.** Debates about overconsumption, fast fashion and sustainable living are, in a sense, about Gandhi's critique of endlessly multiplying wants.

**Personal discipline.** Fasting, silence days, simple living, vows: many people practise forms of self-discipline that Gandhi made political.

## Use it

**1. Run the talisman.** Before a big decision, at work, in politics or in your family, recall the face of the poorest person you know and ask how it will affect them.

**2. Check your means.** When you want something badly, ask: am I using means that will produce the kind of result I want? Shouting rarely produces respect; lies rarely produce trust.

**3. Be truthful about your mistakes.** Gandhi called one of his errors a "Himalayan miscalculation" in public. Next time you're wrong, say so clearly. It builds trust.

**4. Practise one small self-discipline.** A weekly fast from your phone, an hour of silence, a promise kept for a month. Swaraj begins with ruling yourself.

**5. Protest without hatred.** When you oppose something unjust, try to oppose the act, not the person; to persuade, not humiliate; to be open about what you're doing.

**6. Buy close to home.** Once a week, choose something made by local hands. It's a small act of swadeshi.

**7. Clean up after yourself, and others.** Gandhi made cleaning toilets a moral act, to break the caste stigma of "unclean" work. Do your own dirty work, and treat those who do such work with respect.

**8. Read the critics too.** To understand Gandhi, read Tagore's essays and Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste*. A hero understood only through his admirers is not understood at all.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Caste and varna.** As we saw, Gandhi fought untouchability but defended varna for much of his life, opposed separate electorates for Dalits, and used a fast that Ambedkar felt was coercive. Many Dalits see him as a paternalistic reformer who sought to keep them within a Hindu fold on caste Hindus' terms.

**2. Race in South Africa.** In his early years in South Africa, Gandhi campaigned for the rights of Indians, often arguing that they should not be treated like Black Africans, whom he sometimes described with racist terms common among whites at the time. His views changed later, but this record has been sharply criticised, especially in Africa.

**3. The Bihar earthquake and unreason.** His claim that the 1934 earthquake was divine punishment for untouchability showed a willingness to use superstition for a moral cause, as Tagore pointed out.

**4. Personal life.** He was often harsh with his family. His eldest son, **Harilal**, became estranged, alcoholic and bitter. Kasturba bore the burden of his experiments. In his last years, his "experiments" in brahmacharya, sleeping beside young women including his grandniece **Manu** to test his self-control, shocked many of his colleagues then and trouble many readers now.

**5. Economics.** His rejection of industrialisation and modern medicine seemed unrealistic to most of his colleagues. Independent India chose Nehru's path of industry and science, and most economists believe India could not have escaped mass poverty without it.

**6. Limits of non-violence.** Critics like Orwell asked whether non-violence could work against a regime like Hitler's, which would simply kill protesters without caring about world opinion. Gandhi's advice to Jews in Germany and to the British facing Hitler, to resist non-violently even at the cost of death, struck many as morally blind to the reality of genocide.

**7. Khilafat and communal politics.** Mixing a religious cause (the Ottoman Caliphate) with the national movement in 1920 brought Hindus and Muslims together briefly, but some historians argue it strengthened religious identity in politics, with dangerous long-term effects.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have known Gandhi as the smiling face on the currency note, the "Father of the Nation" garlanded on 2 October, a saint beyond criticism or a figure from history with no relevance today. You may have thought non-violence meant weakness or passivity.

**Now,** you can see a man who turned his own weaknesses into a lifelong experiment with truth, who discovered in a cold railway waiting room that ordinary people could resist injustice without hating or harming, and who built from that a method that shook an empire and still shapes protest across the world. You can see that for Gandhi, means and ends were inseparable, freedom began with self-rule, and every decision should pass the test of the poorest face. And you can see clearly where he was challenged, by Tagore on reason and by Ambedkar on caste, and where his critics were right.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A peaceful protest** looks like a disciplined method, not just a crowd.
- **A decision at work or in government** faces the talisman: does it help the poorest?
- **The urge to win by any means** meets the seed and the tree.
- **A khadi shirt or a local product** carries a story of self-reliance.
- **The word "Harijan"** reminds you of the debate with Ambedkar, and why Dalits rejected it.
- **A great man** can be admired and criticised at the same time.

Gandhi wrote that his life was his message. It was a message full of contradictions, mistakes and corrections, and that is exactly why it is still worth reading.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948), lawyer, political leader and moral philosopher; leader of India's non-violent freedom struggle; called Mahatma and Bapu.
- **Context:** British Empire at its height, Indian indentured labour and racism in South Africa, the Congress movement, Jallianwala Bagh, Partition.
- **Life:** child marriage at thirteen; law in London; thrown off a train at Pietermaritzburg (1893); 21 years in South Africa; satyagraha born in 1906; *Hind Swaraj* (1909); Champaran (1917); Non-Cooperation (1920–22); Salt March (1930); Poona Pact (1932); Quit India (1942); assassinated on 30 January 1948.
- **Truth:** "Truth is God"; life as experiments with truth.
- **Ahimsa:** active, courageous non-violence; not cowardice.
- **Satyagraha:** truth-force; open, non-violent resistance with self-suffering, aimed at converting the opponent.
- **Means and ends:** "as the seed to the tree".
- **Swaraj:** self-rule over oneself, villages and nation.
- **Swadeshi:** local production, khadi, critique of modern industrial civilisation.
- **Sarvodaya and trusteeship:** welfare of all; the rich as trustees; the talisman.
- **Caste:** attacked untouchability, defended varna for most of his life; clashed with Ambedkar.
- **Criticisms:** caste paternalism, early racism in South Africa, unreason, family and personal conduct, economic romanticism, limits of non-violence.

**Key terms**

- **Satyagraha:** "holding firmly to truth"; non-violent resistance.
- **Ahimsa:** non-violence, non-harming in thought, word and deed.
- **Swaraj:** self-rule, personal and political.
- **Swadeshi:** use of goods made in one's own country or locality.
- **Khadi:** hand-spun, hand-woven cloth.
- **Charkha:** spinning wheel; symbol of self-reliance.
- **Sarvodaya:** the welfare of all.
- **Trusteeship:** the idea that the rich hold their wealth in trust for society.
- **Brahmacharya:** celibacy and self-control.
- **Poona Pact:** the 1932 agreement replacing separate electorates for Dalits with reserved seats.
- **Harijan:** "children of God", Gandhi's term for Dalits, now rejected as patronising.
