## Life

John Locke was born in 1632 in Wrington, Somerset, into a Puritan family. His father, a country lawyer, fought for Parliament in the English Civil War. Locke was educated at Westminster School and Christ Church, Oxford, where he found the scholastic curriculum tedious but was drawn to the new experimental science. He studied medicine, worked alongside the chemist Robert Boyle, and later collaborated with the great physician Thomas Sydenham.

In 1666 he met Anthony Ashley Cooper, later the first **Earl of Shaftesbury**, and became his physician, secretary and political adviser. In 1668 Locke supervised a risky operation on Shaftesbury's liver abscess that probably saved his life. Shaftesbury led the opposition to the Catholic heir to the throne, the future James II, and Locke was drawn into Whig politics. After the failed Rye House Plot to assassinate the king in 1683, Locke, under suspicion, fled to the Dutch Republic, where he lived for five years, at times under an assumed name.

He returned to England in 1689, after the **Glorious Revolution** had placed William and Mary on the throne, and in a burst of publication released the works he had been writing for years: the *Letter Concerning Toleration*, the *Two Treatises of Government* (anonymously) and *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*. He served on the Board of Trade, advised on currency reform, and spent his last years at Oates in Essex, in the household of his friend Lady Damaris Masham. He died in 1704.

## Key ideas

**The blank slate.** Against the doctrine of innate ideas, Locke argued that the mind at birth is like "white paper, void of all characters". All our ideas come from experience, through **sensation** of the outside world and **reflection** on the workings of our own minds. Complex ideas are built from simple ones.

**Primary and secondary qualities.** Some qualities, such as size, shape and motion, really exist in objects. Others, such as colour, taste and warmth, are powers in objects to produce sensations in us.

**Personal identity.** What makes you the same person over time is not the same body or soul-substance but continuity of **consciousness**, especially memory. The idea still frames debates about identity today.

**Natural rights and the state of nature.** Before government, people live in a state of nature governed by a law of nature that reason can discover: no one ought to harm another "in his life, health, liberty, or possessions."

**Property.** We acquire property by "mixing our labour" with what is unowned in nature.

**Government by consent.** People form political society by consent, in order to protect their rights more securely. Government is a trust. If it violates the people's rights, it forfeits its authority, and the people may resist and replace it.

**Toleration.** The state's business is civil interests, not the salvation of souls, which cannot be compelled. Locke nonetheless excluded atheists and, for political reasons, Catholics from full toleration.

## Influence and legacy

Locke's *Essay* founded British empiricism and shaped Berkeley, Hume and the whole of the Enlightenment. Voltaire popularised him in France. His political philosophy became a cornerstone of liberalism: its echoes in the American Declaration of Independence, "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness", and in the right to alter or abolish a destructive government are unmistakable.

His legacy has blind spots. He helped draft the *Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina*, which entrenched slavery, and he invested in the slave-trading Royal African Company, contradictions that historians and philosophers continue to examine.
