## Start here

Look at a newborn baby. Tiny fingers, closed eyes, a cry that can wake the whole building.

What does that baby *know*?

Does she know that two plus two is four? That fire burns? That stealing is wrong? That God exists? That she is a girl, or Indian, or a Hindu, a Muslim, a Sikh, a Christian? Does she already have any of the ideas you have in your head right now?

Most of us would say no. She will learn all of that. From her parents, from touching things, from tasting things, from falling down, from school, from listening. Her mind at birth is like a blank notebook, waiting to be written in.

Now here is a second question, which seems unrelated, but isn't.

You never signed a contract with the Government of India. Neither did your parents. No one asked you, at eighteen, whether you agreed to be governed. Yet you are expected to obey its laws, pay its taxes and respect its authority. **Why?** What gives any government the right to rule you? And if a government starts abusing its power, do you have the right to resist it?

About three hundred and fifty years ago, an English doctor and philosopher gave answers to both questions that changed the world. To the first: yes, the mind at birth is like "**white paper, void of all characters**". Everything we know comes from **experience**. To the second: governments exist only by the **consent** of the people, and only to protect our natural rights to **life, liberty and property**. If a government betrays that trust, the people may remove it.

His name was **John Locke**. His first answer helped found **empiricism**, the philosophy that knowledge comes from experience, which became the working philosophy of modern science. His second answer helped found **liberalism**, the political tradition of individual rights, limited government and the rule of law. You can hear his words echoing in the American Declaration of Independence, in the French Declaration of the Rights of Man, and, through those traditions, in the Fundamental Rights chapter of India's Constitution.

He also had blind spots, serious ones. He helped write a colonial constitution that allowed slavery. He invested in the slave trade. His theory of property was used to justify taking land from indigenous peoples. A great teacher must show you all of this.

By the end of this page, you will understand how Locke thought the mind is built, what makes you the same person over time, where property comes from, why a government can lose its right to rule, and why he believed the state should leave people's souls alone. And you will see your own rights, and your own mind, a little differently.

## The world he was born into

### A country at war with itself

Locke was born in 1632, in a small village in Somerset, in the west of England. His whole life was shaped by one of the most turbulent centuries in English history.

When Locke was ten, in 1642, England fell into **civil war**. On one side was **King Charles I**, who believed kings ruled by **divine right**: God had given them authority, and subjects had no right to resist. On the other side was **Parliament**, which claimed the king could not tax people or rule without its consent. Religion was tangled in everything: Puritans and other Protestants feared that the king was too close to Catholicism.

Parliament won. In **1649**, in an event that shocked all of Europe, **King Charles I was publicly beheaded** in London. Locke was a seventeen-year-old pupil at Westminster School at the time, a short walk from Whitehall, where the execution took place.

For the next eleven years, England had no king. It was ruled by Parliament and then by **Oliver Cromwell**, a Puritan general who became "Lord Protector". Then, in **1660**, the monarchy was restored, and Charles II became king.

But the conflicts didn't end. Charles II's brother and heir, **James**, was a Catholic. Many feared that a Catholic king would rule absolutely and persecute Protestants, as Louis XIV was doing in France. From 1679 to 1681, in what is called the **Exclusion Crisis**, a group in Parliament, who came to be called the **Whigs**, tried to pass a law excluding James from the throne. They failed. James became king in 1685.

Then, in **1688**, came the **Glorious Revolution**. A group of English leaders invited the Dutch prince **William of Orange**, who was married to James's Protestant daughter **Mary**, to invade. James fled to France almost without a fight. Parliament declared that he had abandoned the throne and offered it to William and Mary, on conditions written into the **Bill of Rights** of 1689: the monarch could not suspend laws, raise taxes or keep an army in peacetime without Parliament's consent.

### Two great questions

So imagine growing up in this world. In your lifetime, a king has been executed, a republic tried and failed, a king restored, and another king driven out. Everyone was asking: **Where does political authority come from? When, if ever, can subjects resist their ruler?**

There were two famous answers already on the table:

- **Divine right:** Sir Robert Filmer, in a book called *Patriarcha*, argued that kings inherit their authority from Adam, the first father, to whom God gave dominion over the earth. Just as children must obey fathers, subjects must obey kings. Resistance is never allowed.
- **Absolute sovereignty by agreement:** **Thomas Hobbes**, in *Leviathan* (1651), argued that without government, life would be a "war of every man against every man", in which life would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". To escape this, people agree to give almost all their power to a sovereign, who must then be obeyed absolutely, except to save one's own life.

Locke would reject both. And his answer would win.

### The new science

At the same time, England was at the heart of the **Scientific Revolution**. The **Royal Society** was founded in 1660 to promote experimental science. **Robert Boyle** was discovering the laws of gases. **Isaac Newton** published his *Principia* in 1687, explaining the motion of the planets with mathematical laws. These scientists built knowledge from **observation and experiment**, not from ancient authorities.

Locke knew these men. He worked with Boyle. He admired Newton enormously, calling him "the incomparable Mr. Newton". And he asked: if science learns about nature through experience, what does that tell us about how the human mind learns anything at all?

## The life, as a story

### A Puritan childhood

John Locke was born on **29 August 1632** in **Wrington**, Somerset, and grew up near Bristol. His father, also John, was a country lawyer and a Puritan, who fought as a captain of cavalry on Parliament's side in the Civil War. Through his father's commander, a powerful local MP, young John won a place at **Westminster School** in London, one of the best schools in England.

### Oxford, and medicine

In 1652, Locke went to **Christ Church, Oxford**. He found the curriculum boring: it was still dominated by Aristotle and scholastic logic, which he thought was mostly empty word games. He later said he had lost a lot of time at Oxford.

But he found the new science exciting. He studied **medicine**, worked in laboratories with **Robert Boyle**, and later collaborated with **Thomas Sydenham**, a famous physician who stressed careful observation of patients and diseases rather than theories. Locke became a capable doctor. This medical training matters for understanding his philosophy: he approached the mind the way a good doctor approaches a body, by observing how it actually works.

### The Earl and the operation

In 1666, Locke met **Anthony Ashley Cooper**, a wealthy and ambitious politician who later became the first **Earl of Shaftesbury**. They became close friends, and Locke moved into his household in London as his physician, secretary and adviser.

In 1668, Shaftesbury fell seriously ill with an abscess on his liver. Locke supervised a risky operation to drain it, and had a small silver tube inserted to keep it draining. It saved Shaftesbury's life. Shaftesbury wore the tube for the rest of his life, and later his enemies mocked him as "Tapski".

Shaftesbury became one of the most powerful men in England, for a time **Lord Chancellor**, and then the leader of the Whig opposition to King Charles II and his Catholic brother James. Locke was drawn deep into this world of power, politics and plots.

### Colonies and slavery

Through Shaftesbury, Locke also became involved in England's **colonies**. Shaftesbury was one of the "Lords Proprietors" of the new colony of **Carolina** in North America. Locke helped draft the **Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina** (1669), which included religious toleration for settlers, but also gave every freeman "absolute power and authority over his negro slaves". Locke also invested in the **Royal African Company**, which traded enslaved Africans, and later served on the Board of Trade, which oversaw colonial affairs.

This is part of Locke's story, and an uncomfortable one. We will return to it.

### Exile in Holland

In 1681, Shaftesbury was arrested for treason, acquitted, then fled to Holland, where he died in 1683. That same year, a plot to assassinate Charles II and James, the **Rye House Plot**, was discovered. Locke had been close to many of the men involved. Whether he knew of the plot is unclear. But he was under suspicion, and he fled to the **Dutch Republic**.

He lived there for about five and a half years, sometimes under a false name, "Dr. van der Linden", as English agents tried to have him extradited. Oxford University, under royal pressure, expelled him. In Holland, he worked on his great books, made friends with Dutch thinkers and English exiles, and wrote, in Latin, *A Letter Concerning Toleration*.

### The triumphant return

In February 1689, after the Glorious Revolution, Locke sailed back to England on the same ship as **Princess Mary**, who was coming to be crowned queen alongside her husband William.

Within a year, three books that he had been writing for years were published:

- ***A Letter Concerning Toleration*** (1689), arguing that the state should not force religious belief.
- ***Two Treatises of Government*** (1689, dated 1690), arguing that political power rests on consent and exists to protect natural rights. He published it anonymously and never publicly admitted he wrote it, though he acknowledged it in his will.
- ***An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*** (1689, dated 1690), his great work on how the mind knows anything. This one he published under his own name, and it made him famous across Europe.

### Public servant and quiet sage

Locke now held public positions. He served on the **Board of Trade**, advising on colonial and economic affairs, and helped plan the reform of England's currency. He also published *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* (1693), which became one of the most popular books on raising children for a century, and *The Reasonableness of Christianity* (1695).

His health, always weak with asthma, made London's smoky air hard to bear. He spent his last years at **Oates**, a manor house in Essex, in the household of his close friend **Lady Damaris Masham**, a philosopher in her own right, with whom he had long exchanged ideas.

He died there on **28 October 1704**, aged seventy-two, while Lady Masham was reading the Psalms to him.

## The big ideas, one by one

Locke had two great projects: understanding **knowledge** (in the *Essay*) and understanding **government** (in the *Two Treatises*). We'll start with the mind.

He explained the purpose of the *Essay* with a modest image. In an age of great scientists like Boyle and Newton, he said, it was "ambition enough to be employed as an under-labourer in clearing the ground a little, and removing some of the rubbish that lies in the way to knowledge". He wanted to figure out what the human mind can and cannot know, so that people would stop wasting time arguing about things beyond their reach, and stop being so certain about things they couldn't really know.

### Idea 1: No one is born with ideas

**The idea in one line:** There are no **innate ideas**; we are not born knowing anything.

Many philosophers, including Plato and Descartes, believed that some ideas are innate: present in the mind from birth. For example, the idea of God, basic moral rules, or logical principles like "whatever is, is" or "it is impossible for the same thing to be and not to be".

Locke attacked this. His main argument was simple and practical. If these ideas were really innate, everyone would know them. But do they? **Children don't know them. Many adults have never thought about them.** Whole societies have different ideas about God, morality and right and wrong. If a principle is truly stamped on the mind from birth, how can a child or an uneducated person not know it?

Defenders replied: maybe people have these ideas but don't know they have them until they use their reason. Locke said: that's like saying something is "written" on the mind but no one can read it. What does it even mean to have an idea you've never thought?

**An example.** Think about morality across cultures. Some societies have practised things we now consider deeply wrong, and been sure they were right. If moral rules were innate in every human mind, how could whole societies get them so wrong? Locke would say: because morality, like other knowledge, is learned, and can be learned well or badly.

**Why it matters.** If there are no innate ideas, then no one can claim that their beliefs are written into human nature by God and therefore beyond question. Every idea must be traced back to experience and tested. That is a powerful weapon against dogmatism, and against anyone who says "everyone just knows this is true".

### Idea 2: The mind as white paper

**The idea in one line:** All our ideas come from experience, either through the senses or through reflecting on our own mind.

So where do ideas come from? Locke's famous answer:

"Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas: How comes it to be furnished? ... To this I answer, in one word, from EXPERIENCE."

(This is often called the **tabula rasa**, Latin for "blank slate", though Locke himself used "white paper".)

Experience comes in two kinds:

- **Sensation:** our senses tell us about the external world: colours, sounds, tastes, smells, heat, cold, hardness, shapes.
- **Reflection:** we notice the operations of our own mind: thinking, doubting, believing, remembering, willing.

These give us **simple ideas**: "red", "sweet", "cold", "thinking". The mind then combines, compares and abstracts from these to build **complex ideas**: "mango", "justice", "university", "God", "infinity".

**An example.** How does a child learn the idea of "mango"? She sees its colour (yellow, orange, green), feels its shape and softness, smells it, tastes its sweetness, perhaps feels the stickiness of its juice on her hands. These simple ideas join together into the complex idea "mango". She can then imagine "mango ice cream" or a "golden mango tree", things she may never have seen, by combining simple ideas in new ways. But she could never imagine the taste of mango if she had never tasted one.

Locke gives a famous example: a blind man who said he now understood what scarlet was: it was like the sound of a trumpet. The man was doing his best, but without the experience of seeing colour, he could not truly have the idea.

**Why it matters.** This is the foundation of **empiricism**, the view that knowledge comes from experience. It gave philosophical backing to the scientific method: if all knowledge comes from experience, then observation and experiment are the way to learn about the world. It also has huge consequences for **education**: if the mind starts blank, then upbringing and experience shape who people become. That gives enormous importance to how we raise and teach children, and suggests that differences between people come largely from their experiences, not their birth.

**A common misunderstanding.** Locke didn't think the mind is completely passive, like paper being written on. The mind has **powers**: to perceive, to remember, to compare, to combine, to abstract, to reason. What it lacks at birth are *ideas*, not abilities.

### Idea 3: What is really out there? Primary and secondary qualities

**The idea in one line:** Some qualities, such as size, shape and motion, really exist in objects; others, such as colour, taste and warmth, are produced in our minds by objects.

Locke distinguished two kinds of qualities:

- **Primary qualities:** solidity, extension (size), shape, motion or rest, and number. These are really in the object, whether anyone perceives them or not. Our ideas of them **resemble** the object.
- **Secondary qualities:** colour, sound, taste, smell, heat and cold. These are not in the object in the way we experience them. The object has a **power**, through its primary qualities (the arrangement and motion of its tiny particles), to produce these sensations in us. But our ideas of them **don't resemble** anything in the object.

**A wonderful experiment.** Locke suggests this. Take three bowls of water: one hot, one cold, one lukewarm. Put one hand in the hot water and the other in the cold for a while. Then put both hands into the lukewarm water. To the hand that was in hot water, the lukewarm water feels **cold**. To the hand that was in cold water, the same water feels **warm**. The same water at the same time feels both warm and cold. So "warmth" can't simply be a property of the water itself; it depends on the relationship between the water and your body.

Try it at home. It works.

**Why it matters.** This distinction matched the new science of Boyle and Newton, which explained the world in terms of particles in motion, measurable quantities, not colours and tastes. It also shows something unsettling: the vivid, colourful, flavourful world we experience is, in part, a creation of our own minds. A mango is not "sweet" in itself; sweetness is what happens when its particles meet your tongue. Later philosophers, especially Berkeley and Hume, would push this point much further.

### Idea 4: What makes you the same person?

**The idea in one line:** You are the same person over time because of the continuity of your **consciousness**, especially memory, not because of the same body or the same soul.

This is one of Locke's most original and influential ideas. Think about it: you were once a baby. Almost every cell in your body has changed since then. Your thoughts, tastes and personality have changed. What makes you the *same person* as that baby?

Locke's answer: being the same **person** is a matter of **consciousness**. As far as your consciousness can extend backwards, through memory, to past thoughts and actions, so far does your personal identity reach. You are the same person who did those things because you can remember doing them, and be conscious of them as yours.

**The prince and the cobbler.** Locke gives a famous thought experiment. Suppose the soul of a **prince**, carrying all his memories, entered the body of a **cobbler** (a shoe-repairer), as soon as the cobbler's own soul left it. Who would this person be? Everyone around would see a cobbler. But the person inside would remember being the prince, would think the prince's thoughts, would feel responsible for the prince's past actions. Locke says: he would be **the same person as the prince**, in the same man's body as the cobbler.

**An Indian angle.** Indian readers will immediately think of **rebirth**. If someone is reborn but remembers nothing of their past life, are they the same person? Many Indian traditions say yes, because the same soul (or, in Buddhism, the same continuing stream of karma) passes on. Locke would say: perhaps the same *soul*, but not the same *person*, unless there is some continuity of consciousness. Folk stories in India and elsewhere of children who "remember" past lives are, interestingly, exactly the kind of case that would satisfy Locke's test, if they were true.

**Why it matters.** Locke connected personal identity with **responsibility**. You can only be justly punished or rewarded for actions that are yours, and they are yours if you are conscious of them as your own. This is why the law treats cases of severe memory loss or mental illness specially. Locke's theory also raises questions that are urgent today: if someone develops dementia and loses their memories, are they still the same person? If, one day, someone's memories could be copied into a computer, would that be them? Philosophers still argue about these questions in Locke's terms.

### Idea 5: Knowledge, probability and the danger of "enthusiasm"

**The idea in one line:** True certain knowledge is rare; most of life runs on probability, and we should believe things only as strongly as the evidence supports.

Locke concluded that **certain knowledge** is limited: we have it about our own existence, about God's existence (he thought this could be proved), and about mathematics and some relations between ideas. About the external world, we have a kind of practical knowledge through the senses. But for most things in life, history, science, medicine, everyday decisions, we must rely on **probability**: judgements based on evidence and testimony, more or less likely to be true.

And he warned that the wise person should **proportion the degree of assent to the evidence**: believe firmly where the evidence is strong, tentatively where it is weak.

He was especially worried about what he called **"enthusiasm"**: people who claim that God has directly revealed truths to them, and therefore refuse to listen to reason or evidence. In Locke's century, religious enthusiasm had fuelled wars and persecutions. He argued that we must test every claim to revelation by reason: if someone says God told them something, we must ask how they know it was God, and not their own imagination.

**An example.** Think of a WhatsApp forward claiming that a particular herb cures cancer. A Lockean asks: what is the evidence? Who says so? How reliable are they? How strong is the evidence compared with the evidence against? How much should I believe this? Most forwards fail this test quickly.

### Idea 6: Words often hide confusion

**The idea in one line:** Many disputes happen because people use the same words for different ideas, or use words that stand for no clear idea at all.

Book III of the *Essay* is about **language**. Locke argued that words stand for ideas in the mind of the speaker. When people use words without clear ideas behind them, or use the same word for different ideas, they argue endlessly without realising they are not even talking about the same thing.

He was especially critical of scholastic philosophers, who, he said, invented technical terms that sounded profound but meant nothing clear.

**An example.** Two people argue fiercely about whether a country is "secular". One means that the state doesn't favour any religion. The other means that religion should have no place in public life. They could argue for hours without progress, because they mean different things by the same word. Locke's advice: before you argue, define your terms.

### Idea 7: Born free and equal

**The idea in one line:** Before any government exists, all people are naturally free and equal, governed by a law of nature that reason can discover.

Now we turn to the *Two Treatises of Government*. The *First Treatise* is a detailed attack on Filmer's divine right of kings. Locke argued that there is no evidence that God gave Adam, or any king, authority over everyone else. The *Second Treatise* builds Locke's own theory.

He starts by imagining the **state of nature**: how people would live with no government at all. Unlike Hobbes, who saw this as a brutal war of all against all, Locke saw it as a state of **perfect freedom** and **equality**, but not of licence. It is governed by the **law of nature**, which reason teaches to everyone who consults it:

"Being all equal and independent, no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions."

Why? Because we are all made by the same God, as his "workmanship", and no one has a natural right to rule another. No one is born a king and no one is born a slave.

**But there are problems.** In the state of nature, everyone has the right to punish those who break the law of nature. But people are biased in their own cases. They exaggerate wrongs done to them and take revenge too far. There is no settled, known law, no impartial judge, and no power to enforce fair judgements. Life is insecure.

**An example.** Imagine a remote village with no police, no courts and no government. Most people may be decent. But when a dispute arises over a field boundary, each family judges its own case, one side takes revenge, the other retaliates, and feuds last for generations. Locke would say: this is why people need government, not because they are evil, but because they need impartial judges and settled rules.

### Idea 8: Where property comes from

**The idea in one line:** You own your own body and your own labour, and when you mix your labour with something unowned in nature, it becomes your property.

This is one of Locke's most famous and controversial arguments.

God gave the earth to humankind **in common**. So how can anyone justly own any part of it privately?

Locke's answer: every person has a **property in his own person**: you own your own body. So you also own the **labour** of your body and the work of your hands. When you take something from nature and **mix your labour** with it, you make it your own. The apple you pick from a wild tree, the water you draw from a river, the field you clear and plough: these become yours because you added your labour to them.

**An example.** Picture a forest where no one owns anything. You spend a week clearing a patch of land, removing stones, digging, planting seeds and watering them. When the crop grows, is it yours? Most people's intuition says yes: you worked for it. That is Locke's intuition.

**Limits.** Locke added two important limits. First, you may take only as much as you can use **before it spoils**: if you pick more apples than you can eat and let them rot, you have taken from others what you had no right to. Second, there must be **"enough, and as good" left in common for others**.

**Then came money.** But Locke argued that the invention of **money**, gold and silver, which don't spoil, allowed people to accumulate far more than they could use without breaking the spoilage limit. Since people have tacitly agreed to use money, he said, they have agreed to the unequal possessions that result.

**Why it matters.** Locke's theory of property became the foundation of a great deal of modern economic thinking: the idea that property rights come from work, and that people have a natural right to keep what they earn. It influenced Adam Smith, and through him, classical economics.

**The Indian Constitution.** Interestingly, India's own constitutional history shows how contested property rights are. When the Constitution came into force in 1950, the **right to property** was a **Fundamental Right**. But as the government tried to carry out land reforms, to break up huge estates and give land to tillers, it ran into conflict with this right. After a long series of court battles and amendments, the **44th Amendment in 1978** removed the right to property from the list of Fundamental Rights and made it an ordinary constitutional right (Article 300A). India decided that the property rights of a few should not block justice for the many. Locke and his critics would both have found that debate familiar.

**The darker side.** Locke wrote: "In the beginning all the world was America." He meant that America represented the original state of nature, before money and property. And he argued that land which was not cultivated, enclosed and "improved" by labour was, in effect, waste, available to be appropriated. Ideas like this were used by European colonists to justify taking land from **indigenous peoples** in the Americas, whose ways of using the land (hunting, gathering, shifting cultivation) did not count as "improvement" in Locke's sense. Similar ideas about "waste land" shaped colonial land policies elsewhere, including in British India, with lasting consequences for forest-dwelling and tribal communities. India's **Forest Rights Act of 2006** was, in part, an attempt to recognise rights that colonial and later laws had ignored.

### Idea 9: Government by consent, as a trust

**The idea in one line:** Political power is legitimate only when it rests on the consent of the governed and is used to protect their rights; it is a trust, not a possession.

To escape the insecurity of the state of nature, people agree to form a **political society**. They give up their private right to judge and punish, and hand it to the community, which sets up a government with settled laws, impartial judges and the power to enforce them.

Crucially, this is not a surrender of all rights, as in Hobbes. The whole point of government is to **protect** people's lives, liberties and estates, which Locke together called their "property" in a broad sense. The government is a **trustee**: it holds power on behalf of the people, for their good. Its power is limited by its purpose.

**Consent.** But you never signed a contract, as we asked at the start. Locke's answer is **tacit consent**: by living in a country, enjoying its protection, using its roads, owning property under its laws, you tacitly consent to be governed by it. Critics have found this weak (what if you have no realistic option to leave?), but it remains the starting point of most modern discussions of political obligation.

**Majority rule and the legislature.** Once a political society is formed, decisions are made by the **majority**. The supreme power is the **legislative**: the law-making body. But even the legislature is limited: it must rule by known, settled laws, not arbitrary decrees; it cannot take people's property without their consent (through their representatives); and it cannot transfer its power to anyone else. Locke also distinguished the **executive** power (enforcing laws) and what he called the **federative** power (foreign affairs and war), an early step towards the idea of **separation of powers** that Montesquieu would develop.

**Why it matters.** This is the core of **liberal democracy**: government derives its authority from the people, exists to protect their rights, and is bound by law. When the Indian Constitution begins with "**We, the people of India**... do hereby adopt, enact and give to ourselves this Constitution", it expresses the Lockean idea that political authority comes from the people.

### Idea 10: The right to revolution

**The idea in one line:** When a government violates the trust given to it, the people have the right to resist and replace it.

This is Locke's most radical idea, and it was written to justify resistance to the Stuart kings.

If a government acts against the purpose for which it was created, if the legislature tries to take away people's property arbitrarily, or the executive tries to rule without law, or the ruler tries to enslave the people, then the government has **dissolved itself** by breaking its trust. Power returns to the people, who may set up a new government.

Locke was careful. He said people do not revolt over small mistakes. They endure a great deal before they rebel. But if there is "a long train of abuses, prevarications and artifices, all tending the same way", making the design of tyranny visible to the people, then they will rise, and they are right to.

And who decides whether the government has broken its trust? Locke's answer: **the people**. Ultimately, he said, when there is no judge on earth, the only remaining appeal is to **heaven**: the people must act on their own judgement and conscience.

**Why it matters.** In 1776, the **American Declaration of Independence**, written by Thomas Jefferson, declared that all men are created equal and endowed with unalienable rights, including "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness"; that governments derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed"; and that "when a long train of abuses and usurpations... evinces a design to reduce them under absolute Despotism, it is their right, it is their duty, to throw off such Government". That is Locke, almost word for word.

India's freedom struggle drew on many sources, Indian and Western, and Gandhi's method of non-violent resistance was very different from Locke's idea of revolution. But the basic conviction that a government which systematically violates the rights and dignity of its people loses its moral authority to rule was central to the case against British rule.

### Idea 11: Leave people's souls alone

**The idea in one line:** The state's job is to protect people's civil interests, not to save their souls; it cannot force belief, and it should tolerate different religions.

In *A Letter Concerning Toleration*, Locke argued:

- The **state** exists to protect **civil interests**: life, liberty, health, property. The care of souls is not its business.
- **True religion** consists in the inward persuasion of the mind. You cannot force someone to truly believe something by threats or punishment; you can only make them pretend. And pretended faith is worthless to God.
- A **church** is a voluntary society, which people join freely to worship God as they think right. It can expel members, but it has no right to use force.
- So the magistrate should **tolerate** different religious groups, as long as they don't threaten civil peace.

**The limits of Locke's tolerance.** Locke did *not* extend toleration to everyone. He excluded **atheists**, because he thought that people who did not believe in God could not be trusted to keep promises and oaths, the bonds of society. And he excluded **Catholics**, at least in practice, because he believed they owed allegiance to a foreign prince, the Pope, and might not keep faith with Protestants. These exclusions seem shocking now, and they show the limits of his vision.

**The Indian parallels.** India has its own long tradition of religious tolerance in statecraft, much older than Locke. The emperor **Ashoka**, in his Rock Edict XII in the third century BCE, urged respect for all religious sects, saying that one who honours his own sect while disparaging others actually injures his own. In the sixteenth century, the Mughal emperor **Akbar** held debates among Hindus, Muslims, Jains, Zoroastrians and Christians in his **Ibadat Khana** (House of Worship) and promoted a policy known as ***sulh-i-kul***, "peace with all". These traditions were not identical to Locke's: they were mostly about rulers respecting all faiths, rather than individuals having rights against the state. But they share the insight that forcing belief is both wrong and dangerous.

**Why it matters.** Locke's argument became a foundation of **religious freedom** in the modern world. India's Constitution guarantees **freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion** (Article 25), and the debates about secularism in India continue to wrestle with how far the state should involve itself in religious matters.

### Idea 12: How to raise a child

**The idea in one line:** Children should be educated through good habits, curiosity, play and kindness, not fear and beating.

In *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* (1693), originally letters to a friend about raising his son, Locke gave practical advice that was surprisingly modern. Since the mind starts as white paper, upbringing is everything.

He urged parents to:

- Build **good habits** early, because habits shape character.
- Use **esteem and disgrace** (praise and gentle shame) rather than beating. Severe physical punishment, he argued, makes children slavish or rebellious.
- Make learning a **pleasure**, not a chore, by using games and children's natural **curiosity**.
- Care for the body: plenty of fresh air, exercise, sleep, plain food. He began the book by quoting the old Latin ideal of "a sound mind in a sound body".
- Teach **reasoning** with children, appropriate to their age, rather than demanding blind obedience.

**Why it matters.** Many of these ideas have become standard in modern education, though not always in practice. Every school that bans corporal punishment, every teacher who uses play-based learning, every parent who reasons with a child instead of hitting them is, knowingly or not, following Locke.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Descartes: the teacher he turned against

Locke's friend Lady Masham reported that the first books that gave Locke a real taste for philosophy were those of **Descartes**. Descartes showed him that philosophy could be written clearly, without scholastic jargon. But Locke rejected Descartes' innate ideas, and his confidence that pure reason could reveal the deep nature of reality. Where Descartes looked inward for certainty, Locke looked at experience.

### Hobbes: the shadow

Locke rarely mentions **Thomas Hobbes** by name, but Hobbes is everywhere in the *Second Treatise*. Both used the idea of a state of nature and a social contract. But their conclusions were opposite. For Hobbes, the state of nature is war, and people must surrender almost all their rights to an absolute sovereign. For Locke, the state of nature is a state of freedom governed by natural law, and people keep their natural rights; government is limited and can be resisted. One built a theory for absolute power; the other for limited, accountable power.

### Leibniz: the reply he never read

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** wrote a long, detailed response to Locke's *Essay*, the *New Essays on Human Understanding*. He argued that the mind is not a blank slate but more like a **block of marble with veins** that mark out the shape of a statue: experience is needed to bring the ideas out, but some are already latent within. He also defended innate truths of logic and mathematics. Locke died in 1704 before Leibniz could publish, and Leibniz decided not to publish a criticism of a man who could no longer reply. The *New Essays* appeared only in 1765.

### Berkeley and Hume: following the road further

Locke's empiricism led his successors to surprising places. **George Berkeley**, an Irish bishop, argued that if all we know are our ideas, and Locke admitted that secondary qualities are only in the mind, then why not primary qualities too? Perhaps there is no matter at all, only minds and their ideas. "To be is to be perceived."

**David Hume** pushed empiricism even further, arguing that experience cannot justify our belief in causation, in a continuing self, or even in the external world, in the way we usually assume. Locke built the road; Hume showed how far it went.

### Montesquieu, Jefferson and the revolutions

**Montesquieu** developed Locke's idea of distinguishing government powers into the full doctrine of the separation of powers. **Thomas Jefferson** and the American founders drew directly on Locke for the Declaration of Independence. French revolutionaries drew on him for the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789). Locke became the patron philosopher of the age of revolutions.

### Mill: liberty grows

**John Stuart Mill**, in the nineteenth century, carried Locke's liberal tradition forward. Mill's *On Liberty* goes beyond Locke in defending free speech and individuality, and it protects atheists and dissenters whom Locke excluded. But the basic idea, that there is a sphere of individual life the state should not invade, is Lockean.

### Marx and the critics of property

**Karl Marx** saw Locke as the philosopher of the rising capitalist class. Locke's natural right to property, his justification of unlimited accumulation through money, and his defence of a government whose chief end is to protect property, all seemed to Marx to serve the interests of property-owners against the propertyless. If property comes from labour, Marx asked, why do the people who do the labour, factory workers, landless labourers, own so little, while those who own the factories and land own so much?

Socialist, feminist and post-colonial thinkers have also criticised Locke for building a theory of universal rights that, in practice, served propertied European men.

## See it around you

**In schools and parenting.** The belief that children are shaped mainly by their upbringing and education, not by fixed nature, is Lockean. So is the idea of learning through play and curiosity rather than fear.

**In science.** Every experiment, every clinical trial, every study that says "show me the evidence" is working in the empiricist tradition Locke championed.

**In your constitution.** "We, the people", Fundamental Rights, the rule of law, limits on government power, freedom of religion: all have Lockean roots, alongside many Indian and other sources.

**In property debates.** Land acquisition for highways and factories, tribal land rights, farmers' protests, the "right to property" debate: all turn on questions Locke asked: where does property come from, and when can the state take it?

**In protests.** Every peaceful protest against a government that is seen to abuse power expresses, in some form, the Lockean idea that power is a trust that can be withdrawn.

**In identity and memory.** When a family member develops dementia and "isn't the same person anymore", we are facing Locke's question about personal identity. So are debates about whether uploading memories to a computer could preserve a person.

**In misinformation.** Locke's principle, proportion your belief to the evidence, is the best defence against fake news and viral rumours.

**In secularism.** India's debates about the relationship between religion and the state, about conversion, about religious freedom, all touch Locke's question: what is the state's business, and what belongs to conscience?

## Use it

**1. Trace your ideas back.** Pick a strong belief you hold. Ask: where did I get this idea? From my own experience, from my family, from school, from social media? Is that a reliable source?

**2. Do the three-bowl experiment.** Hot water, cold water, lukewarm water. Feel how the same water seems both warm and cold. Remember it the next time you assume your perception shows the world exactly as it is.

**3. Proportion your belief.** Before accepting a claim, ask: how strong is the evidence? Then believe it only that strongly. Practise saying "probably", "maybe" and "I'm not sure" when that's the truth.

**4. Define your words.** Before an argument about "freedom", "development", "secularism" or "merit", ask everyone what they mean. Many arguments end there.

**5. Know your rights, and their source.** Read the Fundamental Rights chapter of the Indian Constitution. Ask, for each right, why it exists and what it protects.

**6. Hold power as a trust.** If you have power over others, as a manager, an officer, a parent or a teacher, remember Locke: power is held for the good of those under it, not for your own benefit.

**7. Leave others' consciences alone.** You can argue, persuade and share your beliefs. But Locke reminds us that forcing belief never produces real belief, only fear and pretence.

**8. Raise children with reason.** If you are a parent, an elder sibling or a teacher, try explaining reasons instead of demanding obedience, and praise effort instead of punishing failure.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Slavery and colonialism.** Locke wrote that no one is born a slave, yet he helped draft a constitution that gave masters absolute power over enslaved people, invested in the slave trade, and served on colonial boards. His theory of property, which treated uncultivated land as waste, was used to justify taking land from indigenous peoples. This is not a side issue; it shows how a theory of universal rights could coexist with brutal exclusions.

**2. Limited tolerance.** His *Letter Concerning Toleration* denied toleration to atheists and, in practice, to Catholics. A theory of freedom that leaves out those you distrust is not yet full freedom.

**3. Tacit consent is weak.** Most people cannot realistically leave their country. Is staying really "consent"? Critics, from Hume onward, have found Locke's account of consent unconvincing.

**4. Property and inequality.** Locke's argument that money lets people accumulate far beyond their needs, with everyone's tacit agreement, can justify enormous inequality. And his "enough and as good left for others" condition is hard to satisfy in a world where land and resources are scarce.

**5. The blank slate is too blank.** Modern psychology and biology show that humans are not born completely blank. We come with built-in capacities: for language, for recognising faces, for certain fears and preferences. Leibniz's "veined marble" may be closer to the truth.

**6. Personal identity puzzles.** Locke's memory theory has problems. Memories can be false. People forget most of their lives. A brave officer may remember being a boy who was flogged, and an old general may remember being the officer but not the boy. Is the general the same person as the boy? (This objection was famously raised by the Scottish philosopher Thomas Reid.)

**7. Women.** Locke argued against Filmer that mothers share parental authority with fathers, a progressive point for his time. But in his political theory, women remained mostly within the household, and he assumed the husband's will should prevail in disagreements.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have assumed that your ideas were simply "yours", or simply "true", without asking where they came from. You may have thought of government as something that just exists and must be obeyed, and of rights as gifts from the state.

**Now,** you can see your mind as something built from experience, which means it can be examined, questioned and rebuilt. You can see government as a trust, created by people to protect their rights, which can be lost if it is betrayed. And you can see that the language of rights, so powerful, has also been used to exclude and dispossess.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A newborn baby** becomes a blank page that the world is about to write on, and you feel the responsibility of being one of the writers.
- **A strong opinion**, yours or someone else's, invites a question: where did this idea actually come from?
- **A bowl of lukewarm water** becomes a lesson in how much of the world is shaped by the observer.
- **The words "We, the people"** become an argument, not just a phrase: power comes from us, and is held in trust for us.
- **A land dispute in the news** sends you back to Locke's question: who owns this, and why?
- **A protest against an abusive government** becomes part of a long tradition of withdrawing consent.
- **A great thinker's words about freedom** make you ask: freedom for whom? Who was left out?

Locke called himself an under-labourer, clearing rubbish from the path to knowledge. He cleared a lot of it. Some of the rubbish he left behind, about slavery, colonies and who counts, later generations had to clear themselves.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** John Locke (1632–1704), English physician and philosopher; adviser to the Earl of Shaftesbury; exile in Holland; returned after the Glorious Revolution of 1688.
- **Context:** civil war, the execution of Charles I, the Restoration, the Exclusion Crisis, the Glorious Revolution; the rise of experimental science.
- **Knowledge:** no innate ideas; the mind starts as "white paper"; all ideas come from sensation and reflection; simple ideas combine into complex ones.
- **Qualities:** primary qualities (shape, size, motion) are in objects; secondary qualities (colour, taste, warmth) are powers to produce sensations in us.
- **Identity:** personal identity depends on continuity of consciousness and memory (the prince and the cobbler).
- **Belief:** proportion belief to evidence; beware "enthusiasm"; define your words.
- **Politics:** natural freedom and equality; natural rights to life, liberty and property; property from mixing labour; government by consent as a trust; right of revolution against "a long train of abuses".
- **Toleration:** the state should not force religious belief, though Locke excluded atheists and Catholics.
- **Legacy:** foundation of empiricism and liberalism; echoed in the American Declaration and, through that tradition, in modern constitutions including India's.
- **Limits:** involvement in slavery and colonialism; limited toleration; weak account of consent; a theory of property used to justify dispossession and inequality.

**Key terms**

- **Empiricism:** the view that knowledge comes from experience.
- **Tabula rasa:** "blank slate", the mind at birth (Locke said "white paper").
- **Sensation and reflection:** the two sources of all ideas.
- **Primary and secondary qualities:** properties in objects vs. powers to produce sensations.
- **Personal identity:** what makes someone the same person over time; for Locke, consciousness.
- **State of nature:** human life imagined without government.
- **Natural rights:** rights people have by nature: life, liberty, property.
- **Social contract / consent:** the agreement that makes government legitimate.
- **Right of revolution:** the right to resist and replace a government that betrays its trust.
- **Toleration:** allowing people to practise religions different from the official or majority one.
