## Life

Almost nothing about Laozi is certain, including whether he existed. His name simply means "Old Master". The earliest biography, written by the historian Sima Qian around 100 BCE, already hedges its bets. It identifies him as Li Er, a native of the southern state of Chu who served as keeper of the archives at the Zhou royal court, and it repeats a famous story that the young Confucius visited him to ask about ritual and came away comparing him to a dragon, beyond understanding.

According to the same account, Laozi, seeing the decline of the Zhou, left for the west riding a water buffalo. At the frontier pass the keeper, Yinxi, begged him to write down his wisdom before he disappeared. Laozi composed a book of some five thousand characters in two parts, on the Dao and on De, and departed. No one, Sima Qian writes, knows where he ended his days. Sima Qian then mentions other candidates for the "real" Laozi, and notes claims that he lived to 160 or even 200 years.

Modern scholars generally regard the *Daodejing* as a compilation, probably assembled in the fourth century BCE from earlier sayings, rather than the work of a single author. Bamboo-slip manuscripts discovered at Guodian in 1993, dating to around 300 BCE, contain portions of the text in a different arrangement. Whether or not Laozi lived, the book attributed to him is one of the most influential ever written.

## Key ideas

The *Daodejing* consists of eighty-one short chapters of terse, poetic and often paradoxical verse. Its very ambiguity has invited countless interpretations, as mystical philosophy, political advice, or a guide to living.

**The Dao.** The Dao, or "Way", is the source and pattern of everything: nameless, formless, inexhaustible. It cannot be captured in words, so the book opens by warning that "the Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao." The ten thousand things arise from it and return to it.

**Wu wei: effortless action.** The sage acts without forcing: *wu wei*, often translated "non-action", means acting in harmony with the natural course of things rather than imposing one's will on them. Of the Dao itself the book says that it "does nothing, and so there is nothing which it does not do."

**Ziran: naturalness.** The ideal is spontaneity and simplicity, like the "uncarved block" (*pu*) before it is shaped by human design. Ambition, cleverness, luxury and rigid moral codes are signs that the Way has been lost.

**The power of softness.** Water is the book's favourite image: soft and yielding, it takes the lowest place, yet it wears away stone. Likewise the flexible survives and the rigid breaks, and humility overcomes pride.

**Minimal government.** The best ruler governs lightly, so that people hardly know he exists: "Governing a great state is like cooking small fish", too much stirring ruins it. Laws and prohibitions multiply disorder, and weapons are "instruments of evil omen".

## Influence and legacy

The *Daodejing*, together with the later *Zhuangzi*, founded philosophical Daoism. Laozi was later deified as a god in religious Daoism, which developed its own temples, scriptures and practices of longevity. Daoist ideas shaped Chinese poetry, landscape painting, medicine and martial arts, and blended with Buddhism to produce Chan, known in Japan as Zen.

The *Daodejing* is among the most translated books in the world. It has fascinated Western readers from Tolstoy to Heidegger, and its images of yielding strength and effortless action have entered popular culture, management books and environmental thought.
