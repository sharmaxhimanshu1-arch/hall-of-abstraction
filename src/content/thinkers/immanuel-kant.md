## Life

Immanuel Kant was born in 1724 in Königsberg, a Baltic port in East Prussia (now Kaliningrad, Russia), the fourth of nine children of a harness-maker. His family were devout Pietists, and he never lost his respect for their upright simplicity, though he came to reject their emotional religiosity. He studied at the University of Königsberg, worked for some years as a private tutor, and in 1755 began lecturing as an unsalaried *Privatdozent*, teaching an enormous range of subjects: logic, metaphysics, mathematics, physics, physical geography and anthropology. That year he also proposed that the solar system formed from a rotating cloud of gas, an early version of the nebular hypothesis.

In 1770, at forty-six, he became professor of logic and metaphysics. There followed a "silent decade" in which he published almost nothing while he rethought philosophy from the ground up. The result, the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781), was so difficult that early readers struggled to understand it. Then, in his late fifties and sixties, came an astonishing outpouring of works in metaphysics, ethics, aesthetics, religion, politics and history.

Kant famously never travelled more than a short distance from Königsberg. He never married, and his days ran to a strict routine; legend says his neighbours set their clocks by his afternoon walk. Yet he was also a sociable host and a witty conversationalist. In 1794 the Prussian king's censors forbade him to write further on religion. His mental powers declined in his final years, and he died in 1804. His last words are reported as "*Es ist gut*", "It is good."

## Key ideas

**The Copernican revolution in philosophy.** Rationalists claimed reason could know reality by itself. Hume showed that experience alone cannot justify necessary truths such as "every event has a cause." Kant proposed a radical solution: instead of assuming our knowledge must conform to objects, suppose that objects, as we experience them, must conform to the structure of our minds.

**Forms of intuition and categories.** Space and time are not features of things in themselves but the forms through which we perceive anything at all. The understanding organises experience through **categories** such as substance and causality. This explains how we can have **synthetic a priori** knowledge, truths about the world that are known independently of experience, in mathematics and the foundations of physics.

**Phenomena and noumena.** We can know things only as they appear to us (**phenomena**), never as they are in themselves (**noumena**). When reason tries to go beyond possible experience, to prove God's existence, the soul's immortality or the world's beginning, it falls into contradictions, the **antinomies**. Kant said he had "to deny knowledge, in order to make room for faith."

**The categorical imperative.** Morality cannot rest on desires or consequences, which vary, but only on reason, which is universal. Its supreme principle is the **categorical imperative**: act only on principles you could will as universal laws, and always treat humanity, in yourself and others, as an end and never merely as a means.

**Autonomy and dignity.** Rational beings give themselves the moral law. This autonomy is the source of human **dignity**, a worth beyond all price.

**Enlightenment and peace.** Enlightenment is humanity's emergence from "self-incurred immaturity": the courage to think for oneself. In *Toward Perpetual Peace* Kant proposed a federation of republics, governed by international law, to end war.

## Influence and legacy

Kant is often considered the most important philosopher since antiquity, the figure through whom all later Western philosophy passes. German Idealism began as an attempt to complete his system. Neo-Kantianism dominated German universities in the late nineteenth century, and both analytic and continental philosophy grew from engagement with him.

His ethics of human dignity underlies modern human-rights thought, and John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* is explicitly Kantian. His vision of a league of nations anticipated the League of Nations and the United Nations. Scholars also confront his writings on race, which ranked human groups in ways at odds with his universalist ethics.
