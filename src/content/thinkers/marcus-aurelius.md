## Life

Marcus Annius Verus was born in Rome in 121 CE into a wealthy family of Spanish origin. He caught the attention of the emperor Hadrian, who arranged for his adoption by the future emperor Antoninus Pius in 138, taking the name Marcus Aurelius Antoninus and becoming heir to the throne. He received the finest education in rhetoric and philosophy available. Among his teachers was the Stoic Junius Rusticus, who lent him the discourses of Epictetus, a book that changed his life.

He became emperor in 161 CE, at first sharing power with his adoptive brother Lucius Verus. His reign, often counted as the last of the "Five Good Emperors", was anything but peaceful. A war with Parthia in the east brought home the devastating **Antonine Plague**, which killed millions across the empire. From 166 onward Germanic tribes pressed across the Danube, and Marcus spent most of his final decade on campaign in the cold frontier camps of central Europe. He also faced a rebellion by his trusted general Avidius Cassius in 175. He died in 180 CE, probably near Vindobona (modern Vienna) or Sirmium, and was succeeded by his son Commodus, whose disastrous reign stands in stark contrast to his own.

## Key ideas

The *Meditations* were never meant for publication. They are a private notebook, written in Greek, of reminders to himself: exhortations, arguments and images to keep his mind in order amid war, plague, betrayal and grief. They are not a systematic treatise but a record of Stoic philosophy as a daily discipline.

**The inner citadel.** Events cannot harm us; only our judgements about them can. "Take away thy opinion," he writes, "and then there is taken away the complaint, 'I have been harmed.'" What lies within our power, our assent, intentions and actions, is enough for a good life.

**Impermanence and perspective.** Marcus returns again and again to the vastness of time and the smallness of a single life. Empires, fame and the emperor himself will soon be forgotten. Far from being despairing, this "view from above" releases him from vanity and fear of death.

**Duty and the common good.** Human beings are made for cooperation "like feet, like hands, like eyelids." The emperor's task, like anyone's, is to do the work of a human being: to act justly and for the good of the community, without expecting thanks.

**Tolerance toward wrongdoers.** People do wrong out of ignorance of good and evil. Rather than raging at them, he resolves to understand, correct if possible, and otherwise endure them with patience.

## Influence and legacy

The *Meditations* survived through the Byzantine world and were first printed in 1559. They have since been treasured by readers as different as Frederick the Great, John Stuart Mill, Goethe and modern political leaders. Marcus is often taken as the closest historical approach to Plato's ideal of the philosopher-king, though his record includes the persecution of Christians and the decision to name the unfit Commodus as heir.

In the twenty-first century the *Meditations* became a bestseller again, at the centre of a popular Stoic revival that treats them as practical guidance for resilience, focus and equanimity.
