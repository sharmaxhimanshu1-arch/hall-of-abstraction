## Start here

Every morning, in schools across India, children stand in rows and sing a song. Most of them have sung it hundreds of times. Few have thought about who wrote it, or what the man who wrote it actually believed about nations.

The song is **"Jana Gana Mana"**, India's national anthem. Across the border, in Bangladesh, schoolchildren sing their anthem, **"Amar Shonar Bangla"** ("My Golden Bengal"). Both songs were written by the same man.

Here is the strange part. The man who wrote the national anthems of two nations was one of the twentieth century's fiercest critics of **nationalism**. He travelled to Japan and America in the middle of the First World War and told packed halls that the worship of the nation was "a great menace", a machine that turns human beings into tools and leads to war. He argued, publicly and lovingly, with Mahatma Gandhi about whether burning foreign cloth and spinning on a charkha was the way to free India. He refused to let any loyalty, to nation, religion, caste or race, become a wall around the human spirit.

His name was **Rabindranath Tagore**. He was a poet, songwriter, novelist, playwright, painter, educator, philosopher and social reformer, all at once. He wrote over two thousand songs, which Bengalis still sing every day. He was the first Asian, and the first non-European writer, to win the **Nobel Prize** (Literature, 1913). He gave back the knighthood the British had given him to protest the massacre at **Jallianwala Bagh**. He founded a school under the trees, and a university whose motto was: *"where the whole world meets in a single nest"*.

So here's the question this page will help you answer: **can you love your country without making it a god? Can you be rooted in your own culture and still belong to the whole world?** Tagore spent his life answering yes, and showing how.

## The world he was born into

### The Tagores of Jorasanko

Tagore was born in **1861** into one of the most remarkable families in India: the **Tagores of Jorasanko**, in north Calcutta. Their sprawling mansion was a centre of the **Bengal Renaissance**, the movement of social and religious reform, literature and art in nineteenth-century Bengal.

His grandfather, **Dwarkanath Tagore**, had been a wealthy businessman and a friend of Raja Rammohun Roy. His father, **Debendranath Tagore**, called "Maharshi" (great sage), was a leader of the **Brahmo Samaj**, the reform movement that rejected idol worship and caste and sought a purified, monotheistic faith based on the Upanishads. Rabindranath's brothers and sisters included philosophers, musicians, the first Indian member of the Indian Civil Service (Satyendranath), and one of the first women novelists in Bengali (Swarnakumari Devi).

The household was full of music, poetry, plays, philosophy and debates. It was also, in its own way, a meeting point of cultures: Indian classical music and English literature, the Upanishads and Shakespeare.

### British India and its humiliations

Tagore grew up under the full weight of British rule after the revolt of 1857. Indians, however educated, were treated as second-class subjects in their own country. Educated Bengalis were caught between admiration for European science, literature and liberal ideas, and anger at the racism and exploitation of empire.

### The rise of nationalism

In Tagore's lifetime, Indian **nationalism** grew from small petitions of the educated elite into a mass movement. In **1905**, the British **partitioned Bengal**, splitting the province along religious lines. Bengal exploded in protest: the **Swadeshi movement**, boycotting British goods and promoting Indian ones. Later came Gandhi's mass campaigns of non-cooperation and civil disobedience.

Meanwhile, in Europe, nationalism was driving the great powers into rivalry, empire and the catastrophe of the **First World War**. Tagore watched both, and drew lessons from both.

## The life, as a story

### The boy who ran from school

Rabindranath was born on **7 May 1861**, the youngest of thirteen surviving children. He was largely brought up by servants, in a strict regime, often confined to a room or a courtyard. He later wrote that he spent his childhood gazing out of windows at the garden, the pond, the sky, longing for the world outside.

He hated school. He was sent to several, including the Oriental Seminary and the Bengal Academy, and found them all prison-like: rote learning, harsh discipline, English lessons drilled into frightened children. He dropped out of each. He was educated mostly at home, by tutors, in literature, mathematics, science, Sanskrit, English, gymnastics, music and drawing, and by roaming his family's estates.

At about eleven, his father took him on a long journey to the Himalayas, with a stop at **Santiniketan**, a quiet piece of land in rural Bengal that Debendranath had bought for meditation. The boy loved the open sky, the red earth, the freedom. That place would become his life's work.

He began writing poetry as a child. As a teenager, he published poems under the pen name **Bhanusimha**, imitating medieval Vaishnava poets so convincingly that some readers believed they had found a lost old poet.

### England, and back

In **1878**, aged seventeen, he was sent to England to study, perhaps to become a lawyer. He studied briefly in Brighton and at **University College London**, enjoyed English music and society, and returned to India in **1880** without a degree. His family, by then, accepted that this son was not going to follow a conventional career.

### Love, loss and the river

In **1883**, he married **Mrinalini Devi**, then about ten years old, in an arranged marriage (as was customary at the time, though later he would criticise child marriage). They had five children.

The next year, **Kadambari Devi**, the wife of his elder brother Jyotirindranath and Rabindranath's close companion, friend and muse since his boyhood, took her own life. Tagore was devastated. Death would shadow his life: his wife died in 1902, then a daughter, his father, and his youngest son within a few years. His poetry is full of the closeness of death and the persistence of joy.

In the **1890s**, his father sent him to manage the family's estates in rural East Bengal, at **Shelaidaha** and elsewhere along the great **Padma** river. He lived on a houseboat, travelled among villages, met peasants, fishermen, boatmen and Muslim tenant farmers, and saw rural poverty, debt and ignorance at first hand. He wrote many of his finest short stories here, about ordinary village life. And he began to think about **rural reconstruction**: how villages could become self-reliant through education, cooperatives and local leadership.

### A school under the trees

In **1901**, he founded a small school at **Santiniketan**, with five students, including his own son. It was everything his own schools had not been. Classes were held outdoors, under trees. Children learned through nature, music, art, drama and festivals as well as books. Teachers were companions, not jailers. There was no fear of punishment. The school was poor, and Tagore spent his own money and later his Nobel Prize money on it.

He wrote that the highest education is that which does not merely give us information but makes our life in harmony with all existence.

### Swadeshi, and stepping back

When Bengal was partitioned in **1905**, Tagore threw himself into the protest. He wrote patriotic songs that were sung in the streets, including **"Amar Shonar Bangla"**, and the famous **"Ekla Chalo Re"**: *"If they answer not to thy call, walk alone."* He organised **Raksha Bandhan** ceremonies in which Hindus and Muslims tied threads on each other's wrists as a sign of unity.

But soon he grew uneasy. The Swadeshi movement turned violent in places; boycott was enforced by bullying; poor Muslim peasants, who needed cheap British cloth, were coerced by Hindu nationalist landlords and activists. Tagore withdrew from active politics. He explored these doubts in his novel ***Ghare Baire*** (***The Home and the World***, 1916), whose charismatic nationalist hero, Sandip, is revealed as manipulative and destructive, while the gentle landowner Nikhil, who refuses to force anyone, is the true moral centre.

### Gitanjali and the Nobel Prize

In **1912**, he travelled to England with a notebook of his own English prose translations of his Bengali devotional poems. The painter **William Rothenstein** showed them to the poet **W.B. Yeats**, who was overwhelmed. Yeats wrote an introduction, and ***Gitanjali: Song Offerings*** was published in London in 1912.

In **November 1913**, Tagore was awarded the **Nobel Prize in Literature**, the first Asian to receive any Nobel Prize and the first non-European to win it for literature. India was astonished; so was the world. Overnight he became a global celebrity, lecturing in Europe, America, Japan and China, hailed as a prophet from the East.

In **1915**, the British government gave him a **knighthood**.

### Returning the knighthood

On **13 April 1919**, at **Jallianwala Bagh** in Amritsar, British troops under General Reginald Dyer fired on an unarmed crowd trapped in an enclosed ground, killing hundreds. The British imposed martial law in Punjab and censored the news.

Tagore, horrified, wrote to the Viceroy, **Lord Chelmsford**, on **30 May 1919**, giving up his knighthood:

"The time has come when badges of honour make our shame glaring in their incongruous context of humiliation, and I for my part wish to stand, shorn of all special distinctions, by the side of those of my countrymen who, for their so-called insignificance, are liable to suffer degradation not fit for human beings."

It was one of the bravest public acts of the period, by the most famous Indian in the world.

### Visva-Bharati and Sriniketan

In **1921**, he expanded his school into a university, **Visva-Bharati**. Its motto, from a Sanskrit verse, was *"Yatra visvam bhavatyekanidam"*: "where the whole world meets in a single nest". He invited scholars from Europe, China, Japan and elsewhere to teach and study, to create a meeting place of cultures.

In **1922**, with the English agricultural economist **Leonard Elmhirst**, he set up **Sriniketan**, an institute for rural reconstruction: agriculture, crafts, cooperatives, health and education for the surrounding villages.

### The arguments with Gandhi

Tagore and **Gandhi** admired each other deeply. Tagore is often credited with popularising the title "**Mahatma**" (great soul) for Gandhi; Gandhi called Tagore "**Gurudev**" (revered teacher). But they argued, openly, in print, about the most important questions of the freedom struggle: non-cooperation, the burning of foreign cloth, the charkha, and the place of reason in politics. (We'll look at the debates below.)

### The painter at sixty

In his late sixties, Tagore began to paint, starting from doodles in the margins of his manuscripts. He produced some two thousand paintings and drawings: strange, dark, expressive images of faces, creatures and landscapes, unlike anything else in Indian art at the time. They were exhibited in Paris, Berlin, Moscow and New York.

### Last words: a crisis in civilisation

In **1930**, he met **Albert Einstein** at Einstein's summer home in **Caputh**, near Berlin, and their conversation about whether truth exists independently of humanity became famous.

In his last years, he watched fascism rise in Europe and Japan, and the world move toward another war. For the celebration of his eightieth birthday, in **April 1941**, he wrote his final essay, ***Crisis in Civilisation***, expressing his bitter disappointment with the West, whose civilisation he had once admired, and with British rule in India. Yet he ended with hope: he would not commit the grievous sin of losing faith in humanity.

He died in Calcutta on **7 August 1941**, at the family home in Jorasanko where he had been born.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: The Religion of Man

**The idea in one line:** The divine is found in the human spirit's capacity for love, creativity and union with all existence, not in rituals or dogmas.

Tagore's religion was not that of the temple, the scripture or the priest. It grew from the Upanishads, from his father's Brahmo faith, from the **Baul** wandering singers of Bengal (who sang of the "Man of the Heart" living within), from Kabir and the Bhakti saints, and from the Buddha.

He called his faith "**the Religion of Man**" (the title of his 1930 Hibbert Lectures at Oxford). The infinite, he said, is revealed in the finite, especially in human beings at their best: in their love, art, sacrifice and search for truth. God is not far away; the divine is the "Man of the Heart", the universal human spirit in which each of us shares.

His most famous poem in *Gitanjali* shows how this faith turns away from the temple:

"Leave this chanting and singing and telling of beads! Whom dost thou worship in this lonely dark corner of a temple with doors all shut? Open thine eyes and see thy God is not before thee! He is there where the tiller is tilling the hard ground and where the pathmaker is breaking stones."

Read that last line again. God is with the farmer and the labourer, not in the closed temple.

**Why it matters:** This is a religion of joy, nature and humanity, without fanaticism or exclusion. It speaks to people who feel that conventional religion is too narrow but who still sense something sacred in life, in music, in nature, in love.

**A common misunderstanding:** Tagore was not a vague "spiritual" poet with no hard ideas. His religion had sharp edges: it rejected caste, ritualism, narrow nationalism and blind obedience. It was a religion that judged other religions by whether they freed or enslaved the human spirit.

### Idea 2: The menace of nationalism

**The idea in one line:** The nation, as a political machine organised for power and profit, crushes human beings and leads to war; it must never become an object of worship.

In **1916–17**, during the First World War, Tagore gave lectures in Japan and America, published as ***Nationalism*** (1917). His message was shocking to audiences hungry for national glory.

He distinguished between a **society**, a living community of people bound by culture, cooperation and mutual care, and the **Nation**, which he described as the organisation of a whole people as a mechanical, political and commercial machine for the pursuit of power and profit. The Nation, he said, treats human beings as parts of a machine. It feeds on hatred of other nations. And it was the Nation, in this sense, that had produced European imperialism and was now destroying Europe in war.

He warned Japan, then rapidly militarising and industrialising, not to copy the West's nationalism. (Japan did, with catastrophic results.) And he warned India too:

"Nationalism is a great menace. It is the particular thing which for years has been at the bottom of India's troubles."

He meant that India should not seek to become a copy of a Western nation-state, with its armies, aggression and worship of power. India's problem, he said, was not mainly political but **social**: caste, divisions, ignorance, the inability of people to cooperate. Political freedom without social freedom would be hollow.

He wrote, in a letter, words often quoted:

"Patriotism cannot be our final spiritual shelter; my refuge is humanity. I will not buy glass for the price of diamonds, and I will never allow patriotism to triumph over humanity as long as I live."

**Why it matters:** Within a generation of his warnings came fascism, Nazism, Japanese militarism and the Second World War: nationalism at its most murderous. Today, as nationalism rises again in many countries, including India, Tagore's distinction between love of one's people and worship of the nation is more relevant than ever.

**A common misunderstanding:** Tagore was not anti-Indian or opposed to India's freedom. He loved his land passionately and wrote its anthem. He supported self-rule. What he opposed was the **ideology** that makes the nation-state the highest value, above truth, justice and humanity.

### Idea 3: Where the mind is without fear

**The idea in one line:** True freedom is inner and social freedom: of the mind, of knowledge, of truth, and from narrow divisions.

Poem 35 of *Gitanjali* is perhaps the most famous poem in modern India:

"Where the mind is without fear and the head is held high;
Where knowledge is free;
Where the world has not been broken up into fragments by narrow domestic walls;
Where words come out from the depth of truth;
Where tireless striving stretches its arms towards perfection;
Where the clear stream of reason has not lost its way into the dreary desert sand of dead habit;
Where the mind is led forward by thee into ever-widening thought and action—
Into that heaven of freedom, my Father, let my country awake."

Notice what freedom means here. It is not mainly about who rules. It is about **fearlessness**, **free knowledge**, **truth**, **reason** and breaking down "narrow domestic walls", the walls of caste, religion, region and community that divide people. And notice the phrase "the dreary desert sand of dead habit": tradition followed without thought.

**Why it matters:** This poem is a test for any society, including independent India. Are our minds without fear? Is knowledge free? Have we broken down narrow walls, or built new ones? Tagore's freedom is never finished; it must be achieved again by each generation.

**A common misunderstanding:** The poem is sometimes recited as simple patriotism. But it is really a demanding description of what a country must become to deserve freedom, and it implies criticism of any country, including India, that fails.

### Idea 4: Education in freedom and joy

**The idea in one line:** Children learn best in freedom, close to nature, through art and joy, not through fear, rote and punishment.

Tagore's hatred of his own schooling became the basis of one of the great educational experiments of the twentieth century. In his short story-parable **"The Parrot's Training"** (1918), a king orders that a bird be educated. The scholars build a golden cage, stuff the bird with pages of books, and celebrate as it stops singing, stops moving, and finally dies, its gullet full of rustling paper. "The bird is educated," they announce.

At Santiniketan, he tried the opposite:

- Classes under trees, with the sky above.
- Learning through music, dance, drama, art, crafts and festivals, not just books.
- Mother-tongue education, alongside English.
- Freedom for children to explore, ask questions and follow their interests.
- A connection to the surrounding villages and their work.
- An international outlook, with teachers and students from many countries.

His school produced remarkable people: the filmmaker **Satyajit Ray**, the economist **Amartya Sen**, and Indira Gandhi studied there briefly.

**Why it matters:** Think of India's exam pressure: coaching factories, rote memorisation, students crushed by fear of marks, suicides in coaching hubs. (If exam pressure is weighing on you or someone you know, India's free Tele-MANAS helpline is 14416.) Tagore's vision of joyful, creative, nature-based learning is a powerful critique of this system, and has influenced progressive educators across the world. India's **National Education Policy (2020)** speaks of mother-tongue instruction, arts integration and reducing rote learning, ideas Tagore championed a century earlier.

**A common misunderstanding:** Tagore's education was not "anything goes". It had discipline, but discipline that came from love of the work and the community, not from fear.

### Idea 5: Society before state: rural reconstruction

**The idea in one line:** India's real strength lies in its villages and communities, which must become self-reliant through cooperation, education and local initiative.

From his years on the Padma, Tagore concluded that India's deepest problem was not British rule alone but the **decay of its villages**: poverty, debt, disease, ignorance, caste division and dependence. He argued that India's traditional strength had been its **samaj** (society), its communities that cared for their own schools, tanks and temples, not its states and kings.

So he started cooperative banks for farmers on his estates (even putting his Nobel Prize money into one), and founded **Sriniketan** to train villagers in improved agriculture, crafts, health and cooperation. He wanted villages to help themselves rather than wait for government or charity.

**Why it matters:** These ideas anticipated later movements for rural development, cooperatives, self-help groups and **panchayati raj** (local self-government). When you see women's self-help groups in villages, or farmer producer organisations, you are seeing something Tagore dreamed of.

**A common misunderstanding:** Tagore was not romanticising villages. He saw their misery clearly. He thought they could be revived through modern knowledge combined with local cooperation.

### Idea 6: Unity in diversity, not uniformity

**The idea in one line:** A civilisation's greatness lies in harmonising its many peoples, not in forcing them into one shape.

Tagore saw Indian history as a long meeting of peoples: Aryans and Dravidians, Buddhists and Hindus, Muslims and Christians, Persians and Europeans. India's mission, he said, was to find unity in this diversity, not to erase difference.

His song **"Bharat Tirtha"** (India, the pilgrimage place) describes India as a shore where wave after wave of peoples have come and merged: Aryans, non-Aryans, Dravidians, Chinese, Scythians, Huns, Pathans, Mughals, and now the West. And "Jana Gana Mana" lists India's regions and communities, Punjab, Sindh, Gujarat, Maratha, Dravida, Utkala, Banga, all joined in one song.

**Why it matters:** India today is an argument about exactly this: whether its unity should be built on diversity or on uniformity. Tagore's answer is clear.

**A common misunderstanding:** Unity in diversity does not mean that "anything goes" or that injustice within communities should be tolerated. Tagore criticised caste, child marriage and the oppression of women within Hindu society. Diversity, for him, had to be humane.

### Idea 7: Truth, beauty and the human mind

**The idea in one line:** Truth and beauty are not separate from human beings; they are realised in the meeting between the human mind and the universe.

In **1930**, Tagore and Einstein had a famous conversation. Einstein held that truth exists independently of human beings: the Pythagorean theorem would be true even if there were no humans. Tagore replied that truth, like beauty, is realised through the human mind: "This world is a human world... the scientific view of it is also that of the scientific man." He meant that what we call reality is always the reality of a universal human mind meeting the world.

Einstein said, "Then I am more religious than you are!" Neither convinced the other.

**Why it matters:** This debate, between realism (truth is out there) and a more human-centred view, is still alive in philosophy of science. And Tagore's insistence that science, art and ethics are all human ways of meeting the universe challenges the split between "science" and "humanities" in Indian education.

**A common misunderstanding:** Tagore was not anti-science. He wrote a popular science book, *Visva Parichay* (1937), and admired scientific inquiry. He questioned only the idea that science gives a view of the world from nowhere.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Vivekananda: the other Bengali

Tagore admired **Swami Vivekananda**, and both wanted India to meet the world as an equal, confident in its spiritual heritage. But Vivekananda's message of strength, manliness and national awakening was different in tone from Tagore's gentler, more poetic and more cosmopolitan vision. Tagore was wary of where calls for national strength could lead.

### The Buddha: compassion beyond walls

Tagore loved Buddhist stories and wrote poems and plays from them, including ***Chandalika***, about a low-caste girl to whom a Buddhist monk, Ananda, asks for water, and thereby treats as an equal human being. The Buddha's rejection of caste and his universal compassion shaped Tagore's religion of man.

### Gandhi: love and argument

The friendship and argument between Tagore and **Gandhi** is one of the great dialogues of the twentieth century. They respected each other deeply, and Tagore supported Gandhi's struggle for India's freedom and against untouchability. But they disagreed on key points:

**1. Non-cooperation and the boycott of education (1921).** When Gandhi called on students to leave government schools and colleges, Tagore objected: you cannot fight ignorance by abandoning schools. He worried that non-cooperation was a negative, isolating idea, building walls between India and the West.

**2. Burning foreign cloth.** Gandhi's followers made bonfires of foreign cloth. Tagore was disturbed: the poor needed cloth, and burning it seemed a wasteful, emotional act of hatred rather than a reasoned economic policy.

**3. The charkha.** Gandhi asked everyone to spin for half an hour daily. Tagore objected to a single mechanical activity being imposed on everyone as a national duty, and doubted that spinning would solve India's economic problems. He wrote that the charkha did not require anyone to think; it simply turned the wheel.

**4. The Bihar earthquake (1934).** When a devastating earthquake struck Bihar, Gandhi said it was a divine chastisement for the sin of untouchability. Tagore was appalled. He said such an unscientific view of natural events was harmful, since it made people believe in blind supernatural punishment instead of reason.

Gandhi replied to each criticism with respect and firmness. In one reply he wrote that the poet lived in a magnificent world of his own creation, while he himself was a slave of somebody else's creation, the spinning wheel. Their argument, never bitter, showed what democracy and public reasoning can look like: disagreement between friends, in public, over the deepest questions.

Imagine them meeting at Santiniketan:

*Gandhi:* The starving millions need work, Gurudev. The charkha is the bread of the poor.

*Tagore:* Then let them have a hundred kinds of work, and the freedom to think about which. I fear a nation that obeys a single command, even a good one.

*Gandhi:* And I fear a poet who sings while the house burns.

*Tagore:* My song is my way of putting out the fire, Mahatma. Freedom of mind is also water.

### Amartya Sen: the economist's reading

**Amartya Sen**, who grew up at Santiniketan (Tagore named him), has written that Tagore's commitment to reasoning, freedom and openness to the world is often missed by Westerners who see him as a mystical sage. Sen's *The Argumentative Indian* (2005) celebrates the Tagore–Gandhi debates as a model of public reasoning.

### Later voices

- **Satyajit Ray** made films of Tagore's stories (*Charulata*, *Ghare Baire*, *Teen Kanya*).
- **W.B. Yeats**, **Ezra Pound** and **André Gide** promoted his work in the West; later Western fashion turned against him, partly because of poor translations.
- **Jawaharlal Nehru** admired Tagore's internationalism; Gandhi and Tagore together shaped Nehru's vision of India.
- Bangladesh honours him as one of its own; his songs are central to Bengali identity across the border.

## See it around you

**The national anthem.** Every morning assembly, cricket match and cinema hall rings with Tagore's words. Next time, notice how the anthem names regions and peoples of a diverse land, and ask whether the spirit of "unity in diversity" still holds.

**Debates about nationalism.** When someone is called "anti-national" for criticising the government, or when love of country is measured by loud slogans, Tagore's distinction between patriotism and nation-worship is directly relevant.

**Exam stress and schooling.** Every news story about coaching-centre pressure, rote learning or student suicides is a reminder of "The Parrot's Training". Alternative schools, art-based curricula and the NEP's emphasis on mother-tongue and holistic learning echo Santiniketan.

**Protest by renunciation.** When writers and artists return awards in protest against violence or injustice, they follow the path Tagore took with his knighthood in 1919.

**Self-help groups and cooperatives.** Women's SHGs, dairy cooperatives and farmer producer organisations continue his dream of self-reliant villages.

**Rabindra Sangeet.** In Bengal and Bangladesh, Tagore's songs are sung at weddings, funerals, festivals, protests and quiet evenings, a whole emotional life in song.

**The meeting of cultures.** Every time an Indian student studies abroad, a foreign scholar studies Indian classical music, or an Indian city celebrates a festival of many communities, the Visva-Bharati motto lives on: where the world meets in a single nest.

## Use it

**1. Love your country, but don't worship it.** Ask yourself: is my love of my country making me more open, generous and just, or more fearful, hateful and narrow? If the second, it has become what Tagore called the Nation.

**2. Read the "narrow domestic walls" in your life.** Notice the walls of caste, religion, language, region and class around you. Choose one, and step across it: a friendship, a meal, a conversation.

**3. Learn with joy.** If you're a student, find one subject you love and study it freely, beyond the syllabus. If you teach or parent, ask whether your child is a parrot in a golden cage.

**4. Make something.** Tagore wrote, composed, painted, acted and built institutions. Whatever your job, make space for creation: music, drawing, writing, cooking, gardening. Creativity is a form of freedom.

**5. Disagree with people you love, in public, kindly.** Tagore and Gandhi showed that you can criticise your heroes and friends without hatred. Try it.

**6. Walk alone when you must.** "Ekla Chalo Re": if no one answers your call, walk alone. When you're sure something is right, don't wait for the crowd.

**7. Begin late.** Tagore began painting at sixty-seven and became a major artist. It is never too late to start.

**8. Spend time in nature.** Tagore's philosophy grew from the sky, the river and the trees. Sit under a tree for twenty minutes without your phone. Notice what you think.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Idealism and impracticality.** Critics, including Gandhi, felt that Tagore's poetic vision was too far from the harsh realities of mass politics. India's freedom was won by organised mass movements, not by poets' warnings. His rural reconstruction projects were small and often struggled financially.

**2. An aristocrat's perspective.** Tagore was born into wealth, a zamindar family (landowners living off peasants' rents). Some critics argue his romantic view of village society and harmony underestimated the conflict between landlords and tenants, and that his own position shaped his dislike of agitational politics.

**3. "The East" and "the West".** Like Vivekananda, Tagore sometimes spoke of a spiritual East and a materialistic West, a generalisation that flattered Asian audiences but oversimplified both.

**4. Personal life and child marriage.** He married a ten-year-old girl and arranged early marriages for his own daughters, which ended unhappily, even as he criticised such customs in his writing.

**5. Underestimating the need for power.** Tagore's distrust of politics and the state sometimes meant underestimating how much organised political power was needed to fight injustice, whether colonial rule or caste oppression. Ambedkar's insistence on law and state power to end caste offers a sharp contrast.

**6. Translation and fame.** His English translations of his own work were often weaker and more vaguely mystical than the Bengali originals, which helped create a misleading image of him in the West as a soft-spoken Eastern sage rather than a sharp, modern, rational thinker.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have known Tagore as the man who wrote the national anthem, a bearded sage in old photographs, a Nobel laureate in a quiz answer. You may have assumed that loving your country means never questioning nationalism.

**Now,** you can see a restless, many-sided genius who refused every narrow wall: the wall of the classroom, the wall of the temple, the wall of the nation. You can see that he distinguished between love of one's people and worship of the nation-state, warned that the second leads to war and cruelty, and returned the Empire's honour in the name of the people it degraded. You can see that he argued in public with Gandhi, out of love and respect, and showed what free public reasoning looks like. And you can see the limits of a poet's politics.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **The national anthem** sounds like a song of a diverse people, not of a uniform nation.
- **A shouting nationalist debate** on television looks like what Tagore called the Nation.
- **A classroom of frightened students** looks like a golden cage.
- **A wall of caste or religion** looks like the "narrow domestic walls" of Gitanjali.
- **An argument with a friend you admire** becomes a duty, not a betrayal.
- **A tree, a river, a song** becomes a place where the mind can become free.

A boy who gazed through windows at a garden he wasn't allowed to enter grew up to open a school without walls and to tell the whole world: let my country awake into a heaven of freedom where the mind is without fear.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Rabindranath Tagore (1861–1941), Bengali poet, songwriter, novelist, playwright, painter, educator and philosopher; first Asian Nobel laureate (Literature, 1913).
- **Context:** the Tagore family and the Bengal Renaissance, British rule, the partition of Bengal and the Swadeshi movement, Gandhi's mass movements, the World Wars.
- **Life:** hated school; Santiniketan school (1901); *Gitanjali* and the Nobel Prize; returned his knighthood after Jallianwala Bagh (1919); Visva-Bharati (1921) and Sriniketan (1922); painted in old age; *Crisis in Civilisation* (1941).
- **Religion of Man:** the divine in the human spirit, in love, creativity and union with all.
- **Against nationalism:** the Nation as a machine of power is "a great menace"; patriotism must not triumph over humanity.
- **Freedom:** "Where the mind is without fear"; breaking narrow domestic walls.
- **Education:** joyful learning in nature and the arts; "The Parrot's Training".
- **Society first:** rural reconstruction, cooperatives, self-reliant villages.
- **Debates with Gandhi:** non-cooperation, cloth burning, the charkha, the Bihar earthquake.
- **Anthems:** "Jana Gana Mana" (India) and "Amar Shonar Bangla" (Bangladesh).
- **Criticisms:** impractical idealism, an aristocrat's view, East–West generalisations, personal contradictions on child marriage, weak translations.

**Key terms**

- **Gitanjali:** "song offerings", his collection of devotional poems.
- **Santiniketan:** "abode of peace", his school and later university in rural Bengal.
- **Visva-Bharati:** the university he founded, "where the whole world meets in a single nest".
- **Sriniketan:** his institute for rural reconstruction.
- **Swadeshi:** the movement to boycott foreign goods and use Indian ones, begun in 1905.
- **Religion of Man:** Tagore's humanist faith in the divine within the universal human spirit.
- **Nation (Tagore's sense):** the people organised as a political and commercial machine for power.
- **Samaj:** society, the self-governing community he valued over the state.
- **Rabindra Sangeet:** the more than two thousand songs he composed.
- **Ekla Chalo Re:** "Walk alone", his song of individual courage.
