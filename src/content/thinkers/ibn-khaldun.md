## Start here

Think of a successful family business in your town. A sweet shop, perhaps, or a textile firm. The grandfather started it with nothing: he worked sixteen hours a day, knew every customer by name, slept in the shop during festivals. His son grew up watching him, and kept the business strong, with the same discipline. But the grandson grew up rich. He went to an expensive school, drove a fine car, and thought of the business as something that simply existed. And by the great-grandson's time, the shop is closed, or sold, or fought over in court.

Many cultures have a proverb about this. In English: "Shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations." In Japan: "Rice paddy to rice paddy in three generations."

Now think of empires. The Mughals came down from the hills of Central Asia as tough, united warriors. Within two centuries, their emperors lived in marble palaces, surrounded by luxury, and the empire was falling apart. The same story repeats with the Romans, the Abbasids, the Ottomans, the British.

Why? Is it just chance, or is there a pattern?

Six and a half centuries ago, a North African statesman who had served half a dozen kings, been thrown into prison, lost his parents to the plague and his wife and children to a shipwreck, retreated to a remote castle in the Algerian hills. There, in **five months**, he wrote the introduction to a history of the world. The introduction became more famous than the history. It is called the ***Muqaddimah***, and in it he tried to do something no one had done before: to find the **laws** of human society, the hidden causes behind the rise and fall of dynasties, cities and civilisations.

His name was **Abd al-Rahman ibn Khaldun**. The British historian **Arnold Toynbee** called the *Muqaddimah* "a philosophy of history which is undoubtedly the greatest work of its kind that has ever yet been created by any mind in any time or place." He is called the father of sociology, of the science of history, and even of economics. By the end of this page, you'll be able to see his patterns in families, companies, political parties, nations and your own life.

## The world he was born into

### The Maghreb: a land of shifting dynasties

Ibn Khaldun was born in **1332** in **Tunis**, in North Africa. Arabs call this region the **Maghreb** ("the West"): modern Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Libya. In his time, it was divided among three rival dynasties: the **Hafsids** in Tunis, the **Zayyanids** in Tlemcen (western Algeria), and the **Marinids** in Fez (Morocco). They fought each other constantly. Dynasties rose and fell, rulers were murdered by their brothers or ministers, and powerful tribes switched sides.

Much of the land was home to **Berber** and **Arab tribes**, many of them nomadic or semi-nomadic, living in the mountains and deserts. Rulers depended on them for soldiers. A clever politician needed to know which tribes to win over.

### The family from Seville

Ibn Khaldun's family were proud of their history. They traced their ancestry to an Arab from **Hadramawt** in Yemen who had come to Spain with the early Muslim armies. For five centuries, the family lived in **Seville**, where they held high political office. In the thirteenth century, as Christian Castile advanced, they left Spain for North Africa, shortly before Seville fell in **1248**.

So Ibn Khaldun grew up in a family that had personally experienced the fall of a civilisation: Muslim Spain, once the most brilliant culture in Europe, now shrinking to the small kingdom of Granada.

### The Black Death

In **1348**, when he was sixteen, the **Black Death** arrived in Tunis. This plague killed perhaps a third or more of the population of Europe, North Africa and the Middle East. It killed both of Ibn Khaldun's parents and most of his teachers.

He later wrote about it as if civilisation itself had been wiped clean: "Cities and buildings were laid waste, roads and way signs were obliterated, settlements and mansions became empty, dynasties and tribes grew weak. The entire inhabited world changed." (paraphrase). It seemed to him that a new age had begun, and that someone needed to describe the world afresh.

### The wider world: Timur and the Delhi Sultanate

Far to the east, the Islamic world was in turmoil. The Mongols had destroyed Baghdad in 1258. In Central Asia, a new conqueror, **Timur** (Tamerlane), would rise in the 1360s and sweep across Persia, Iraq, Syria and India. In **1398**, Timur sacked **Delhi**. Ibn Khaldun would meet him face to face.

In India, the **Delhi Sultanate** was ruled when Ibn Khaldun was born by **Muhammad bin Tughluq**, famous for his bold, disastrous experiments: moving the capital from Delhi to Daulatabad and issuing a copper "token currency". The Moroccan traveller **Ibn Battuta** served as a judge in Delhi under Tughluq, and his stories would appear, briefly, in Ibn Khaldun's book.

## The life, as a story

### A brilliant young man in a hurry

Abd al-Rahman ibn Khaldun was born on **27 May 1332**. He received the best education Tunis could offer: the Qur'an, Hadith, Maliki law, Arabic language and poetry, and also philosophy, logic and mathematics, from a teacher named **al-Abili**, who had studied the works of Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd. As a young man, Ibn Khaldun wrote summaries of several of Ibn Rushd's works.

After the plague, he entered politics. At just **twenty**, he became an official in the Hafsid court in Tunis, responsible for writing the sultan's official signature on documents.

### Prison in Fez

Ambitious and restless, he moved in **1354** to **Fez**, the capital of the Marinid sultan **Abu Inan**, who gathered scholars at his court. Ibn Khaldun became a secretary and member of the sultan's scholarly circle.

But he was a political gambler. In **1357**, he was suspected of plotting with a deposed prince, and **thrown into prison** for about **twenty-one months**. He was released only when the sultan died in 1358. He then helped a rival claimant win the throne, and was rewarded with high office.

### Granada and the King of Castile

In **1362**, he went to **Granada**, the last Muslim kingdom in Spain, where the sultan **Muhammad V** and his famous vizier, the poet **Ibn al-Khatib**, welcomed him. (Ibn al-Khatib's poems are still carved on the walls of the Alhambra palace.)

In **1364**, the sultan sent Ibn Khaldun as ambassador to **Pedro I**, the Christian King of Castile, in **Seville**, the city of his ancestors. He saw the old family houses. Pedro was so impressed that he offered to give back the family's estates if Ibn Khaldun would stay and serve him. He politely refused.

Soon his friendship with Ibn al-Khatib cooled, perhaps from jealousy, and he left Spain.

### Kingmaker among the tribes

For the next decade, he served one North African ruler after another. He was **chamberlain** (chief minister) in **Bougie** (Béjaïa, in Algeria). He spent long periods among the **Arab and Berber tribes**, recruiting them as soldiers for one ruler or another. He learned how tribal loyalty worked: who followed whom, why tribes united and why they split, what made fighters brave and what made them desert.

It was dangerous work. Rulers he served were killed. He was robbed, chased, stripped of his possessions, and accused of treachery. By the age of forty-three, he was exhausted with politics.

### The castle in the hills

In **1375**, he took his family to **Qal'at Ibn Salama**, a remote fortress in western Algeria, under the protection of a friendly tribe. He stayed there for nearly **four years**.

And there, he began to write. He planned a history of the Arabs and Berbers. But first, he decided, he needed to explain **how to write history properly**: what society is, how it works, why states rise and fall, and how to tell true reports from false ones. He wrote this introduction, the *Muqaddimah*, in **five months**, finishing in **1377**. He said that ideas poured into his mind "like cream into a churn". He would keep revising it for the rest of his life.

### Cairo: judge, teacher, mourner

To finish his history, he needed libraries. He returned to Tunis in 1378, but court rivals made life difficult. In **1382** he sailed for **Egypt**, saying he was going on pilgrimage to Mecca. He never returned to the Maghreb.

**Cairo** was then the greatest city of the Islamic world, capital of the **Mamluk** sultanate. Ibn Khaldun lectured at the famous **al-Azhar** mosque-university, and in **1384** the sultan **Barquq** appointed him **chief Maliki judge**. He was a strict, honest judge who refused to bend to influence. He made enemies, and was dismissed and reappointed **six times** over the next twenty years.

In **1384**, his wife and children set out from Tunis to join him. Their ship sank in a storm near Alexandria. All of them drowned, with all his possessions. He wrote little about it, but said he gave up the world for a time.

### The meeting with Timur

In **1400**, **Timur**, the most feared conqueror of the age, invaded Syria. The young Mamluk sultan marched to defend **Damascus**, taking Ibn Khaldun with him. But the sultan rushed back to Cairo to deal with a plot, leaving the city to its fate.

Inside Damascus, the leading citizens debated whether to surrender. In **January 1401**, Ibn Khaldun, now nearly seventy, had himself **lowered over the city wall on a rope** and went to Timur's camp.

He spent over a month with the conqueror. They talked about history, kings and the Maghreb. Timur asked him to write a detailed description of North Africa, perhaps planning to conquer it. Ibn Khaldun flattered Timur, studied him, and later described him as one of the greatest and most intelligent of kings, but also as a destroyer. He saw in Timur exactly the kind of leader his theory predicted: the head of a tough, united group, sweeping aside soft, divided rulers.

He got safe passage home, though he was robbed on the way. Damascus was sacked and burned.

### The end

He spent his last years in Cairo, teaching, judging and revising his books. He wrote an **autobiography**, one of the first by a historian. He died on **17 March 1406**, aged seventy-three, and was buried in the Sufi cemetery outside Cairo's **Bab al-Nasr** gate.

For centuries, the *Muqaddimah* was read by a few Ottoman scholars. Then, in the nineteenth century, European scholars discovered it, translated it, and were astonished. Here, they said, was sociology, economics and a scientific philosophy of history, four centuries before Europe invented them.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: History must be tested against the nature of things

**The idea in one line:** Don't believe a historical report just because a respected person repeated it; ask whether it could possibly be true, given how societies actually work.

The *Muqaddimah* begins with a complaint. Historians, even famous ones, fill their books with nonsense. Why? Ibn Khaldun lists reasons:

- **Partisanship:** people accept reports that favour their own side.
- **Trust in sources:** they believe whoever they heard it from, without checking.
- **Not understanding what they report:** they repeat words without grasping the situation.
- **Flattery:** they praise the powerful to win their favour.
- **Ignorance of how society works:** this, he says, is the most important reason of all.

He gives a famous example. The great historian **al-Mas'udi** had written that Moses counted the army of the Israelites in the desert, and found **600,000** fighting men or more. Ibn Khaldun asks: is this possible? The lands of Egypt and Syria could not support or move such an army. An army that size could not even fight as a unit; its ranks would stretch far beyond sight. And according to the same sources, only four generations separated Moses from Israel (Jacob); a single family could not grow to 600,000 fighting men in that time. The number, he concludes, must be wrong.

The tool he uses is what he calls the science of **umran**: the study of human social organisation, its nature and its laws. If you know how societies work, you can **test** reports: a story that contradicts the nature of things is false, however respected its source.

He also warns against the opposite error. At the court of Fez, he tells us, people whispered that the traveller **Ibn Battuta** was a liar, because his tales of the wealth of the **Sultan of Delhi** seemed impossible. A wise vizier warned Ibn Khaldun not to reject a report simply because it was strange to people who had never seen such things. The test is not "Is it familiar?" but "Is it possible, given how the world works?"

**Why it matters:** This is the beginning of **critical history**: the idea that historians must not just collect stories but test them. It is the same test we need today against fake news and viral rumours.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Khaldun did not reject tradition or religious sources. He applied his test to historical reports about events, not to matters of faith. He was a devout Muslim and a judge of religious law.

### Idea 2: Society is natural, and humans need each other

**The idea in one line:** Human beings cannot survive alone; they must cooperate, and from cooperation come society, the division of labour, and the need for authority.

Ibn Khaldun begins his science with the philosophers' saying: **"man is political by nature"**. He explains it with a simple example. Consider the food you need for one day. To get bread, someone must grow the wheat, harvest it, grind it into flour, knead the dough and bake it. Each of these steps needs tools, which need carpenters and blacksmiths. No single person could do all of this. **Cooperation** is necessary even to eat.

The same is true for defence. Animals have claws, horns and speed. Humans have none of these; instead, they have **thought** and **hands**, which make tools and weapons. But one person with a weapon is no match for a group. So humans must defend themselves together.

And because humans are also aggressive, they need someone to **restrain** them from harming each other. So every society needs some form of authority, which he calls **royal authority** (mulk).

**Why it matters:** This is a clear, practical explanation of why society and government exist, based not on myth or divine command but on human needs. Over three centuries later, **Adam Smith** made the same point about the **division of labour** with his famous pin factory.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Khaldun did not think authority must be gentle or democratic. He saw that authority usually rests on force and group power. His point was descriptive: this is how societies actually work.

### Idea 3: Asabiyya, the glue of power

**The idea in one line:** Groups gain power through asabiyya, a strong feeling of solidarity and loyalty; whichever group has the strongest asabiyya rules.

This is Ibn Khaldun's most famous idea. **Asabiyya** is usually translated as **"group feeling"**, **"social cohesion"** or **"solidarity"**. It is the bond that makes people stand together, defend each other, and willingly sacrifice for the group.

Where does it come from? Above all from **kinship**: shared blood, real or believed. But it can also come from long alliance, shared hardship, and shared religion. People who have lived, suffered and fought together trust each other with their lives.

Asabiyya, he says, is strongest among people living in hard conditions: nomads of the desert and the mountains. They have no walls, no police, no army to protect them. They must defend themselves, together. They are tough, brave, used to hardship and fiercely loyal.

The group with the strongest asabiyya can conquer others and found a **dynasty**. Royal authority, Ibn Khaldun says, is the natural goal of asabiyya.

And **religion** can multiply asabiyya enormously. When a prophet or reformer unites people under one faith, rivalries and jealousies disappear, and everyone looks in one direction. That, he argues, is how the Arabs, a divided people, united by Islam, conquered an empire stretching from Spain to Central Asia within a century.

**Why it matters:** Asabiyya explains why small, united groups often defeat large, divided ones. Think of a startup team that outcompetes a huge company; a disciplined political cadre that defeats a much bigger but divided party; a team of underdogs in sport whose unity beats a team of stars.

**A common misunderstanding:** Asabiyya is not the same as tribalism or racism. It can be based on any shared bond. Ibn Khaldun did not praise or condemn it; he studied it as a force, like gravity, that explains how power works.

### Idea 4: Desert and city, toughness and luxury

**The idea in one line:** Hard rural life builds solidarity and courage; comfortable city life builds wealth, arts and luxury, but slowly destroys solidarity.

Ibn Khaldun contrasts two ways of life:

- **Badawa** (rural, nomadic, "desert" life): simple, tough, focused on bare necessities. People are brave, self-reliant, and closely bound to their group.
- **Hadara** (settled, urban life): comfortable, refined, focused on luxuries. It produces fine buildings, crafts, sciences and arts. Ibn Khaldun admired these: cities are where civilisation reaches its height.

But urban life has a cost. City people rely on walls, soldiers and laws for their protection, so they lose the habit of defending themselves. They come to love comfort and luxury. They compete for wealth and status, and their solidarity weakens. And so they become vulnerable to a tougher group from outside.

There is a cycle: tough rural groups conquer the soft cities, settle in them, enjoy their luxuries, grow soft in turn, and are themselves conquered by the next tough group from the margins.

**Why it matters:** This explains many patterns in history: the Mongols conquering China and Persia, the Turks conquering the Abbasid world, the Mughals coming from Central Asia to rule the rich plains of India, and, in turn, the rise of hardy new groups like the **Marathas** and the **Sikh Khalsa** as the Mughals weakened.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Khaldun did not say that rural life is better than city life, or that we should all go back to the desert. He saw that civilisation needs cities, and that urban culture is the height of human achievement. He was describing a trade-off, not giving moral advice.

### Idea 5: Dynasties last about three generations

**The idea in one line:** A dynasty's founders are tough and united; their children keep what their fathers built; their grandchildren imitate without understanding; the next generation destroys it.

Ibn Khaldun noticed that dynasties have a natural lifespan, like a human being. He described it in terms of **generations** (of about forty years each):

1. **The builder:** knows what it cost to build the dynasty. He shares hardship with his followers, keeps his group's asabiyya strong, and wins power.
2. **The one in contact with the builder:** learned directly from the founder, watched how it was done, and keeps the tradition, though with less personal understanding.
3. **The imitator:** follows the tradition but doesn't understand why it matters. He relies on what came before.
4. **The destroyer:** has lost all connection with the qualities that built the dynasty. He thinks power is his by birth, not something earned. He despises his followers, and they abandon him.

"Prestige," he concluded, lasts **at most four generations**. A dynasty, as a rule, does not last more than about **three generations**, around **120 years**.

He also described the stages of a dynasty: conquest, then the ruler's seizure of all power from his kinsmen, then a period of leisure, building and prosperity, then contentment and imitation of predecessors, and finally waste and squandering, when the dynasty grows senile and collapses.

**Why it matters:** This pattern fits not only kingdoms but family businesses, political parties, sports clubs and companies. Think of how often a founder's vision fades as power passes to children and grandchildren who never knew the struggle.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Khaldun's "three generations" is a general pattern, not an exact law. He knew dynasties could last longer or fall sooner. He was identifying a tendency, like an economist describing a trend.

### Idea 6: Injustice ruins civilisation

**The idea in one line:** When rulers seize property, tax heavily and force people to work, people lose the incentive to work and produce, and civilisation decays.

One chapter title of the *Muqaddimah* says it simply: **"Injustice brings about the ruin of civilization."**

Ibn Khaldun explains: when people's property is not safe, when the government can take what they earn, they stop trying to earn. Why build a business if it can be confiscated? Why farm if the crop will be seized? Production falls, markets shrink, cities decline, and in the end the government itself has nothing to tax.

And injustice, he says, is not just outright robbery. Anyone who takes someone's property, forces them to work without pay, or makes unjustified demands on them, commits injustice. Forced labour, he notes, is one of the worst forms of injustice, because people's labour is their capital.

He also warned rulers against going into business themselves. When the government trades, it competes unfairly with its own subjects, buys cheap and sells dear using its power, and ruins the merchants and farmers. In the end, this ruins the tax revenue too.

**Why it matters:** This is an early and powerful argument for the **rule of law**, **property rights** and **limited government** as foundations of prosperity. Modern economists who study why some nations grow rich and others stay poor make similar arguments.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Khaldun was not a modern free-market libertarian. He thought government was necessary and that a good ruler should spend on public works and protect the people. His point was that a government that preys on its people destroys its own foundations.

### Idea 7: High taxes, low revenue

**The idea in one line:** A new dynasty collects a lot of revenue from low taxes; an old dynasty collects little revenue from high taxes, and the high taxes help destroy it.

Ibn Khaldun observed a pattern: **"At the beginning of the dynasty, taxation yields a large revenue from small assessments. At the end of the dynasty, taxation yields a small revenue from large assessments."**

Why? At the beginning, the rulers live simply, so taxes are low. Low taxes encourage people to work and trade; the economy grows, and total revenue is high.

As the dynasty ages, the rulers grow used to luxury and their expenses rise. They raise taxes, and add new ones. People find it less worthwhile to work. Some stop; others hide their activity. The economy shrinks, and revenue falls. The rulers, needing more money, raise taxes again, and the decline speeds up.

In 1981, US President **Ronald Reagan** cited Ibn Khaldun in defending his tax cuts. The American economist **Arthur Laffer**, whose "Laffer curve" describes the same idea, acknowledged Ibn Khaldun as a forerunner.

**Why it matters:** Every budget debate about whether lower tax rates can bring higher collections, by encouraging growth and reducing evasion, is a debate Ibn Khaldun started.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Khaldun did not say lower taxes always mean higher revenue. He described a pattern within a dynasty's life, linked to luxury, spending and the ruler's appetite. Modern economists still debate where the tipping point lies.

### Idea 8: Labour creates value

**The idea in one line:** Wealth comes from human labour; profit is the value created by people's work.

Ibn Khaldun argued that **labour** is the source of wealth. Gold and silver are only measures of value. What creates value is the work people do: farming, crafts, trade. Even in things that seem to come from nature, like crops or minerals, human labour is part of their value.

From this, he drew a striking conclusion about cities. Large, populous cities are rich because they have more labour, more division of labour, and more demand for skilled crafts. Prices, wages and luxury all rise with population. Small towns, with less labour and less specialisation, stay poor.

**Why it matters:** This anticipates the **labour theory of value** developed by Adam Smith, David Ricardo and Karl Marx. It also explains why cities like Mumbai, Bengaluru and Delhi draw millions of migrants: where labour concentrates, opportunity grows.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Khaldun did not have a full economic theory in the modern sense. But his insights, written in the fourteenth century, are remarkably close to ideas Europe developed four centuries later.

### Idea 9: The defeated imitate the victor

**The idea in one line:** Conquered peoples tend to copy the dress, language, customs and manners of those who conquered them, believing them to be superior.

Ibn Khaldun noticed that defeated people always want to imitate their conquerors. They think the victor's success must come from his customs, so they copy his dress, his speech, his habits. Or, he says, they cannot accept that they were defeated by ordinary means, so they believe the victor must be superior in everything.

He gives the example of the Muslims of Spain, who in his own time were copying the dress and customs of the Christian Galicians who were defeating them.

**Why it matters:** This is a sharp insight into colonialism and its after-effects. In 1835, the British official **Thomas Babington Macaulay** wrote that Britain should create in India "a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect." Long after independence, the prestige of English, Western dress, and fair skin in India still reflects the pattern Ibn Khaldun described.

**A common misunderstanding:** He was not saying that all borrowing from other cultures is bad. He was pointing out that the defeated often imitate not what is better, but what is powerful, and confuse the two.

### Idea 10: Harsh teaching harms students

**The idea in one line:** Teachers who rule students by severity and fear make them timid, lazy, deceitful and dishonest.

In a section on education, Ibn Khaldun argues that **severity to students does them harm**. Students who are treated harshly lose their spirit and energy. They become lazy, they learn to lie and cheat to avoid punishment, and they lose the ability to defend themselves and stand on their own. The same, he says, happens to peoples ruled by tyranny.

He also recommended teaching gradually, starting with general principles and moving step by step to details, and warned against overloading students with too many books and summaries.

**Why it matters:** Modern educational research agrees. India's **Right to Education Act (2009)** banned physical punishment and mental harassment of children in schools, a principle Ibn Khaldun argued for over six hundred years earlier.

**A common misunderstanding:** He did not reject discipline altogether. He thought teachers should guide firmly, but without humiliation and fear.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Aristotle and the philosophers

Ibn Khaldun took from the Greek philosophers the idea that humans are political by nature, and the method of seeking **causes**. His whole science of society is an attempt to find the natural causes of social events. But he criticised the philosophers' political works: they wrote about the **ideal city**, which never existed, rather than about real societies as they actually are.

### Ibn Rushd: the doubtful legacy

As a young man, Ibn Khaldun summarised Ibn Rushd's works. But in the *Muqaddimah*, he wrote a chapter "refuting philosophy". He did not attack logic, which he valued as a tool for sharpening the mind. He attacked the claim that reason alone can reach certain truth about God, the soul and the world beyond the senses. Imagine them talking:

*Ibn Rushd:* Demonstration can reach certain truth, even about the highest things. That is what Aristotle showed.

*Ibn Khaldun:* Reason is a fine balance for weighing gold, but you cannot use it to weigh mountains. Beyond experience, it cannot reach certainty.

*Ibn Rushd:* Then you leave the highest questions without answers.

*Ibn Khaldun:* I leave them to revelation. Reason should study what it can: the world of society, history and the causes we can observe.

### Machiavelli: the realist cousin

Machiavelli is not yet in this Hall, but Ibn Khaldun is often compared with **Niccolò Machiavelli** (1469–1527), who also studied power as it actually works, not as it should. Both had served rulers and seen their falls. Both saw that cohesion, discipline and fortune shape the fate of states.

### Montesquieu: climate and laws

**Montesquieu**, writing *The Spirit of the Laws* in 1748, argued that climate, geography and way of life shape the character of a people and their laws. Ibn Khaldun had made similar arguments in the *Muqaddimah*. There is no evidence Montesquieu knew his work. But the parallel shows how far ahead Ibn Khaldun was.

### Adam Smith: the division of labour

**Adam Smith**, in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), explained how the division of labour creates wealth, how labour is the source of value, and how the state should not interfere too much with trade. Ibn Khaldun had made many of the same points four centuries earlier, though Smith knew nothing of him.

### Later voices

- **Ottoman historians**, especially in the seventeenth century, read the *Muqaddimah* to understand the decline of their own empire.
- **Arnold Toynbee** praised it as the greatest work of its kind.
- **Ernest Gellner**, a twentieth-century anthropologist, built on Ibn Khaldun's ideas to explain Muslim societies.
- **Peter Turchin**, a modern scientist who uses mathematics to study the rise and fall of states, made asabiyya a central idea in his models.
- In 2015, **Mark Zuckerberg** chose the *Muqaddimah* for his online book club, bringing it to a new audience.

## See it around you

**The rise and fall of dynasties.** The Mughals came from Central Asia with tough soldiers and strong unity; **Babur**, the founder, was a descendant of **Timur**. Within two centuries came marble palaces, court factions, crushing revenue demands, and rebels on every side. The Marathas, Sikhs and others rose as tougher, more united groups. Ibn Khaldun's cycle fits closely.

**Political dynasties and parties.** In India and elsewhere, parties born in a struggle often weaken when leadership passes to heirs who never shared the struggle. A new party, born in a movement, rises with fresh asabiyya, and the cycle begins again.

**Family businesses.** The grandfather who built the business, the father who kept it, the grandson who imitated it, the great-grandson who lost it. Business families from Mumbai to Ludhiana know the pattern well, and many now hire professional managers to escape it.

**Startups and giants.** A small, hungry startup with a united team beats a giant company grown comfortable. Then the startup becomes a giant, and the cycle repeats.

**Tax debates.** Every Indian budget debate about whether lower tax rates can widen the tax base and bring in more revenue is a version of his question.

**English and fair skin.** The craze for English-medium schools, Western brands and fairness creams in post-colonial India is the vanquished imitating the victor, long after the victor has left.

**Fake news.** When a viral message claims something absurd, Ibn Khaldun's test applies: is it possible, given how the world works? Who benefits from believing it?

**City migration.** Millions move from villages to cities for work, because, as Ibn Khaldun saw, where labour and specialisation concentrate, wealth grows.

**Harsh teachers.** Every story of a child who stopped learning because of a teacher's cruelty confirms his warning that severity does students harm.

## Use it

**1. Test the story against reality.** Before you believe or forward a story, ask: could this really have happened, given how the world works? Who is telling it, and why?

**2. Watch your own asabiyya.** Your family, team or community is strong when people trust each other and share hardships. Invest in that trust. Notice when comfort, status competition or favouritism begin to weaken it.

**3. Beware of comfort.** Success brings comfort, comfort brings complacency, and complacency brings decline. Keep some of the discipline you had when you were struggling.

**4. Teach the next generation the "why".** If you build something, a business, an institution, a family tradition, don't just hand it on. Make sure the next generation understands why it matters, so they don't become imitators.

**5. Look for rising groups at the margins.** In politics, markets and organisations, change often comes from small, united groups outside the comfortable centre. Pay attention to them.

**6. Respect people's work.** If you manage people, remember that their labour is their capital. Exploitation and unfairness destroy motivation, and in the end they destroy the organisation.

**7. Ask whether you are imitating power or quality.** When you copy a style, a brand or a way of speaking, ask: is it really better, or is it just associated with power?

**8. Teach gently.** If you teach, parent or manage, remember that fear makes people timid, deceitful and lazy. Firmness without humiliation works better.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Racist generalisations.** Ibn Khaldun's theory that climate shapes character led him to offensive statements about black Africans and northern Europeans, describing peoples of extreme climates as less civilised. These views reflect the prejudices of his time, and they are a real stain on his work.

**2. Harsh words about Bedouin Arabs.** He wrote that lands conquered by the Bedouin Arabs soon fall into ruin, because they plunder rather than build. Whatever his evidence, such sweeping judgements about a whole people are dangerous, and have been misused against Arabs ever since.

**3. Too cyclical.** His theory describes a world of endless repetition, where dynasties rise and fall in the same pattern. It did not foresee how technology, institutions, constitutions and the rule of law could allow states to last far longer, and break the cycle.

**4. A political opportunist.** In his own life, he switched masters often, plotted, betrayed and flattered, including flattering Timur, a conqueror who massacred whole cities. Critics note that his clear-eyed realism about power could slip into cynicism.

**5. Women are almost absent.** His science of society says almost nothing about women, families as economic units, or how half the population lives and works. A complete science of society would need to include them.

**6. Fatalism.** If dynasties must decline, can anything be done? Some readers find his theory leaves little room for human choice or reform. Ibn Khaldun himself believed God's will stood behind the pattern, and offered rulers few remedies.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought of history as a list of kings, dates and battles, and of the rise and fall of empires as a matter of luck, heroes and villains. You may have thought sociology and economics were European inventions of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. You may never have heard of Ibn Khaldun.

**Now,** you can see a fourteenth-century North African who looked for the hidden laws behind history: solidarity that builds power, luxury that dissolves it, injustice that ruins civilisation, taxes that kill the economy that pays them, and conquered peoples who imitate their conquerors. You can test stories against the nature of things. You can see the same cycle in empires, parties, businesses and families. And you can see the limits: his prejudices, his cyclical fatalism, and the parts of society he left out.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A family business in decline** reveals the four generations: builder, keeper, imitator, destroyer.
- **A new political movement** makes you ask about its asabiyya, and how long it will last once it reaches power.
- **A viral rumour** becomes something to test against the nature of things.
- **A tax debate** brings back his warning about high rates and low revenue.
- **The prestige of English or Western brands** looks like the vanquished imitating the victor.
- **A comfortable success** carries a quiet warning: this is how decline begins.

A man who lost his parents to plague and his family to the sea, who served kings and was jailed by them, who was lowered over a city wall on a rope to meet the most feared conqueror of his age, sat in a castle in the hills and tried to find the laws behind the chaos. He found some of them, and they still work.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406), North African Arab historian, statesman and judge; father of sociology and the science of history.
- **Context:** the warring dynasties of the Maghreb; the decline of Muslim Spain; the Black Death (1348); the Mamluks in Cairo; Timur's conquests.
- **Life:** born in Tunis; parents killed by the plague; court official at twenty; prison in Fez (1357–58); ambassador to Pedro of Castile (1364); among the tribes; *Muqaddimah* written at Qal'at Ibn Salama (1377); chief judge in Cairo (from 1384); family lost at sea (1384); met Timur at Damascus (1401); died in Cairo (1406).
- **Critical history:** test reports against the nature of society.
- **Society is natural:** cooperation and the division of labour are necessary.
- **Asabiyya:** group solidarity, the force behind power.
- **Desert and city:** toughness and solidarity versus comfort and luxury.
- **Dynastic cycle:** about three generations; builder, keeper, imitator, destroyer.
- **Injustice ruins civilisation:** insecure property and forced labour destroy prosperity.
- **Taxation:** low rates, high revenue; high rates, low revenue.
- **Labour:** the source of value.
- **Imitation:** the vanquished copy the victor.
- **Education:** severity harms students.
- **Criticisms:** racist generalisations, sweeping judgements, too cyclical, opportunism, absence of women, fatalism.

**Key terms**

- **Muqaddimah:** "Introduction", the first part of his universal history, now read on its own.
- **Umran:** human social organisation, civilisation; the subject of his new science.
- **Asabiyya:** group feeling, solidarity, social cohesion.
- **Badawa:** rural, nomadic or "desert" life.
- **Hadara:** settled, urban, civilised life.
- **Mulk:** royal authority, the natural goal of asabiyya.
- **Maghreb:** north-west Africa, from Libya to Morocco.
- **Mamluks:** the dynasty of former slave-soldiers who ruled Egypt and Syria (1250–1517).
- **Laffer curve:** the modern idea that beyond a point, higher tax rates bring lower revenue.
