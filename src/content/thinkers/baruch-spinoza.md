## Start here

You are driving, and a car suddenly cuts in front of you, nearly causing an accident. You slam the brakes. Your heart pounds. You are furious. "What an idiot! Who drives like that?"

Now imagine that, a moment later, you could see everything that led to that moment. The driver got a phone call ten minutes ago: his father has collapsed and is being rushed to hospital. He hasn't slept properly in two days. He grew up in a house where nobody taught him patience. The road markings at that junction are badly faded. His car's mirror is broken.

Would you still be as angry?

Most people say: no, not as much. Something softens. The anger doesn't vanish completely, but it changes into something like understanding, even sympathy.

Now here is the radical thought. What if **everything** that anyone ever does, including everything *you* do, has causes like this, stretching back endlessly? What if every action, every feeling, every thought follows from causes as surely as the angles of a triangle follow from its shape? Then the person who cut you off was not "free" to do otherwise, in the way we usually imagine. And neither were you, when you got angry.

Does that sound depressing? A seventeenth-century Dutch philosopher thought it was the opposite. He believed that when we truly understand the causes of things, including our own emotions, we become **calmer, kinder and freer**. Not free in the sense of escaping causes, but free in the sense of no longer being pushed around by passions we don't understand. "I have laboured carefully," he wrote, "not to mock, lament, or execrate, but to understand human actions."

His name was **Baruch Spinoza**. At twenty-three, he was thrown out of his Jewish community in Amsterdam with one of the harshest curses ever pronounced. He lived quietly, grinding lenses for microscopes and telescopes, refused a professorship to protect his freedom, published his most dangerous book anonymously, and died at forty-four. He identified God with Nature, denied that God has plans or purposes, argued that the Bible was written by human beings, and demanded freedom of thought and speech for everyone. For a century after his death, his name was an insult: calling someone a "Spinozist" was like calling them an atheist.

And yet Albert Einstein, asked whether he believed in God, said he believed in **"Spinoza's God"**. Hegel said that to be a philosopher, you must first be a Spinozist. Nietzsche called him a precursor. George Eliot translated his *Ethics*. Neuroscientists today study his theory of emotions.

By the end of this page, you will understand his strange and beautiful picture of reality, why it scared his contemporaries so much, and how his idea of freedom through understanding can change how you handle anger, fear, envy and the deepest questions of life and death.

## The world he was born into

### The Jews of Amsterdam

Spinoza was born in **Amsterdam** in 1632. To understand him, you need to understand who his people were and how they got there.

His family were **Sephardic Jews**: Jews whose ancestors had lived in Spain and Portugal for many centuries. In 1492, Spain expelled all Jews who would not convert to Christianity. In 1497, Portugal forced its Jews to convert. Many became "New Christians" outwardly, while some secretly kept Jewish practices. For generations, the **Inquisition** hunted these converts, torturing and burning those suspected of secretly practising Judaism.

By the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, many of these families fled to places where they could live openly as Jews again. One of the most welcoming was the young **Dutch Republic**, which had just won its independence from Spain. Amsterdam's Portuguese Jewish community grew rich through trade and built magnificent synagogues.

But think about what this meant for the community's identity. These were people whose grandparents had been forced to live as Catholics. Many had little knowledge of Jewish law or tradition. Their leaders worked hard to rebuild a strong, orthodox Jewish identity, and to maintain good relations with the Dutch authorities, who tolerated them on the condition that they did not cause scandal. Heresy within the community was not just a religious problem; it was a threat to their fragile security.

There had already been a tragic case. **Uriel da Costa**, a member of the community, questioned the immortality of the soul and the authority of rabbinic tradition. He was excommunicated twice, publicly humiliated and whipped in the synagogue, and in 1640, when Spinoza was a boy of about eight, he took his own life. Spinoza grew up in a community that had seen what happened to dissenters.

### The Dutch Golden Age

Meanwhile, the Dutch Republic was enjoying its **Golden Age**. Amsterdam was the richest trading city in the world. Dutch ships sailed to Asia and the Americas. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) traded in spices, textiles and, in India, had posts on the Coromandel and Malabar coasts. Painters like Rembrandt and Vermeer were at work. Science flourished: Christiaan Huygens studied light and invented the pendulum clock; Antonie van Leeuwenhoek looked through microscopes and discovered microorganisms.

The Republic was the most tolerant country in Europe. Books banned elsewhere were printed in Amsterdam. Descartes lived there for twenty years. But tolerance had limits. The official Calvinist church was powerful, and its preachers demanded that "blasphemous" books be banned and their authors punished. Politics was divided between a more tolerant republican party, led in Spinoza's time by **Johan de Witt**, and a party loyal to the House of Orange, which allied with strict Calvinist preachers.

So Spinoza lived in the freest place in Europe, and even there, freedom was fragile.

### The new philosophy

And intellectually, his world was being transformed by **Descartes**. Descartes' new philosophy, with its mathematical method, its mechanical view of nature and its sharp division of mind and matter, was the most exciting and controversial thought of the age. Young intellectuals in the Netherlands debated it furiously. Spinoza would learn from Descartes, and then go much further than Descartes ever dared.

## The life, as a story

### A merchant's son

Baruch de Spinoza was born on 24 November 1632. His name *Baruch* means "blessed" in Hebrew; in Portuguese he was Bento, in Latin, Benedictus. His father, Michael, was a respected merchant who imported dried fruit and other goods, and an official of the synagogue. His mother died when he was six.

Baruch attended the community's school, studying Hebrew, the Bible and Jewish texts. He was clearly brilliant. He probably did not go on to advanced rabbinical studies, because he was needed in the family business. After his father died in 1654, Baruch and his brother Gabriel ran the firm, which was struggling with debts.

### Forbidden books

At the same time, Spinoza was reading things he was not supposed to. He learned **Latin**, the language of philosophy and science, from **Franciscus van den Enden**, a former Jesuit turned radical freethinker, who ran a school in Amsterdam. Through Latin, Spinoza read Descartes and the new science. He mixed with Christian dissenters, freethinkers and merchants who met to discuss religion without priests.

He began to doubt. Did God really give the Torah word for word to Moses? Is the soul immortal? Does God really intervene in history, choose one people, perform miracles? He seems to have expressed these doubts to people in the community.

### The curse

On **27 July 1656**, when Spinoza was twenty-three, the leaders of the Talmud Torah congregation pronounced a ***cherem***, a writ of expulsion, against him. It was read aloud in the synagogue. Its language was extraordinarily harsh, much harsher than usual. It said that the leaders had long known of his "evil opinions and acts", his "abominable heresies" and "monstrous deeds", and had tried to turn him from his ways without success. Then it cursed him:

Cursed be he by day and cursed be he by night; cursed be he when he lies down and cursed be he when he rises up; cursed be he when he goes out and cursed be he when he comes in.

It forbade any member of the community to speak with him, write to him, do him any favour, stay under the same roof with him, come within four cubits of him, or read anything he had written.

Strangely, the *cherem* never specified which heresies. But from Spinoza's later writings, we can guess: he probably denied that the soul is immortal, that the Torah came directly from God, and that God is a lawgiver who chooses peoples and judges them.

There is a story, told later, that around this time someone attacked him with a knife as he left a theatre, and that he kept his torn cloak as a reminder. It may be a legend. But it reflects how dangerous his position was.

Spinoza did not convert to Christianity, as many expelled Jews did. He joined no religious community at all. He became, in a sense, one of the first people in modern European history to live outside every religious community.

### The lens-grinder

He left Amsterdam and lived quietly in small towns: first **Rijnsburg**, near Leiden, then **Voorburg**, near The Hague, and finally **The Hague** itself. He earned his living partly by **grinding optical lenses** for microscopes and telescopes, a highly skilled craft. Leading scientists such as Christiaan Huygens respected his lenses. He also received modest support from friends who admired him.

He lived very simply. He rented rooms from ordinary families, ate plain food, dressed modestly, and spent his days grinding lenses, writing and corresponding. His landlord's family in The Hague, the painter Hendrik van der Spyck and his wife, later remembered him as gentle, quiet, kind to their children and calm in all circumstances.

His personal seal bore a single Latin word, with a rose: ***Caute***, "Cautiously". It was a fitting motto for a man whose ideas could get him killed.

### Friends, letters and a circle of admirers

Though he lived quietly, Spinoza was not isolated. A circle of friends in Amsterdam met to read and discuss his drafts. He corresponded with scientists and philosophers across Europe, including **Henry Oldenburg**, secretary of the Royal Society in London. His letters are full of patient, careful arguments, and sometimes sharp ones.

In 1663 he published his first book, an exposition of **Descartes' *Principles of Philosophy*** in geometrical form, with an appendix of his own thoughts. It was the only book published under his own name in his lifetime.

### The dangerous book

In 1670, he published the ***Theological-Political Treatise***. It was anonymous, with a false place of publication ("Hamburg") on the title page. It argued that the Bible should be studied like any other historical text, that Moses did not write the first five books of the Bible, that prophets were people with vivid imaginations rather than special knowledge, that miracles are only events whose natural causes we don't understand, and that the state must allow freedom of thought and speech.

The reaction was explosive. One critic called it a book "forged in hell". Theologians across Europe attacked it. Its authorship soon became an open secret. In 1674, the Dutch authorities officially banned it.

### The terrible year

In 1672, the Dutch Republic was invaded by France and its allies, a year the Dutch still call the ***Rampjaar***, the "disaster year". In the panic, a mob in The Hague seized the republican leader **Johan de Witt**, Spinoza's political ally, and his brother Cornelis, and brutally murdered them, mutilating their bodies in the street.

Spinoza, normally so calm, was devastated. According to an account Leibniz later recorded, Spinoza wanted to put up a placard near the scene reading ***Ultimi barbarorum***, "the lowest of barbarians". His landlord locked the door to stop him, knowing the mob would have torn him apart too.

Even the philosopher who taught understanding over outrage felt the urge to cry out.

### Saying no to a professorship

In 1673, the **Elector Palatine** offered Spinoza a professorship of philosophy at the University of Heidelberg, one of Germany's great universities. The offer promised him "the most ample freedom of philosophising", with one condition: that he would not misuse it to disturb the publicly established religion.

Spinoza thought carefully and politely declined. He wrote that he did not know within what limits that freedom would have to be kept, and that teaching young students would take time from his own philosophy. He preferred his freedom and his lenses.

### The visitor

In November 1676, a brilliant young German philosopher came to The Hague and spent several days talking with Spinoza: **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz**. Leibniz was fascinated by Spinoza's ideas and alarmed by them. He would spend the rest of his life developing a philosophy that tried to avoid Spinoza's conclusions.

### The early death

Spinoza had long suffered from a lung illness, possibly tuberculosis, which may have been worsened by breathing glass dust from lens-grinding. On **21 February 1677**, he died in The Hague, aged forty-four.

His friends had his manuscripts sent to Amsterdam and, later that year, published his ***Opera Posthuma*** (posthumous works), including his masterpiece, the ***Ethics***, which he had worked on for over fifteen years but never dared to publish. The book was immediately banned.

## The big ideas, one by one

### A word about the *Ethics*

Before we dive in, you need to know how strange Spinoza's main book looks. The *Ethics* is written like a geometry textbook, in the style of Euclid. It begins with **definitions** and **axioms**, then states **propositions**, each followed by a **proof** that refers back to earlier definitions, axioms and propositions. Occasionally there are "scholia", explanatory notes where Spinoza speaks more freely.

Why write about God, emotions and freedom like a geometry book? Because Spinoza believed that everything in reality follows from God's nature as necessarily as the properties of a triangle follow from its definition. If that is true, then the right way to explain reality is the way you explain a triangle: step by step, with proofs. And he wanted to discuss human emotions without moralising, "as though I were concerned with lines, planes, and solids".

You don't need to follow the proofs to understand his main ideas. Let us go through them.

### Idea 1: God or Nature

**The idea in one line:** There is only one substance, infinite and eternal, and it can be called either God or Nature; everything that exists is a part or expression of it.

Descartes had said there are two kinds of created substance, mind and matter, plus God, who created them. Spinoza asked: what does "substance" actually mean? Something that exists in itself and is understood through itself, that depends on nothing else.

But then, he argued, only **one** thing can truly be a substance: the whole of reality, which depends on nothing outside itself. Everything else, you, me, this table, a thought, a star, depends on something else and is therefore not a separate substance, but a **mode**: a particular way in which the one substance expresses itself.

He called this one substance **God**. And he also called it **Nature**. His famous phrase is ***Deus sive Natura***: "God, or Nature".

**An example.** Think of the ocean and its waves. Each wave has its own shape, size and movement. It rises, travels and falls. But no wave is separate from the ocean. A wave is just the ocean, waving in a particular way, at a particular place and time. In Spinoza's picture, you and I are like waves, and God or Nature is like the ocean. (No analogy is perfect, but this one helps.)

**Not a person in the sky.** This is crucial. Spinoza's God is not a person who sits in heaven, has feelings, makes plans, gives commandments, answers prayers, rewards and punishes. It is the infinite, eternal, necessary reality of which everything is a part. That is why many readers called him an atheist, and others a **pantheist** (someone who believes God is everything). Spinoza himself would say he simply took the idea of an infinite God more seriously than anyone else: if God is truly infinite, nothing can exist outside God.

**The Indian parallel.** Readers from India often notice a resemblance to Indian ideas. The Upanishads speak of **Brahman** as the one reality underlying everything: *sarvam khalv idam brahma*, "all this is indeed Brahman" (Chandogya Upanishad). The philosopher **Ramanuja** described the world and souls as the **body of God**. In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna is shown Krishna's cosmic form, in which the entire universe is contained.

But be careful. These traditions are devotional: Brahman or Krishna can be known, loved and worshipped, and in many forms responds to devotion. Spinoza's God does not respond. It does not love you back in the way a person would. The resemblance is real, but so is the difference.

**Why it matters.** This was one of the most radical ideas in the history of Western thought. It meant that there is no gap between the natural world and the divine. To study nature, through physics, biology or psychology, is to study God. Science and spirituality, for Spinoza, are not enemies.

### Idea 2: God has no purposes, and nature is not made for us

**The idea in one line:** God does not act for any goal, and the idea that nature was designed for human benefit is a projection of our own desires.

At the end of Part I of the *Ethics*, Spinoza writes a brilliant, angry appendix attacking what he calls the most common human prejudice: the belief that everything in nature acts, like humans, for a purpose, and that God made everything for humans, and humans to worship him.

He explains how this prejudice arises. People are born ignorant of the causes of things. They are conscious of their own desires and act for ends, like seeking food or comfort. When they find useful things in nature, eyes for seeing, teeth for chewing, plants and animals for food, the sun for light, they assume someone made these things for them. And since they don't know who, they imagine a ruler of nature, like a human king, who made everything for their use.

Then a problem arises: earthquakes, storms and diseases. Why would God send these? People conclude that the gods must be angry about human sins. And when the disasters strike good and bad people alike, they say God's judgements are beyond human understanding. Spinoza says that this way of thinking would have kept the human race in darkness forever, if **mathematics**, which deals not with purposes but with the essences and properties of figures, had not shown us another standard of truth.

He has a memorable phrase. When people can't explain something, he says, they keep asking "why?" until they take refuge in the will of God, "**the sanctuary of ignorance**".

**An example.** A roof tile falls and kills a man walking below. People ask: why did God want this man to die? Spinoza would say: the wind was blowing, the tile was loose, the man happened to walk there at that moment because he had been invited to a friend's house. Every cause has a cause. There is no cosmic plan aimed at this man. Saying "it was God's will" stops the inquiry; asking about causes continues it.

**Why it matters.** This was a direct attack on a central idea of traditional religion, and of Aristotle's physics: that nature acts for purposes. It aligned Spinoza with the new mechanical science. It also has psychological consequences. If the universe was not designed around you, then misfortunes are not personal punishments. Many people find this frightening. Spinoza found it liberating.

### Idea 3: Free will is an illusion

**The idea in one line:** Everything, including every human thought and action, follows necessarily from causes; we feel free only because we are aware of our desires but ignorant of their causes.

This is Spinoza's most challenging idea for most readers. He wrote in a letter (in Elwes' translation) that if a stone, thrown into the air and continuing to move, could think and be conscious, it would believe itself completely free and think that it continued to move solely because it wished to. And that, he says, "is that human freedom which all boast that they possess, and which consists solely in the fact, that men are conscious of their own desire, but are ignorant of the causes whereby that desire has been determined."

He gives examples. A baby believes it freely wants milk. An angry boy believes he freely wants revenge. A drunk believes he speaks freely, but when sober wishes he had kept quiet. They all think they are free, because they know what they want, but not *why* they want it.

**An example.** Think of a choice you made today: chai or coffee, this shirt or that one, replying to this message first. It felt free. But why did you want chai? Habit from childhood, the weather, how tired you are, what your friend was drinking, a memory, your body chemistry. You weren't aware of all these causes, so the choice felt uncaused. Spinoza says that feeling of freedom is just the shadow of our ignorance.

**Why it matters.** If Spinoza is right, then blame, praise, anger and resentment need to be rethought. The person who wronged you could not have done otherwise, given all the causes acting on them. That doesn't mean society shouldn't protect itself, Spinoza says we still need laws and punishments, just as we keep a mad dog away even though it isn't to blame. But it does mean that **hatred** makes no sense. Understanding replaces blame.

**A common misunderstanding.** This does not mean "nothing matters" or "I might as well do nothing". Your thinking, effort and understanding are themselves causes. If you study, you will understand more; if you understand more, you will act better. Determinism does not mean your actions make no difference. It means they make a difference *as causes in a chain*, not as uncaused miracles.

### Idea 4: Mind and body are one thing seen two ways

**The idea in one line:** Mind and body are not two substances that interact; they are the same thing understood through two different "attributes", thought and extension.

Descartes could not explain how a non-physical mind moves a physical body. Spinoza's solution was elegant: they don't interact, because they are not two things. God or Nature has infinitely many **attributes**, ways of being understood, of which we know two: **thought** and **extension** (physical space). Every event can be described in both ways. Your decision to raise your arm and the physical movement of your arm are **one event**, described once in the language of mind and once in the language of body.

He wrote that "the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things". In other words, the mental and the physical run in perfect parallel, because they are the same reality.

**An example.** Think of a song on a music app. You can describe it as music, with melody, rhythm and lyrics. Or you can describe it as data: patterns of numbers stored and processed in the phone. These are not two different songs, one musical and one digital, somehow interacting. They are one song, described two ways. (Again, no analogy is perfect.)

**Why it matters.** Many philosophers and scientists today find Spinoza's view surprisingly modern. Neuroscience shows that every mental event corresponds to brain activity. Spinoza's "two aspects of one thing" is one way of thinking about this without either reducing the mind to "just" physics or treating it as a ghost. The neuroscientist **Antonio Damasio** wrote a whole book, *Looking for Spinoza* (2003), arguing that Spinoza anticipated modern understanding of how emotions and the body are bound together.

### Idea 5: Every being strives to persist

**The idea in one line:** The essence of every thing is its striving to keep existing and to increase its power; Spinoza calls this ***conatus***.

"Everything, in so far as it is in itself, endeavours to persist in its own being." A stone resists being broken. A plant grows towards light. An animal flees danger and seeks food. A human being strives to live, to understand, to flourish. This striving, *conatus*, is not something a thing *has*; it is what the thing *is*.

In humans, when this striving relates to the mind and body together, Spinoza calls it **appetite**, and when we are conscious of it, **desire**. "Desire is the very essence of man."

### Idea 6: Emotions as changes in our power

**The idea in one line:** Joy is the feeling of our power increasing; sadness is the feeling of our power decreasing; all other emotions are combinations of these with desire.

Spinoza built an entire theory of emotions from just three basic ones:

- **Desire:** our striving itself.
- **Joy** (or pleasure): the passage to a greater power of acting.
- **Sadness** (or pain): the passage to a lesser power of acting.

All other emotions are built from these, combined with ideas of their causes:

- **Love** is joy accompanied by the idea of an external cause. ("I feel joy, and I think *you* are the cause.")
- **Hatred** is sadness accompanied by the idea of an external cause.
- **Hope** is an inconstant joy arising from the idea of something future or past whose outcome we doubt.
- **Fear** is an inconstant sadness arising from the same uncertainty.
- **Envy** is hatred that makes us sad at another's happiness and glad at their misfortune.
- **Pity** is sadness accompanied by the idea of harm to someone we consider like ourselves.

He goes on to analyse dozens of emotions this way, precisely and without moral judgement, "as though I were concerned with lines, planes, and solids".

**An example.** Think about envy on social media. You see a friend's holiday photos and feel a twinge of sadness: your sense of your own life's worth decreases. You connect that sadness to your friend as its cause, and something like hostility arises. Spinoza's analysis helps you see the mechanism: the sadness is a feeling about *your* power, and your friend is not really its cause. Seeing this clearly weakens the envy.

**Why it matters.** Spinoza was one of the first thinkers to treat emotions as natural phenomena to be understood, rather than sins to be condemned or forces from outside. This is close to the spirit of modern psychology.

### Idea 7: Bondage and freedom

**The idea in one line:** We are in bondage when we are driven by passions we don't understand; we become free when we understand the causes of our emotions and act from reason.

Part IV of the *Ethics* is called "Of Human Bondage". Spinoza explains: "Human infirmity in moderating and checking the emotions I name bondage: for, when a man is a prey to his emotions, he is not his own master."

Notice: bondage is not about chains on the body. It is about being pushed around by **passions**: emotions caused by external things we don't understand. The jealous lover, the greedy businessman, the person ruled by fear of others' opinions: all are, in Spinoza's sense, slaves.

How do we become free? Not by suppressing emotions through willpower, which Spinoza thought doesn't work. The key is **understanding**. "An emotion, which is a passion, ceases to be a passion, as soon as we form a clear and distinct idea thereof."

**An example.** Imagine you get anxious every time your phone buzzes with a message from your boss. That anxiety is a passion: you are passive, pushed around by an external cause. Now you sit down and understand it: past experiences of harsh criticism, uncertainty about your job, the way your body reacts to uncertainty, the fact that most messages are routine. As you understand it more clearly, the anxiety loses some of its grip. You are becoming active rather than passive, in Spinoza's terms. You're not free of all causes, but you are now acting from your own understanding.

**This is close to modern therapy.** Much of modern psychotherapy works on exactly this principle: understanding the sources of our emotions changes our relationship with them. Spinoza got there in the 1670s.

### Idea 8: Hatred is overcome by love

**The idea in one line:** Returning hatred with hatred makes it grow; returning it with love can dissolve it.

"Hatred is increased by being reciprocated, and can on the other hand be destroyed by love." Spinoza also argued that a person who lives by the guidance of reason will try, as far as they can, to repay another's hatred, anger and contempt with love and generosity.

**The parallels.** Nearly two thousand years earlier in India, the *Dhammapada* recorded the Buddha's teaching: hatred does not cease by hatred at any time; hatred ceases by love; this is an old rule. And the Sermon on the Mount says to love your enemies. Spinoza arrived at the same conclusion, not from scripture or revelation, but from his geometrical analysis of emotions: hatred is sadness linked to a cause; reciprocated hatred adds more sadness; love is joy linked to a cause, and joy increases our power. So love is not only morally better; it is literally more empowering.

**Why it matters.** Think of family feuds that last generations, neighbourhood disputes, communal tensions, political polarisation. Each side responds to the other's hostility with more hostility, and the hatred grows. Spinoza explains why, and points to the only real exit.

### Idea 9: Think about life, not death

**The idea in one line:** A free person is focused on living well, not on fear of death.

"A free man thinks of death least of all things; and his wisdom is a meditation not of death but of life."

This is a striking contrast with many traditions, including the Stoics, who recommended frequent meditation on death. Spinoza's point is that fear of death is a passion, driven by imagination and sadness. A person guided by reason focuses on what increases their power of living: understanding, friendship, joy, doing good.

**An example.** Two people are diagnosed with a serious illness. One spends their remaining time terrified, thinking constantly about death. The other spends it with family, finishing meaningful work, laughing, learning. Spinoza would say the second has understood something essential.

### Idea 10: The intellectual love of God

**The idea in one line:** The highest happiness is to understand things as necessary parts of the eternal whole, and this understanding brings a calm, profound joy.

At the end of the *Ethics*, Spinoza describes the highest state a human can reach. When we understand things not just in time, as passing events, but **"under the aspect of eternity"** (*sub specie aeternitatis*), as necessary expressions of the eternal nature of God or Nature, we experience a deep, steady joy. He calls this the ***intellectual love of God***. It is not a feeling of being loved by a heavenly father; it is the joy of understanding our place in the infinite order of things.

And he says that in this state, the mind finds true peace of mind (*acquiescentia*), which no passion can disturb.

Then the famous ending: if the way to this freedom seems very difficult, it can still be found. It must be hard, since it is so rarely found. "But all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare."

### Idea 11: The Bible is a human book

**The idea in one line:** Scripture should be studied historically, like any other text, and its true message is about justice and charity, not philosophy or science.

In the *Theological-Political Treatise*, Spinoza applied careful historical and linguistic analysis to the Bible. He argued, among other things, that:

- **Moses did not write the first five books of the Bible** as they stand; they were compiled much later, probably by Ezra.
- **Prophets** were not people with special knowledge of nature or God's essence, but people with vivid imaginations and strong moral sense. Their messages were adapted to their audiences.
- **Miracles**, understood as violations of the laws of nature, are impossible, because the laws of nature are God's own nature. What people call miracles are events whose natural causes they do not understand.
- The core message of Scripture is simple: **love God and love your neighbour**, practise justice and charity. Scripture aims at obedience and good conduct, not at teaching philosophy. So faith and philosophy should be kept separate: philosophy seeks truth; faith seeks obedience and piety.

**Why it matters.** This was one of the founding texts of modern **biblical criticism**, the historical study of scripture. It was also a defence of reason's independence from religious authority. Spinoza wanted to free philosophy from theologians and to protect religion from being turned into a weapon of persecution.

### Idea 12: Freedom of thought and the free state

**The idea in one line:** A state should allow every person to think what they like and say what they think; the true aim of government is liberty.

The final chapter of the *Theological-Political Treatise* has a title that is itself a manifesto: "That in a Free State every man may think what he likes, and say what he thinks."

Spinoza argued that it is impossible to control people's minds. Laws against opinions don't change what people believe; they only make people hypocrites, and turn honest people into rebels. And they damage the state, because a society where people cannot speak freely loses the benefit of their ideas, and fills with flatterers and liars.

"In fact," he wrote, "the true aim of government is liberty." The state exists not to turn people into beasts or puppets, but to let them develop their minds and bodies in security and use their reason freely.

He also argued that **democracy** is the most natural form of government, the one closest to the freedom people have in nature, because in it everyone has a share in power and no one transfers their rights so completely that they lose all say.

**Why it matters.** These ideas, written over a century before the American and French revolutions, made Spinoza one of the founders of modern liberal democracy and secularism. Every constitution that protects freedom of speech, of religion and of conscience, including India's, with its guarantees of freedom of speech and expression and freedom of conscience, stands in a tradition Spinoza helped build.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Descartes: the teacher he went beyond

**Descartes** gave Spinoza his method: start from clear, certain ideas, proceed by rigorous reasoning. Spinoza's first book explained Descartes' philosophy. But then Spinoza took Descartes' principles to conclusions Descartes never accepted. Descartes kept a personal God who creates freely; Spinoza made God identical with Nature and necessity. Descartes had two substances, mind and matter; Spinoza had one. Descartes believed in free will; Spinoza denied it.

You could say Spinoza was more Cartesian than Descartes, pushing his logic all the way, and in doing so he destroyed much of what Descartes cared about.

### Leibniz: the anxious admirer

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** visited Spinoza in 1676 and studied his manuscripts. He was deeply impressed and deeply troubled. If Spinoza was right, then God has no free choice, there are no purposes in nature, and this world is simply necessary. Leibniz spent years constructing an alternative: a God who freely chooses the **best of all possible worlds**, many distinct substances (monads) rather than one, and real purposes. Some scholars think much of Leibniz's mature philosophy was shaped as a reaction to Spinoza. Publicly, Leibniz distanced himself, sometimes harshly; privately, he engaged with Spinoza closely.

### The Stoics: ancient cousins

Spinoza's ethics has often been compared to **Stoicism**. Like **Epictetus**, he taught that we suffer because of how we relate to things, that understanding necessity brings peace, and that the passions can be calmed by reason. Both rejected the idea that external goods determine our happiness. But Spinoza was not a Stoic: he rejected their belief that the will can directly control the passions, and he placed joy and the increase of power at the centre of his ethics, where the Stoics placed virtue and calm.

### The Buddha: love and the illusion of the separate self

The parallel between Spinoza's "hatred is destroyed by love" and the Buddha's teaching in the *Dhammapada* is striking. There is another parallel: both thinkers saw ordinary human life as bondage to forces we don't understand (craving for the Buddha; passions for Spinoza), and both saw the way out in clear understanding. Both also challenged the idea of a separate, independent self: for the Buddha, there is no permanent self; for Spinoza, we are modes of one substance, not independent substances. But the Buddha's goal is liberation from rebirth; Spinoza's is joyful understanding within nature.

### Hegel, Marx and the German explosion

For about a century after his death, Spinoza was mostly denounced. Then, in the **1780s**, a public controversy broke out in Germany when it was revealed that the great writer **Gotthold Lessing** had privately admitted to being a Spinozist. This "**Pantheism Controversy**" made Spinoza suddenly central to German thought. The poets Goethe and Novalis admired him; Novalis called him "a God-intoxicated man".

**Hegel** said, in his lectures, that one is either a Spinozist or not a philosopher at all. But Hegel also criticised Spinoza: his substance was too static, a frozen ocean. Hegel wanted reality to be not just substance but **subject**: living, developing Spirit that comes to know itself through history.

The young **Karl Marx**, in 1841, copied out long extracts from Spinoza's *Theological-Political Treatise* in his notebooks, focusing on religion, the state and freedom. Spinoza's materialism, his critique of religion and his political realism fed into the radical tradition that Marx developed.

### Nietzsche: "a precursor, and what a precursor!"

In 1881, **Friedrich Nietzsche** wrote an excited postcard to his friend Franz Overbeck. He had discovered that he had a precursor: Spinoza. Like Nietzsche, Spinoza denied free will, denied purposes in nature, denied a moral world order, denied that good and evil exist in nature itself, and made the drive to increase power central. Nietzsche disagreed with much else in Spinoza, especially his faith in reason, but he felt a deep kinship.

### Einstein: "Spinoza's God"

In 1929, a New York rabbi sent **Albert Einstein** a telegram asking directly: "Do you believe in God?" Einstein replied that he believed in "Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the orderly harmony of what exists, not in a God who concerns himself with fates and actions of human beings."

Einstein also shared Spinoza's determinism. In 1930, at his summer house in Caputh, near Berlin, he had a famous conversation with **Rabindranath Tagore** about the nature of reality and truth. Tagore suggested that truth is related to human consciousness; Einstein held that truth exists independently of humans. Spinoza's spirit was in that room.

### Modern readers

**George Eliot**, the English novelist, translated the *Ethics* into English in the 1850s. Twentieth-century philosophers such as **Gilles Deleuze** saw Spinoza as a philosopher of joy and power. Political theorists see him as a founder of radical democracy. And neuroscientists like Damasio see him as a pioneer of the science of emotion.

## See it around you

**In anger management.** The idea that understanding the causes of someone's behaviour reduces anger is the heart of many conflict-resolution techniques. The driver who cut you off had causes. So did your reaction.

**In therapy.** Modern psychotherapy's basic insight, that understanding the sources of our emotions changes their power over us, is Spinoza's freedom through understanding.

**In neuroscience.** Research showing that mind and brain activity are inseparable fits Spinoza's view of mind and body as one thing seen two ways.

**In science and religion debates.** Whenever someone says that studying nature is a way of encountering the sacred, or that the universe's order inspires awe without a personal God, they are close to Spinoza. Einstein's famous religiosity was Spinozist.

**In freedom of speech.** Every debate about censorship, blasphemy laws, banning books or silencing critics replays Spinoza's argument: you cannot control minds by force; you only create hypocrites and rebels.

**In secularism.** The idea that religion should not control the state, and that the state should protect freedom of belief, owes a great deal to the *Theological-Political Treatise*.

**In ecology.** Some environmental philosophers, especially the Norwegian Arne Naess, founder of "deep ecology", drew on Spinoza's idea that humans are part of nature, not its masters, and that nature was not created for human use.

**In grief and misfortune.** When something terrible happens, people often ask, "Why me? What did I do to deserve this?" Spinoza's answer, that misfortunes are not personal messages but the working of countless natural causes, can feel cold. It can also be a relief from guilt and the sense of being punished.

**In envy and social media.** Spinoza's analysis of envy, sadness at another's good linked to them as cause, describes exactly what many people feel scrolling through other people's highlights.

## Use it

**1. Look for the causes.** When someone upsets you, pause and ask: what causes might have led them to act this way? You don't have to excuse the behaviour, but understanding often turns anger into something calmer.

**2. Understand your own emotions.** When a strong emotion arises, write down: What am I feeling? What do I think caused it? What older experiences might be involved? Spinoza says the clearer this understanding, the weaker the passion's grip.

**3. Track joy and sadness.** Notice which people, activities and habits increase your sense of power and joy, and which decrease it. Spend more time with the first.

**4. Answer hatred with something else.** The next time someone is hostile to you, try responding with calm and generosity instead of hostility. Notice whether the conflict grows or shrinks.

**5. Meditate on life.** Instead of dwelling on fear of death, illness or failure, focus on what you can do today to live more fully: learn something, help someone, enjoy something.

**6. Don't mock, lament or curse: understand.** When reading the news about something terrible, try Spinoza's approach first: what were the causes? This doesn't mean accepting injustice; it means responding with clarity rather than only outrage.

**7. Defend others' freedom to think.** When you disagree with someone's opinion, remember Spinoza's argument: banning opinions doesn't change minds. Argue with ideas; don't try to silence people.

**8. See yourself as part of the whole.** Occasionally, step back and see yourself as one small wave in a vast ocean, connected to everything. Many people find this both humbling and calming.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Is determinism true?** Spinoza was certain that everything follows necessarily from God's nature. Modern physics, especially quantum mechanics, suggests that at the smallest scales, nature may involve genuine randomness. (Einstein, as a Spinozist, resisted this, famously saying God does not play dice.) And many philosophers argue that human freedom is real in some sense, even if our choices have causes.

**2. Moral responsibility.** If no one could have done otherwise, how can we hold people responsible? Spinoza says we can still protect society from harmful people, but many feel that something important about blame, guilt, apology and forgiveness is lost.

**3. A God no one can pray to.** For many religious people, Spinoza's God, without will, purpose, love or response, is not God at all. Is a God who is identical with Nature still worth calling God? Many believers and many atheists agree: Spinoza's "God" is just Nature with a grander name.

**4. The geometric method can be an illusion of certainty.** Spinoza's definitions and axioms are not as self-evident as Euclid's. If you reject his definition of substance at the start, much of the rest collapses. The geometrical form makes his conclusions look more certain than they are.

**5. Reason may be weaker than he thought.** Spinoza believed clear understanding could transform passions. Modern psychology suggests that understanding helps, but that emotions are often stubborn, rooted in biology, trauma and habit, and that insight alone is rarely enough.

**6. Women and the limits of his democracy.** In his unfinished *Political Treatise*, Spinoza argued that women should be excluded from government, claiming they were naturally weaker. His democratic vision, radical for its time, also excluded servants and others. Even a thinker who championed freedom for all could not see past some prejudices of his age.

**7. Elitism of reason.** Spinoza suggested that most people would never reach the life of reason and would need religion's simple moral teachings to behave well. Critics find this condescending: true freedom for the few philosophers, obedience for the many.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you probably thought of God and nature as separate, of your choices as completely free, and of your anger, fear and envy as natural responses to what others do to you. You may also have thought that freedom means doing whatever you want.

**Now,** you can see a different picture: everything, including you, as part of one vast, connected reality; every action, including your own, as the result of countless causes; and freedom not as escape from causes but as understanding them so well that you are no longer pushed around by passions.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **The driver who cuts you off** becomes a person with a history of causes, and your anger loosens.
- **Your own anger** becomes something you can study, like a scientist, rather than something that controls you.
- **Envy at a friend's success** reveals itself as sadness about your own sense of power, which you can work on directly.
- **A disaster or misfortune** stops looking like a personal message from the universe. It becomes the meeting of many causes, which takes away some of the guilt and the terror.
- **Someone's hostility** becomes a test: will you add more hatred, or try to dissolve it?
- **An opinion you hate** becomes something to argue with, not something to ban.
- **Nature** stops looking like a backdrop for human life. It becomes the infinite whole of which you are a small, connected part.

Spinoza lived in a rented room, ground lenses so others could see more clearly, and died young, with his greatest work still unpublished. His lenses are lost. His other lens, the one that makes the causes of things visible, is still being used.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Baruch (Benedictus) de Spinoza (1632–1677), from Amsterdam's Portuguese Jewish community; expelled by a *cherem* in 1656; lens-grinder and philosopher in Rijnsburg, Voorburg and The Hague.
- **Works:** an exposition of Descartes (1663); the *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670, anonymous); the *Ethics* and *Political Treatise* (published after his death, 1677).
- **God or Nature:** only one infinite substance exists; everything is a mode of it; God has no purposes and is not a person.
- **Determinism:** everything follows necessarily from causes; free will is the feeling of knowing our desires but not their causes.
- **Mind and body:** two attributes of one reality, not two interacting substances.
- **Emotions:** desire, joy and sadness are the basic emotions; joy is increase of power, sadness decrease.
- **Freedom:** bondage is being ruled by passions we don't understand; freedom comes through clear understanding; hatred is overcome by love; the free person thinks of life, not death; the highest joy is the intellectual love of God.
- **Politics and religion:** the Bible is a human, historical text; the true aim of government is liberty; everyone may think what they like and say what they think.
- **Legacy:** influenced the Enlightenment, Hegel, Marx, Nietzsche, Einstein and modern neuroscience; criticised for determinism, an impersonal God, and the exclusion of women from his politics.

**Key terms**

- **Deus sive Natura:** "God, or Nature", Spinoza's identification of God with the whole of reality.
- **Substance:** that which exists in itself and depends on nothing else; for Spinoza, only God.
- **Mode:** a particular expression or modification of substance, such as a person or a thought.
- **Attribute:** a way of understanding substance; we know thought and extension.
- **Conatus:** the striving of each thing to persist in its being.
- **Passion:** an emotion caused by external things we don't understand; makes us passive.
- **Bondage:** being ruled by passions.
- **Sub specie aeternitatis:** "under the aspect of eternity", seeing things as necessary parts of the eternal whole.
- **Intellectual love of God:** the joy of understanding everything as an expression of God or Nature.
- **Cherem:** a writ of expulsion from the Jewish community.
