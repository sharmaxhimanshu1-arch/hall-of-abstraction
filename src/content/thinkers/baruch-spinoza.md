## Life

Baruch (later Benedictus) de Spinoza was born in Amsterdam in 1632 into a family of Portuguese Jewish merchants whose ancestors had fled the Inquisition. He received a traditional Jewish education and worked in the family import business. But he was also learning Latin and reading the new philosophy of Descartes, partly under the tutelage of Franciscus van den Enden, a freethinking former Jesuit.

In July 1656, at twenty-three, Spinoza was placed under *cherem*, a writ of expulsion, by Amsterdam's Portuguese Jewish congregation. Its language was unusually severe, condemning his "abominable heresies" and "monstrous deeds", though it did not specify them. He apparently already doubted the immortality of the soul, the divine origin of the Torah and a God who intervenes in history. He never joined another religious community.

Spinoza supported himself by grinding lenses for microscopes and telescopes, work that earned him respect among scientists such as Christiaan Huygens, and he lived modestly in Rijnsburg, Voorburg and finally The Hague. He corresponded with leading thinkers including Henry Oldenburg of the Royal Society, and Leibniz visited him in 1676. In 1670 he anonymously published the *Theological-Political Treatise*, which was soon banned and denounced across Europe as a work forged "in hell". In 1673 he declined a professorship at Heidelberg, fearing for his freedom to philosophise. He died in 1677, aged forty-four, of a lung disease possibly worsened by glass dust. His friends published his *Ethics* later that year.

## Key ideas

**God or Nature.** In the *Ethics*, set out like Euclid's geometry with definitions, axioms and propositions, Spinoza argues that there can be only one substance, infinite and self-caused, and he calls it "God or Nature" (*Deus sive Natura*). Everything that exists is a "mode", a way in which this one substance expresses itself. God is not a person who plans or judges, but the infinite, necessary order of reality itself.

**Mind and body as one.** Thought and extension are not two substances, as Descartes held, but two attributes of the one substance, two ways of understanding the same reality. The human mind and body are one thing seen under different aspects, which dissolves Descartes' problem of how they interact.

**Determinism.** Everything follows necessarily from the nature of God, as the properties of a triangle follow from its definition. There is no free will in the sense of uncaused choice. People believe themselves free "simply because they are conscious of their actions, and unconscious of the causes whereby those actions are determined."

**Emotions and freedom.** Spinoza analysed the emotions with scientific detachment, "as though I were concerned with lines, planes, and solids". Passive emotions arise when we are pushed about by external causes we do not understand. By understanding their causes we turn passions into actions and gain true freedom, which is life according to reason. The highest happiness is the "intellectual love of God": understanding our place in the whole of Nature.

**Scripture, toleration and democracy.** The *Theological-Political Treatise* pioneered historical criticism of the Bible, treating it as a human document written by many hands. It argued that the state should permit freedom of thought and speech, and that democracy is the most natural form of government.

## Influence and legacy

Long denounced as an atheist, Spinoza was read secretly throughout the Enlightenment and became a hero of its radical wing. A public controversy over Lessing's alleged Spinozism in the 1780s brought his ideas to the centre of German thought. Hegel declared that one is "either a Spinozist or not a philosopher at all", and the poet Novalis called him "a God-intoxicated man". George Eliot translated the *Ethics* into English.

When asked whether he believed in God, Albert Einstein replied that he believed in "Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the orderly harmony of what exists". Today Spinoza is admired as a pioneer of secular democracy and biblical scholarship, and his account of the emotions has drawn the interest of neuroscientists such as Antonio Damasio.
