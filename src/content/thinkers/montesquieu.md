## Start here

Imagine a school where the principal makes all the rules, catches the students who break them, decides whether they are guilty, and chooses their punishment. There is no appeal. There is no teacher, parent or committee who can question the principal's decisions.

Now imagine the principal is a decent, kind person. Things might be fine, for a while. But what happens when the principal is in a bad mood? Or has a favourite student? Or dislikes a particular family? Or simply makes a mistake? Who can stop it?

You'd probably feel nervous in that school, even if you never broke a rule. Because your safety would depend not on the rules, but on one person's mood.

Now scale it up. Imagine one person, or one small group, who makes the country's laws, enforces them through the police and the army, and also runs the courts that judge every case. Would you feel free? Even if they promised to be fair?

Most of us instinctively feel: no. Power should be **divided**. The people who make laws shouldn't be the same people who enforce them, and neither should be the people who judge whether the laws were broken. That's why, in India, Parliament makes laws, the government (the executive) carries them out, and the courts, up to the Supreme Court, can strike down laws or actions that violate the Constitution.

We take this arrangement for granted. But it is not natural or obvious. For most of human history, kings and emperors held all these powers at once. The idea that liberty depends on **separating** and **balancing** powers was made famous, and turned into a doctrine that shaped constitutions across the world, by a French aristocrat and judge in the eighteenth century.

His name was **Charles-Louis de Secondat, Baron de La Brède et de Montesquieu**, usually known simply as **Montesquieu**. He first became famous for a witty, scandalous novel in which two Persian travellers describe Paris to their friends back home, making French customs look as strange to his readers as Persian customs looked to them. Then he spent twenty years writing an enormous book, ***The Spirit of the Laws***, which tried to explain why different nations have different laws, and what makes some governments free and others tyrannical.

His central warning, written more than two hundred and seventy years ago, still sounds like it was written yesterday:

"Constant experience shows us that every man invested with power is apt to abuse it, and to carry his authority as far as it will go."

By the end of this page, you will understand why he believed power must check power, how he explained the "spirit" of a nation's laws, why the American Constitution's founders called him "the oracle", how his ideas live in India's Constitution, and also where his thinking went wrong, including what he said about climate, Asia and India. And you'll be able to look at any government, any organisation, even any family, and ask the Montesquieu question: **who can stop the person in charge from abusing their power?**

## The world he was born into

### France under the Sun King

Montesquieu was born in **1689**, when France was ruled by **Louis XIV**, the "**Sun King**". Louis had reigned since 1643 and would continue until 1715, seventy-two years, the longest reign in European history.

Louis XIV was the model of an **absolute monarch**. He claimed to rule by divine right, answerable only to God. He built the enormous palace of **Versailles** and forced the great nobles to live there, under his eye, competing for his favour through elaborate rituals. The phrase "**L'État, c'est moi**" ("I am the State") is often attributed to him, though there is no reliable evidence he actually said it. It captures the spirit of his rule.

Louis waged long, expensive wars against much of Europe. In **1685**, he revoked the **Edict of Nantes**, which had given French Protestants (called Huguenots) limited religious freedom. Protestants were forced to convert or flee; around two hundred thousand fled to other countries. Religious freedom in France was crushed.

After Louis XIV died in 1715, there was a period of relaxation under a regent, and then the long reign of **Louis XV**. In 1720, a spectacular financial scheme run by a Scottish financier, **John Law**, collapsed, ruining thousands of investors and shaking trust in the government's finances. Montesquieu satirised such follies in his *Persian Letters*.

### Intermediate powers: the parlements

But French absolutism wasn't quite total. Between the king and the people stood various **intermediate bodies**: the nobility, the Church, provincial assemblies, and above all the ***parlements***, the high courts of law in Paris and the provinces. (Despite the name, they weren't parliaments like Britain's; they were courts.) The parlements had to register royal decrees before they became law, and sometimes refused or protested. Their judges, who often inherited or bought their positions, formed a powerful class called the "**nobility of the robe**".

Montesquieu was one of them. He inherited a top position in the Parlement of Bordeaux. This matters. He saw from the inside how intermediate bodies could restrain a king, and he would argue that they were essential to preventing despotism.

### Across the Channel: a different model

Meanwhile, just across the English Channel, things were very different. After the **Glorious Revolution of 1688**, England had a **constitutional monarchy**: a king with limited powers, a Parliament that made laws and controlled taxes, relatively independent courts, a free press (mostly), and religious toleration for most Protestants. **John Locke** had provided the philosophical justification: government by consent, protection of natural rights, and a distinction between legislative and executive power.

For many French thinkers, England became a fascinating example of a free country. Montesquieu would go and see it for himself.

### The Enlightenment

Montesquieu was one of the first great figures of the **French Enlightenment**. Thinkers in Paris salons and provincial academies were questioning tradition, religious authority and absolute power, using reason, science, satire and comparison. Travel writing was hugely popular: books about Persia, India, China, the Ottoman Empire and the Americas let Europeans see that their own customs were not the only possible ones.

Montesquieu's question was: **why are there so many different kinds of laws and governments in the world? And which ones allow human beings to live in freedom and security?**

## The life, as a story

### A beggar for a godfather

Charles-Louis de Secondat was born on **18 January 1689** at the Château de **La Brède**, a moated castle near **Bordeaux**, in south-western France, a region famous for its wine. His family belonged to the provincial nobility.

According to a story that has been passed down, at his baptism a **beggar** was chosen to be his godfather, so that the child would always remember that the poor were his brothers. Whether or not it shaped him, Montesquieu later showed real concern for the dignity of ordinary people and a hatred of cruelty.

His mother died when he was seven. He was educated at the Oratorian college of **Juilly**, near Paris, which was known for its relatively modern teaching, and then studied **law** at Bordeaux and in Paris.

### The baron-judge

In 1715 he married **Jeanne de Lartigue**, a wealthy woman who was a **Protestant**, at a time when Protestantism was officially banned in France. Their marriage, in that era, says something about Montesquieu's tolerance.

In **1716**, his uncle died and left him his title, **Baron de Montesquieu**, along with his fortune and his position as ***président à mortier*** (a presiding judge) of the **Parlement of Bordeaux**. He was twenty-seven, rich, titled and a senior judge.

He took his judicial duties seriously, though he later admitted he found the procedural details tedious. He was more interested in **why** laws were as they were. He joined the **Bordeaux Academy**, a society for science and learning, and presented papers on topics in natural science, like the causes of echoes and the functions of glands. His scientific curiosity would shape his study of laws: he wanted to find causes, not just describe.

### The book that made him famous: Persian Letters

In **1721**, Montesquieu published, anonymously, a novel called ***Persian Letters*** (*Lettres persanes*). It was an instant sensation.

The novel consists of letters written by two Persian noblemen, **Usbek** and **Rica**, who travel to France and write home to friends, and to Usbek's wives in his harem in Isfahan, about what they see.

Through their supposedly innocent eyes, French society looks ridiculous. The king is described as a great magician who can make people believe that one coin is worth two, and that a piece of paper is money (a dig at John Law's paper money). The Pope is another magician, who makes people believe that three are one and that bread is not bread. Parisians rush around, obsessed with fashion, which changes so fast that a woman who leaves Paris for six months comes back as out of date as if she'd been away thirty years. Scholars waste their lives on useless debates. Religious intolerance is absurd.

There's a famous moment. Rica, walking in Paris in his Persian clothes, is followed by curious crowds. When he changes into French clothes, no one notices him anymore. And when someone learns that he is Persian, they exclaim in wonder: "**Ah! ah! Monsieur is Persian? What a most extraordinary thing! How can anyone be Persian?**"

That question, "how can anyone be Persian?", has become famous because it captures how every society treats its own customs as normal and everyone else's as strange.

But the novel also has a darker story. Usbek, the "enlightened" traveller who criticises French despotism and superstition, rules his own harem in Persia as a **despot**, through eunuchs, fear and confinement. As he travels, the harem falls into chaos. The novel ends with a letter from his favourite wife, **Roxana**, who has taken poison. She tells him that she may have lived in servitude, but she was always free: she rewrote his laws according to the laws of nature, and her mind always remained independent.

So the *Persian Letters* is not just about Persia or France. It's about **despotism** everywhere, including in the household, and about how even people who see tyranny clearly in others can be blind to their own.

### Travelling to learn

The book's success brought Montesquieu to Paris, to the salons, and in 1728 to the **Académie française**, France's most prestigious literary institution.

He then sold his judicial office and spent about three years **travelling** across Europe, from 1728 to 1731: Austria, Hungary, Italy (Venice, Rome, Naples, Florence), Germany, the Netherlands, and finally **England**, where he spent about eighteen months.

In England, he attended debates in **Parliament**, read English newspapers, studied the constitution, met politicians and the royal family, was elected a Fellow of the **Royal Society**, and became a **Freemason**. He was impressed by English liberty, though he also noticed English corruption and political bitterness. England became, in his eyes, the example of a nation whose constitution was designed for political liberty.

### Twenty years of work

Back at La Brède, Montesquieu began his life's great work. First, he published ***Considerations on the Causes of the Greatness of the Romans and Their Decline*** (1734), an analysis of why Rome rose and fell, focusing on causes: its institutions, its love of liberty under the Republic, and how conquest and wealth corrupted it.

Then he spent around **twenty years** writing ***The Spirit of the Laws***. He read enormously: histories of every nation, travel accounts, legal codes, ancient authors. His eyesight was failing, and secretaries read to him. He was also managing his estates and his vineyards, which exported wine to England.

***The Spirit of the Laws*** was published in **Geneva** in **1748**, anonymously, to avoid French censorship. It was huge: thirty-one books, hundreds of chapters, some just a sentence long. It was an immediate success across Europe, going through many editions within two years. It was also attacked: by Jesuits and Jansenists, who saw it as hostile to religion. Montesquieu published a *Defence* in 1750. In **1751**, the Catholic Church placed it on the **Index of Prohibited Books**.

### Death

Montesquieu died in **Paris** on **10 February 1755**, of a fever, aged sixty-six, nearly blind. According to accounts, Jesuits at his bedside pressed him to hand over his papers and corrections, and his friends had to protect them. Of the great writers of the French Enlightenment, it is said that only **Denis Diderot**, editor of the *Encyclopédie*, attended his funeral.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: See your own society as a stranger would

**The idea in one line:** To understand your own customs, imagine how they look to an outsider; what seems natural may be strange, arbitrary or unjust.

This is the lesson of the *Persian Letters*. By having Persians describe France, Montesquieu made his readers see their own society from outside. Customs they had never questioned, royal power, Church authority, fashion, social hierarchy, suddenly looked odd.

**An Indian example.** Imagine a visitor from another planet arriving at an Indian wedding. How would they describe it? "Thousands of people gather; a man arrives on a horse, surrounded by dancing relatives blocking the road; the bride's family spends years of savings on feeding people who will forget the food by next week; everyone asks the young couple when they'll have children..." Some of it is beautiful, some funny, some perhaps worth questioning. Seeing it through outside eyes helps us decide which is which.

Or think of how many Indians visiting other countries come back and see Indian habits, queues, traffic, hospitality, family closeness, with fresh eyes, both good and bad.

**Why it matters.** This "**outsider's view**" is one of the most powerful tools of critical thinking. It's also the foundation of **anthropology** and comparative social science: studying societies by comparison, without assuming your own is the standard. Montesquieu's joke "How can anyone be Persian?" reminds us how easily we treat our own normal as everyone's normal.

**But notice the trap.** Montesquieu's Persians are themselves a European fantasy: the harem, the eunuchs, the despotic husband. The novel uses an exotic stereotype of the East to criticise the West. This ambiguity runs through Montesquieu's work, and we'll return to it.

### Idea 2: Laws have a "spirit"

**The idea in one line:** Laws are not arbitrary commands; they are shaped by and connected to everything about a nation: its government, climate, geography, economy, religion, customs and history.

*The Spirit of the Laws* begins with a famous definition: "Laws, in their most general signification, are the necessary relations arising from the nature of things."

This sounds abstract, but the idea is simple. Just as physical laws describe how things in nature relate (gravity, motion), human laws arise from and relate to the circumstances of each society. To understand why a country has the laws it does, you have to understand its **whole situation**.

Montesquieu listed many factors that shape a nation's laws: its form of government, its climate, its soil and geography, its size, its way of making a living (farming, trade, herding), its religion, its population, its wealth, its customs and its manners. Together, these form what he called the "**general spirit**" (*esprit général*) of a nation.

He wrote that many things govern people: climate, religion, laws, the maxims of government, past examples, morals and customs, and from these a general spirit is formed. Wise lawmakers must respect it. Laws that go too strongly against a nation's general spirit will fail or cause harm.

**An example.** Think of how India's Constitution handles diversity. India didn't simply copy the laws of another country. It created a federal structure with states drawn largely along linguistic lines, allowed different personal laws for different religious communities (a hotly debated choice), provided special protections for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and recognised many languages. Whatever one thinks of each choice, they show lawmakers trying to fit laws to the "general spirit" of a vast, diverse country. Laws that ignore a society's realities, or impose a single model on very different regions, often face resistance.

**Why it matters.** This idea made Montesquieu one of the founders of **sociology** and **comparative politics**. It moved the study of law and government from "what is the ideal system?" to "what causes different systems, and what works where?" It also warns against assuming that one model of government can simply be exported everywhere without regard to local conditions, a lesson that has been learned painfully by many countries trying to impose systems on others.

**A common misunderstanding.** Montesquieu wasn't saying that whatever exists is right, or that laws can never be reformed. He was saying that **good reform** requires understanding a society deeply. He still had strong views about which governments were better and worse.

### Idea 3: Three kinds of government, three principles

**The idea in one line:** Every form of government has a "nature" (who holds power) and a "principle" (the human passion that makes it work): republics run on virtue, monarchies on honour, and despotisms on fear.

Montesquieu classified governments into three types. Each has a **nature**, its structure, and a **principle**, the feeling or motive that makes it function.

**1. Republics.** The people as a whole (democracy) or a part of the people (aristocracy) hold sovereign power. The principle of a republic is **virtue**: not personal holiness, but **political virtue**, which he defined as love of the laws and of one's country, and love of equality. Citizens must care about the common good more than their private interests. When that love decays, when people care only about getting rich, republics collapse. That's what he thought had happened to Rome.

**2. Monarchies.** One person rules, but by **fixed and established laws**, through intermediate powers like the nobility, the courts and the Church. The principle of monarchy is **honour**: people's desire for distinction, rank and reputation. Nobles serve the king to win honour; honour also makes them refuse dishonourable orders. Honour, in a way, does what virtue does in a republic: it makes people serve the state, even if their motives are partly selfish.

**3. Despotism.** One person rules by their own **will and whims**, with no fixed laws and no intermediate powers. The principle of despotism is **fear**. Everyone is equal, in the sense that everyone is equally nothing before the despot. Education in a despotism teaches only obedience. People live in terror, and the despot himself lives in fear of being overthrown.

**An example of honour.** Montesquieu's idea of honour can sound old-fashioned, but think about professional honour today. A doctor who refuses to falsify a medical report, even when ordered to by a powerful person, because "that's not how a doctor behaves", is acting from a kind of honour. So is a civil servant who refuses an unlawful order from a minister, or a journalist who won't reveal a source. Montesquieu saw that such professional pride and codes of conduct can be powerful brakes on abuse of power.

**Corruption of principles.** Montesquieu argued that governments decay when their principles are corrupted. A democracy is corrupted not only when people lose the spirit of equality, but also when they take equality to an extreme, when everyone wants to be equal to those they elected to command them, refusing any authority, until the state becomes chaotic and vulnerable to a tyrant. A monarchy is corrupted when the monarch tries to concentrate everything in himself, destroying intermediate powers. Despotism, he said, is corrupt by its very nature.

**Why it matters.** This framework asks us to think not just about the **formal structure** of a government, but about the **spirit** that sustains it. A democracy with elections but no civic virtue, where people care only about their own group's gain, is in danger. Constitutions on paper are not enough; they need habits, values and feelings to support them.

### Idea 4: What liberty really means

**The idea in one line:** Political liberty is not doing whatever you want; it is the security of living under laws, and the peace of mind that comes from knowing you are safe from arbitrary power.

Montesquieu was careful to define liberty. It is not, he said, doing whatever you please. "**Liberty is a right of doing whatever the laws permit**." If you could do things the law forbids, others could too, and no one would be free.

And then a more personal definition: "The political liberty of the subject is a **tranquillity of mind** arising from the opinion each person has of his safety. In order to have this liberty, it is requisite the government be so constituted as one man need not be afraid of another."

**Think about this.** Liberty isn't only a legal status; it's a **feeling** of security. Do you feel safe from arbitrary arrest? Can you criticise the government without fearing a knock at the door? Can you go to court and expect a fair hearing, whoever your opponent is? If yes, you have political liberty in Montesquieu's sense. If you live in fear of the powerful, even if no one has yet harmed you, you don't.

### Idea 5: Power must check power

**The idea in one line:** Since everyone with power tends to abuse it, liberty requires that power be divided, so that one power can stop another.

This brings us to Montesquieu's most famous argument. He begins with a hard-headed observation about human nature:

"Constant experience shows us that every man invested with power is apt to abuse it, and to carry his authority as far as it will go. Is it not strange, though true, to say that virtue itself has need of limits?"

Notice that last line. Even a **virtuous** person with unlimited power is dangerous. Good intentions are not enough. And so:

"To prevent this abuse, it is necessary from the very nature of things that **power should be a check to power**."

The solution isn't to find a perfectly good ruler. It's to design a system where **power is divided** among different bodies, each able to restrain the others.

**An everyday example.** Think of a cricket match. The players play; the umpires judge; the rules are made by a separate body. If the batting captain could also be the umpire, and could change the rules mid-match, would you trust the result? Of course not. We separate these roles not because we think captains are evil, but because no one should judge their own case.

### Idea 6: The separation of powers

**The idea in one line:** Legislative, executive and judicial powers must be held by different hands; if any two are combined, liberty is lost.

In the famous Chapter 6 of Book XI, "Of the Constitution of England", Montesquieu described three kinds of power in every state:

1. **Legislative power:** making laws.
2. **Executive power:** carrying out the laws, conducting foreign affairs, keeping public order.
3. **Judicial power:** punishing crimes and judging disputes between individuals.

And then the core warnings:

"When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person, or in the same body of magistrates, there can be no liberty; because apprehensions may arise, lest the same monarch or senate should enact tyrannical laws, to execute them in a tyrannical manner."

"Again, **there is no liberty, if the judiciary power be not separated from the legislative and executive**. Were it joined with the legislative, the life and liberty of the subject would be exposed to arbitrary control; for the judge would be then the legislator. Were it joined to the executive power, the judge might behave with violence and oppression."

"There would be an end of everything, were the same man, or the same body, whether of the nobles or of the people, to exercise those three powers."

He described how England, as he saw it, divided these powers: Parliament (with two houses, Lords and Commons, checking each other) made laws; the king held executive power, but needed Parliament's consent for taxes and could veto laws; juries and judges judged cases. Each part could check the others.

**Historians note** that Montesquieu's description of England was somewhat idealised; in practice, the powers there were more intertwined than he suggested. But his theory became more influential than the reality it described.

**The American Constitution.** When the founders of the United States wrote their Constitution in 1787, they built it explicitly on separated and balanced powers: Congress (legislative, divided into the House and Senate), the President (executive, with a veto), and an independent federal judiciary. **James Madison**, defending the Constitution in *The Federalist* No. 47, wrote that "the oracle who is always consulted and cited on this subject is the celebrated Montesquieu". He also said that the accumulation of all powers in the same hands may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny.

**The Indian Constitution.** India's Constitution follows a parliamentary system, in which the executive (the Prime Minister and Cabinet) comes from and is accountable to the legislature (Parliament), so the separation is less strict than in the US. But the principle is deeply embedded: Parliament makes laws; the executive implements them; an **independent judiciary**, headed by the Supreme Court, can review and strike down laws and executive actions that violate the Constitution. Article 50 directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in public services. In the landmark **Kesavananda Bharati case (1973)**, the Supreme Court held that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution in a way that destroys its "**basic structure**", and later judgments have treated the separation of powers as part of that basic structure. Montesquieu's idea is, in a real sense, written into India's constitutional DNA.

**A lesson from India's history.** During the **Emergency of 1975–77**, civil liberties were suspended, the press was censored, opposition leaders were jailed, and the 42nd Amendment tried to reduce the courts' power to review laws. In the notorious **ADM Jabalpur case (1976)**, a majority of the Supreme Court held that during the Emergency, people could not go to court to challenge unlawful detention, a judgment widely regarded today as one of the court's darkest moments, and later overruled. Justice **H. R. Khanna** dissented, at the cost of being passed over for Chief Justice. When the Emergency ended, Parliament restored many safeguards through the 44th Amendment. This episode shows exactly what Montesquieu warned about: when power is concentrated and the checks fail, liberty disappears, even in a democracy.

### Idea 7: Intermediate bodies

**The idea in one line:** Between the ruler and the people, there should be other institutions, such as nobles, courts, local bodies and associations, that slow down and restrain central power.

Montesquieu believed that in a monarchy, "intermediate, subordinate and dependent powers" are essential. Without them, a monarchy slides into despotism. The nobility, the courts, the clergy and the towns all have their own rights and privileges, which the king must respect.

**Why this matters today.** We may not have nobles, but the idea of **intermediate bodies** is alive in the concept of a strong **civil society**: independent universities, professional associations, trade unions, religious organisations, a free press, NGOs, local governments. All of these create centres of power and loyalty that are not simply controlled from the top. They make it harder for any central authority to dominate everything.

India's **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992)**, which gave constitutional status to **panchayats** (village councils) and municipalities, were attempts to strengthen local self-government: to place real power closer to the people and away from the centre. Montesquieu would have recognised this as a way to prevent the concentration of power.

### Idea 8: The theory of climate (and its problems)

**The idea in one line:** Montesquieu argued that climate shapes people's temperaments and therefore their laws: hot climates producing passive peoples and despotism, cold climates producing vigorous, free peoples.

In Books XIV to XVII of *The Spirit of the Laws*, Montesquieu argued that **climate** has a powerful effect on human character. Cold air, he claimed, tightens the body's fibres, making people more vigorous, courageous and frank. Heat relaxes the fibres, making people more sensitive, lazy, passive and prone to pleasure. He conducted an experiment on a sheep's tongue, observing its surface under a microscope while frozen and thawed, to support his theory about how heat affects the nerves.

From this, he drew political conclusions: hot countries tend towards **despotism** and slavery; cold countries towards liberty. He wrote about the peoples of India, among others, as gentle and passive in character, linking this to their climate, and suggested that this made them suited to certain kinds of government.

**This is where we must be critical.** Montesquieu's climate theory is scientifically unfounded, and it fed a long tradition of European stereotypes about "**Oriental despotism**": the idea that Asian societies were naturally despotic, static and servile, while Europe was naturally free and dynamic. This idea was used to justify European colonial rule: if Asians were naturally suited to despotism, then European rule could be presented as no worse, or even as better.

Interestingly, one of the strongest early critiques came from a Frenchman who had actually been to India: **Abraham Hyacinthe Anquetil-Duperron**, who travelled to India in the 1750s, studied Persian and ancient Iranian texts, and later translated the Upanishads into Latin (from a Persian version). In his *Législation orientale* (1778), he argued that Montesquieu's picture of "Oriental despotism" was false: Asian societies had laws, property rights and limits on rulers, just as European ones did. Montesquieu had built a theory on travellers' tales; Anquetil-Duperron had looked more closely.

Indian history, too, contradicts the stereotype: from the Buddhist sanghas and republican *gana-sanghas* of ancient times, through the village assemblies described in South Indian inscriptions, such as the famous **Uttaramerur** inscriptions of the Chola period that describe rules for electing members of a village assembly, to the vigorous political debates of every era, India was never simply a land of passive subjects under despots.

**Why it still matters.** Montesquieu was right that geography and environment affect societies, through agriculture, trade routes, disease and resources. Modern historians and economists still study this. But he was wrong to turn it into a theory of fixed national characters. His mistake is a warning: even a careful, comparative thinker can turn stereotypes into "science" when they confirm what his own culture already believes.

### Idea 9: Commerce softens manners

**The idea in one line:** Trade tends to make people gentler, more tolerant and more peaceful, because nations that trade depend on each other.

"Commerce is a cure for the most destructive prejudices," Montesquieu wrote, "for it is almost a general rule that wherever we find agreeable manners, there commerce flourishes; and that wherever there is commerce, there we meet with agreeable manners."

And: "**Peace is the natural effect of trade.** Two nations who traffic with each other become reciprocally dependent; for if one has an interest in buying, the other has an interest in selling; and thus their union is founded on their mutual necessities."

This idea is sometimes called "***doux commerce***", "gentle commerce". Merchants need trust, reputation, contracts and peace. They learn to deal with strangers from other countries and religions. Commerce, Montesquieu argued, pulls people away from the warrior values of conquest and glory.

He also noted a cost: commerce can make people calculate everything in terms of money, and can weaken some virtues like hospitality.

**An example.** Many people argue that the economic ties between countries make war less likely, because war would destroy profitable trade. The European Union began, after the Second World War, as an economic community linking coal and steel production between former enemies, France and Germany, precisely so that war between them would become materially impossible.

**Why it matters.** This idea influenced **Adam Smith** and many later liberal thinkers. It's still debated: trade has sometimes reduced conflict, but it has also gone hand in hand with colonial conquest (think of the East India Company, which began as a trading company and became a ruler of India). Montesquieu's optimism needs to be tested against history.

### Idea 10: Humane laws and fair punishment

**The idea in one line:** Punishments should be moderate and proportionate to crimes; cruelty in punishment is the mark of despotism, not good government.

Montesquieu argued that severe punishments suit despotisms, which rule by fear, while moderate governments rely more on honour, shame and education. He argued that punishments should be **proportionate** to crimes. If the punishment for robbery is the same as for murder, robbers will kill their victims to avoid witnesses. He also criticised the use of **torture**.

These ideas influenced the Italian reformer **Cesare Beccaria**, whose book *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764) became the founding text of modern criminal justice reform, arguing against torture and the death penalty.

### Idea 11: The slavery chapter: irony as a weapon

**The idea in one line:** Montesquieu attacked slavery by setting out, with bitter irony, the ridiculous arguments that would be needed to defend it.

In Book XV, Chapter 5, "Of the Slavery of the Negroes", Montesquieu wrote a short chapter that pretends to argue in favour of slavery. "Were I to vindicate our right to make slaves of the negroes, these should be my arguments..." He then lists "arguments" such as: sugar would be too expensive if it were not grown by slaves; it is hardly to be believed that God, a wise being, would put a soul, especially a good soul, in a black body; the proof that they lack common sense is that they value a glass necklace more than gold.

These "arguments" are deliberately absurd. The point is to expose the greed, racism and stupidity behind real defences of slavery. The chapter ends: it is impossible for us to suppose these creatures to be men, because, allowing them to be men, a suspicion would follow that we ourselves are not Christians.

**Be careful how you read it.** Because it's ironic, some readers have taken it literally, and it has sometimes been quoted out of context as if Montesquieu meant it. Read in context, it is one of the most biting attacks on slavery of its time. But Montesquieu's broader treatment of slavery is less consistent: elsewhere, in discussing climate, he suggested that in some very hot countries, slavery might be "founded on natural reason". His anti-slavery irony and his climate determinism sit uncomfortably together.

### Idea 12: Toleration, and a plea to the Inquisitors

Montesquieu argued for religious **toleration**: a state should not try to force religious belief, and where several religions exist, the laws should require them to tolerate each other. In Book XXV, he included a moving "**Very humble remonstrance to the inquisitors of Spain and Portugal**", written as if by a Jewish author after a young Jewish woman was burned alive in Lisbon by the Inquisition. It asks the inquisitors how they can claim to follow Christ while burning people for their beliefs, and warns that future ages will look back on them as barbarians.

### Idea 13: Federal republics

Montesquieu noted a dilemma: small republics are good for liberty but weak and vulnerable to conquest; large states are strong but tend towards despotism. His solution: a **confederate republic**, a union of several small republics that combine for defence while each keeps its own internal government. This idea directly influenced the design of the **United States** as a federation of states, and the broader idea of **federalism**, which is central to India's Constitution, with its division of powers between the Union and the states.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Aristotle: the first comparative political scientist

Montesquieu's method of comparing constitutions and classifying governments follows **Aristotle**, who studied 158 Greek constitutions. Aristotle classified governments by who rules (one, few, many) and whether they rule for the common good. Montesquieu added the idea of each government's **principle**, the passion that drives it, and placed far more emphasis on the causes (like climate, commerce and religion) that shape laws.

### Locke: from two powers to three

**John Locke** had distinguished legislative power from executive power and argued that government must be limited and based on consent. Montesquieu built on this, but went further: he made the **judiciary** a separate power, and he focused not just on where power comes from (consent) but on how it must be **structured** to prevent abuse (checks and balances).

### Burke: an admirer

**Edmund Burke** called Montesquieu the greatest genius which had enlightened that age. Burke shared his respect for the particular circumstances and traditions of each nation, his distrust of abstract, one-size-fits-all reforms, and his admiration for the English constitution's balance of powers. Montesquieu's insistence on the "general spirit" of each nation is one root of the **conservative** tradition, even though he is usually counted as a founder of **liberalism**.

### Adam Smith: commerce and civility

**Adam Smith** shared Montesquieu's view that commerce civilises societies and that trade between nations is better than conquest. Both were part of a broad eighteenth-century movement that saw commercial society as a new stage of human history.

### Rousseau and the democrats

Some later thinkers found Montesquieu too cautious. **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** argued for the direct sovereignty of the people, expressed in the "general will", rather than for divided powers. Radical democrats in the French Revolution sometimes saw Montesquieu's intermediate bodies, the nobility and parlements, as defenders of privilege rather than liberty, and in some ways they were right: the parlements often defended aristocratic interests against reforms.

### Hegel: the "philosophical" view of law

**Hegel** praised Montesquieu for his "genuinely philosophical" insight that laws must be understood in relation to the whole character of a nation and its historical moment, not as isolated rules. This historical way of understanding law and institutions was central to Hegel's own philosophy.

### Anquetil-Duperron and the critics of "Oriental despotism"

As we saw, the first serious challenge to Montesquieu's picture of Asia came from **Anquetil-Duperron**, who had studied in India. Much later, the Palestinian-American scholar **Edward Said**, in *Orientalism* (1978), analysed how European writers had constructed images of "the East" as exotic, irrational and despotic, images that served colonial power. Montesquieu's *Persian Letters* and his theory of Oriental despotism are often discussed as early examples.

### Madison and the American founders

The American founders were Montesquieu's most enthusiastic students. **James Madison**, **Alexander Hamilton** and others cited him constantly. The US Constitution's system of **checks and balances**, a President who can veto laws, a Congress that can override the veto and impeach the President, courts that can strike down laws, is perhaps the most complete practical expression of Montesquieu's principle that power must check power.

## See it around you

**In the Supreme Court.** When the Supreme Court of India strikes down a law or an executive action as unconstitutional, you're watching Montesquieu's judicial power check the other two.

**In elections and the Election Commission.** Independent institutions like the Election Commission, the Comptroller and Auditor General, and the Reserve Bank are designed, in part, to prevent any single authority from controlling everything. Debates about their independence are Montesquieu debates.

**In local government.** Panchayats and municipal bodies, with their own powers and elections, are a modern version of the "intermediate bodies" Montesquieu saw as protections against central despotism.

**In federalism.** Disputes between the Union and states over finances, police, education and language are debates about how power should be divided, a federal structure with roots in Montesquieu's confederate republic.

**In any office or institution.** When the person who sets targets is also the person who evaluates performance and decides promotions, with no appeal, you see why Montesquieu insisted on separated powers. Good organisations create independent review, audits and grievance processes.

**In your family.** If one person makes all the rules, enforces them and judges every dispute, with no one able to question them, family life can become despotic, as Usbek's harem did. Healthy families, like healthy states, have ways for decisions to be questioned.

**In travel and culture shock.** Every time you visit another place and find its customs strange, or a foreigner finds yours strange, you're living the *Persian Letters*. "How can anyone be Persian?" becomes "How can anyone eat with a fork?" or "How can anyone not eat rice every day?"

**In stereotypes about other nations.** Every time someone says "those people are naturally lazy" or "that culture is naturally violent", they repeat Montesquieu's climate error: turning complex history into fixed national character.

**In international trade debates.** Arguments that trade promotes peace and prosperity, and counterarguments about exploitation and dependency, continue the debate Montesquieu began.

## Use it

**1. Ask the Montesquieu question.** For any institution you're part of, a company, a college, a housing society, a political party, a family, ask: if the person in charge abused their power, who could stop them?

**2. Be your own Persian visitor.** Pick one custom in your life or community that you've never questioned. Describe it as an outsider would. Does it still make sense? Is it beautiful, harmless, or worth changing?

**3. Separate your roles.** If you are a manager, parent, teacher or team leader, try not to be the rule-maker, enforcer and sole judge all at once. Let others review your decisions. It makes you fairer and more trusted.

**4. Respect the "general spirit".** Before trying to change something, in your workplace, community or family, understand the customs, history and feelings behind it. Changes that ignore them often fail.

**5. Value intermediate bodies.** Support independent institutions, from a free press to resident welfare associations, professional bodies and local governments. They are the "speed-breakers" that slow down abuses of power.

**6. Watch out for climate-style stereotypes.** When you hear someone explain a group's behaviour as their fixed "nature", whether because of geography, region, religion or caste, ask what history, economics and circumstances might really explain it.

**7. Remember that virtue needs limits.** Even if you trust a leader completely, support the checks on their power. Montesquieu's point is not that leaders are bad, but that even good people with unchecked power become dangerous.

**8. Use irony wisely.** Montesquieu showed that irony can expose injustice powerfully. But make sure your irony is understood; otherwise, like his slavery chapter, it may be quoted against your own meaning.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Climate determinism.** His theory that climate shapes national character, making hot countries passive and suited to despotism, is unscientific and fed racist and colonial stereotypes. It contradicts the actual histories of societies in hot climates, including India's.

**2. "Oriental despotism".** His picture of Asian governments as lawless despotisms, built on travellers' tales, was inaccurate and was used to justify European colonial rule. Critics from Anquetil-Duperron to Edward Said have shown how distorted it was.

**3. Defending privilege.** Montesquieu's "intermediate bodies" included the hereditary nobility and the parlements, whose members, like himself, inherited or bought their positions. Critics argue that he was defending the privileges of his own class against both royal reform and popular democracy.

**4. An idealised England.** His description of the English constitution as a model of separated powers was simplified. In practice, the English system blended powers more than he admitted, and was riddled with corruption and limited voting rights.

**5. Inconsistency on slavery.** His brilliant ironic attack on Black slavery coexists with passages that suggest slavery might be natural in some hot climates. He opposed slavery in principle while leaving loopholes in theory.

**6. Women.** Though the *Persian Letters* shows sympathy for women trapped in the harem, *The Spirit of the Laws* often treats women's status as a function of climate and government rather than as a question of their rights as equal human beings.

**7. Separation can produce paralysis.** Critics of strict separation of powers point out that it can lead to gridlock: governments unable to act because different branches block each other. Parliamentary systems like India's deliberately blend legislative and executive power to allow decisive government, while relying on courts and other institutions for checks.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought of good government as finding good leaders and giving them power to act. You may have thought of laws as rules that are simply right or wrong, and of your own customs as simply "normal".

**Now,** you can see that liberty depends less on good leaders than on good **structures**: on dividing power so that no person or group can hold it all, so that power always meets another power that can stop it. You can see laws as part of a nation's whole "spirit", shaped by its history and circumstances. And you can see your own customs from outside, as a Persian visitor might.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A Supreme Court judgement** striking down a law becomes a living example of power checking power.
- **A leader who wants to control everything**, however well-meaning, starts to look dangerous, not efficient.
- **An independent institution** under pressure becomes something worth defending.
- **A boss who sets targets, evaluates you and decides your promotion alone** reveals why fair systems separate those roles.
- **Your own wedding customs, festivals or family rules** become things you can see both from inside and from outside.
- **Stereotypes about whole nations or regions** look less like knowledge and more like Montesquieu's climate error.
- **Liberty** becomes a feeling you can check: do you feel safe from arbitrary power?

Montesquieu spent twenty years trying to understand the spirit of all the world's laws. He got a lot wrong about the world. But he got one thing profoundly right: every person with power is apt to abuse it, and so power must always meet another power. That sentence has protected more people than he could ever have imagined.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Charles-Louis de Secondat, Baron de Montesquieu (1689–1755), French aristocrat, judge in the Parlement of Bordeaux, writer and political theorist.
- **Context:** the absolutism of Louis XIV, the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, John Law's financial crash, the English constitution after 1688, the early French Enlightenment.
- **Persian Letters (1721):** two Persians satirise French society ("How can anyone be Persian?"); a story of despotism in the harem.
- **The Spirit of the Laws (1748):** laws are "necessary relations arising from the nature of things", shaped by a nation's "general spirit".
- **Three governments:** republics (virtue), monarchies (honour), despotisms (fear).
- **Liberty:** the right to do what the laws permit; "tranquillity of mind" from feeling safe.
- **Separation of powers:** legislative, executive and judicial powers must be held by different hands; "power should be a check to power".
- **Other ideas:** intermediate bodies; commerce softens manners; moderate punishments; toleration; confederate republics.
- **Legacy:** the US Constitution (Madison called him "the oracle"), India's independent judiciary and basic structure doctrine, liberal constitutionalism worldwide.
- **Criticisms:** climate determinism, "Oriental despotism" stereotypes, defence of aristocratic privilege, an idealised England, inconsistencies on slavery.

**Key terms**

- **Separation of powers:** dividing legislative, executive and judicial authority among different bodies.
- **Checks and balances:** arrangements that let each power restrain the others.
- **General spirit:** the combination of climate, religion, laws, customs and history that shapes a nation.
- **Principle of government:** the passion that makes a government work: virtue, honour or fear.
- **Despotism:** rule by one person's will, without fixed laws, sustained by fear.
- **Intermediate bodies:** institutions between ruler and people that restrain central power.
- **Doux commerce:** the idea that trade makes people gentler and more peaceful.
- **Confederate republic:** a union of small republics for common defence.
- **Basic structure doctrine:** the Indian Supreme Court's principle that Parliament cannot destroy the Constitution's essential features.
