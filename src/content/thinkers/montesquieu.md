## Life

Charles-Louis de Secondat was born in 1689 at the Château de La Brède, near Bordeaux, into a family of the provincial nobility. At his baptism, so the story goes, a beggar was chosen as his godfather, so that he would always remember that the poor were his brothers. He studied law, and in 1716 he inherited from an uncle both the title **Baron de Montesquieu** and the office of *président à mortier* in the Parlement of Bordeaux, one of France's high courts. He was also an active member of the Bordeaux Academy, presenting papers on natural science.

In 1721 he published, anonymously, the *Persian Letters*, a satirical novel in which two Persian travellers describe Paris to friends at home. Through their supposedly naïve eyes Montesquieu mocked French absolutism, the Church, fashion and intolerance, while a parallel story about a harem in Persia explored despotism. It was a sensation, and its authorship soon became an open secret. He was elected to the Académie française in 1728.

He then sold his judicial office and spent three years travelling through Austria, Hungary, Italy, Germany and the Netherlands, ending with nearly two years in **England** (1729–31). There he observed Parliament, was elected to the Royal Society and became a Freemason. England's constitution, with its free press and balance between king, Lords and Commons, made a lasting impression. Back at La Brède he spent almost twenty years on his masterpiece, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), working through an immense range of sources as his eyesight failed. The book was placed on the Catholic Church's Index of Prohibited Books in 1751. He died in Paris in 1755.

## Key ideas

**Laws have a "spirit".** Laws are not arbitrary commands but "the necessary relations arising from the nature of things". To understand a nation's laws one must relate them to its form of government, climate, geography, economy, religion, customs and population, which together make up its "general spirit". This comparative, almost sociological approach was new.

**Three kinds of government.** Montesquieu distinguished **republics**, whether democratic or aristocratic, whose animating principle is civic *virtue*; **monarchies**, ruled by one person under fixed laws and animated by *honour*; and **despotisms**, ruled by one person's caprice and sustained by *fear*. Despotism was the great danger.

**Separation of powers.** Political liberty is "a tranquillity of mind arising from the opinion each person has of his safety." Since everyone with power tends to abuse it, "power should be a check to power." Liberty requires that legislative, executive and judicial powers be held by separate hands, as he believed they were in England. Above all, "there is no liberty, if the judiciary power be not separated from the legislative and executive."

**Commerce and moderation.** "Commerce is a cure for the most destructive prejudices"; it softens manners and makes nations mutually dependent: "Peace is the natural effect of trade."

**Humane laws.** Montesquieu argued for proportionate and moderate punishments and religious toleration, and he attacked slavery with biting irony, in a chapter that lists the absurd arguments a slaveholder would need.

## Influence and legacy

*The Spirit of the Laws* was one of the most influential books of the eighteenth century. Its doctrine of separated and balanced powers shaped the **United States Constitution**: James Madison called Montesquieu "the oracle who is always consulted and cited on this subject." His ideas also informed French constitutional debates after 1789 and constitutions around the world.

His method of explaining institutions by their social and physical context made him a forerunner of sociology and comparative politics, admired by Tocqueville and Durkheim. His theory of climate's influence on national character, by contrast, has not aged well.
