## Life

John Stuart Mill was born in London in 1806, the eldest son of the Scottish philosopher and historian James Mill, a close ally of Jeremy Bentham. His father subjected him to one of the most famous educational experiments in history. Mill began Greek at three and Latin at eight. By his early teens he had read much of classical literature and studied logic, mathematics and political economy, all at home, without the company of other children. The goal was to raise a champion of utilitarian reform.

At twenty, in 1826, Mill suffered a severe depression. He asked himself whether, if all the reforms he worked for were achieved, he would be happy, and "an irrepressible self-consciousness distinctly answered, 'No!'" He recovered in part through reading Wordsworth's poetry, which taught him the value of feeling and inner culture that his analytic education had neglected. The crisis permanently broadened his philosophy.

From 1823 to 1858 Mill worked for the East India Company in London, rising to a senior post. In 1830 he met **Harriet Taylor**, a married woman with whom he formed an intense intellectual partnership. They married in 1851, two years after her husband's death. Mill credited her as co-author of much of his work, including *On Liberty*. She died in Avignon in 1858, and he bought a house nearby to be close to her grave.

From 1865 to 1868 Mill served as Member of Parliament for Westminster. In 1867 he proposed amending the Reform Bill to give women the vote by replacing the word "man" with "person". The amendment was defeated, but it won 73 votes and launched the parliamentary campaign for women's suffrage. He was godfather to the infant Bertrand Russell. He died in Avignon in 1873.

## Key ideas

**The harm principle.** In *On Liberty* (1859) Mill argued that society may restrict an individual's liberty only to prevent harm to others. A person's own good, physical or moral, "is not a sufficient warrant." Over himself, over his own body and mind, "the individual is sovereign."

**Freedom of thought and discussion.** Silencing an opinion is wrong even if the opinion is false. If it is true, we lose the truth. If it is partly true, we lose the part. And if it is wholly false, we lose the clearer understanding of truth that comes from its collision with error, so that our beliefs become "dead dogma".

**Individuality and the tyranny of the majority.** The greatest threat to liberty in a democracy is not only the state but social pressure to conform. Mill championed "experiments in living" and eccentricity as sources of progress.

**Higher and lower pleasures.** In *Utilitarianism* Mill defended Bentham's principle but argued that pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity. Those who have experienced both prefer the pleasures of intellect, feeling and imagination: better "Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."

**The equality of women.** *The Subjection of Women* (1869) argued that the legal subordination of women was wrong in itself and "one of the chief hindrances to human improvement," and that women should have full equality in law, education, employment and politics.

**Representative government.** Mill supported a broad franchise, proportional representation and public debate as means of educating citizens, though he also proposed extra votes for the educated.

## Influence and legacy

Mill was the most influential English-speaking philosopher of the nineteenth century. *On Liberty* remains the classic statement of liberal individualism and is cited in debates over free speech, drug laws, paternalism and privacy. *The Subjection of Women* became a founding text of liberal feminism. His *System of Logic* shaped the philosophy of science, and his *Principles of Political Economy*, increasingly sympathetic to cooperatives and reform, was the standard economics textbook for decades.

Critics then and since have pressed on his work's tensions: between utility and liberty, between his democratic ideals and his paternalism toward "backward" societies under British rule. Yet his voice, humane, open and self-critical, continues to speak directly to modern readers.
