## Start here

You order a pair of sneakers online for ₹3,000. Two days later, a delivery partner hands them to you. You check the label: "Made in Vietnam" or "Made in India". Think for a moment about what went into those shoes.

Somewhere, workers cut the rubber, stitched the uppers, glued the soles. Perhaps a woman in a factory near Chennai or Ho Chi Minh City did one small step, again and again, hundreds of times a day. She may earn, for the day's work, less than one pair costs. Then there are the designers, the advertisers, the warehouse workers, the delivery partner who earns a small amount per drop, the company's shareholders.

Now ask a strange question: **who actually created the value of these shoes?** And who got most of the money?

The worker who made them? The delivery rider who brought them? Or the company that owns the brand, the factory and the app?

Here's another question. Why does the woman in the factory work so hard for so little? Nobody is holding a gun to her head. She is "free" to quit. But if she quits, how will she eat? She owns nothing except her ability to work. So she must sell it, to someone who owns a factory, on whatever terms she can get.

And one more: why does it feel natural to most of us that the brand owner becomes a billionaire while the stitcher stays poor? Who taught us to see it that way?

These questions were the obsession of **Karl Marx**, a German philosopher who lived much of his life in poverty in London and spent decades studying how **capitalism** works. His answers were explosive. He argued that capitalism is built on the **exploitation** of workers; that history is a story of **class struggle**; that the ideas we take for granted are often the ideas of the powerful; and that capitalism, for all its incredible productive power, would one day be replaced by a society without classes.

His ideas inspired revolutions that ruled a third of humanity. Movements in his name produced both hope and horror: workers' rights and welfare states on one side; dictatorships, labour camps and famines on the other. India has had elected communist governments, Marxist historians, trade unions and Maoist insurgents.

You don't have to agree with Marx. Many of his predictions failed. But once you've understood him, you will never again look at a price tag, a salary slip, a news channel or a strike in quite the same way.

## The world he was born into

### Germany: divided and backward

Marx was born in **1818** in **Trier**, a small, ancient town in the **Rhineland**, then part of the Kingdom of **Prussia**. Germany was not yet a single country; it was a patchwork of kingdoms and principalities. Prussia was an authoritarian monarchy, with censorship, a powerful aristocracy and no real parliament.

The Rhineland, however, had been ruled by **France** during the Napoleonic wars and had absorbed French ideas of legal equality. Liberal ideas were in the air.

### The Industrial Revolution

Meanwhile in **Britain**, the **Industrial Revolution** was transforming the world. Cotton mills, coal mines, iron works and railways created vast wealth. They also created a new class: the industrial working class, or **proletariat**, people who owned nothing but their labour.

Working conditions were terrible. Men, women and young children worked twelve to sixteen hours a day in dangerous factories. Cities like **Manchester** grew into smoky slums, where workers lived in crowded, filthy housing, and disease spread fast. Life expectancy for workers in parts of industrial cities was shockingly low.

At the same time, factory owners grew enormously rich. This combination, unprecedented wealth beside unprecedented misery, was the problem Marx set out to understand.

### Economics and philosophy

Marx inherited three great intellectual traditions, and his work is a fusion of them:

- **German philosophy**, above all **Hegel**, who taught that history develops through conflict and contradiction.
- **British political economy**, especially **Adam Smith** and **David Ricardo**, who analysed how wealth is produced and argued that labour is the source of value.
- **French socialism and revolutionary politics**, from the French Revolution to thinkers like Saint-Simon, Fourier and Proudhon, who dreamed of a fairer society.

### India and the world

Marx's lifetime also saw the expansion of European empires across Asia and Africa. The East India Company ruled India until **1858**, when the British Crown took over after the great **revolt of 1857**. Marx followed Indian events closely as a journalist, and wrote some of the earliest Western analyses of British rule in India.

## The life, as a story

### A lawyer's son in Trier

Karl Marx was born on **5 May 1818** in Trier. His father, **Heinrich Marx**, was a lawyer, from a long line of Jewish rabbis. Under Prussian law, Jews faced restrictions in public professions, so Heinrich converted to Lutheran Protestantism, shortly before Karl was born. Karl was baptised as a child.

Heinrich admired the Enlightenment, read Voltaire and Kant, and hoped his brilliant, stormy son would become a successful lawyer.

### Students, debts and Hegel

In **1835**, at seventeen, Karl went to the **University of Bonn** to study law. He drank, ran up debts, joined a students' drinking club, and was briefly jailed for drunkenness. Some accounts say he fought a duel. His father moved him to the **University of Berlin**.

In Berlin, everything changed. The philosopher **Hegel** had died in 1831, but his ideas dominated the university. Marx joined a group of radical young philosophers known as the **Young Hegelians**, who used Hegel's ideas to criticise religion and the Prussian state. He wrote to his father that he had stayed up night after night reading Hegel and was now, reluctantly, converted.

He completed a doctorate in **1841**, on the ancient Greek philosophers **Democritus and Epicurus**, submitted to the University of Jena. But with radicals barred from university posts, an academic career was closed to him.

### The journalist

In **1842**, Marx became editor of a liberal newspaper, the ***Rheinische Zeitung***, in Cologne. He wrote about censorship, the poverty of wine-growers on the Moselle, and a new law that made it a crime for poor peasants to gather fallen wood from forests, as they had done for centuries. This was his first encounter with economic questions: why did the law protect the forest owner's property against the poor? He later said this was when he first realised he needed to study economics.

The paper was shut down by the Prussian authorities in **1843**.

### Jenny and Paris

That same year, he married **Jenny von Westphalen**, the beautiful, intelligent daughter of a Prussian aristocrat and a childhood friend from Trier. They had been secretly engaged for seven years. Jenny shared his ideas and sacrificed a comfortable life to share his poverty. It was a deep, lasting love, though their life together would be full of hardship.

They moved to **Paris**, the capital of revolutionary politics, where Marx met socialists, workers' groups and radicals from across Europe. He wrote the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*, exploring the idea of **alienation** (we'll see this below).

### Engels

In Paris, in **August 1844**, Marx met **Friedrich Engels** at the Café de la Régence. They had met once before, coldly. This time, they talked for ten days and found they agreed on almost everything.

Engels was the son of a wealthy German textile manufacturer who owned part of a cotton mill in **Manchester**. Engels had just written *The Condition of the Working Class in England*, a searing account of the lives of Manchester's workers, based on his own observations, guided through the slums by his Irish working-class partner, **Mary Burns**.

Marx and Engels became the most famous intellectual partnership in history. Engels was practical, cheerful and quick; Marx deep, slow, and thorough. For the rest of Marx's life, Engels supported him intellectually, politically and, crucially, **financially**, from the profits of the very capitalist business they both attacked.

### Expelled, and the Manifesto

Expelled from Paris in **1845** at the request of the Prussian government, Marx moved to **Brussels**. There, with Engels, he wrote *The German Ideology* and developed his theory of history.

In **1847**, a small group of radical workers, the **Communist League**, asked Marx and Engels to write a statement of their principles. The result, published in London in **February 1848**, was ***The Manifesto of the Communist Party***, a pamphlet of about forty pages. It opened with an image of fear: "A spectre is haunting Europe—the spectre of Communism."

Within weeks, revolutions broke out across Europe: in France, the German states, Austria, Italy. The Manifesto had nothing to do with starting them (it was barely noticed at first), but Marx rushed back to Germany and ran a radical newspaper in Cologne. By **1849**, the revolutions had been crushed. Marx was expelled again.

### London: poverty and the Reading Room

In **August 1849**, Marx arrived in **London**, then the refuge of exiles from across Europe. He would live there for the rest of his life, thirty-four years.

The early London years were desperate. The family lived in two small rooms in **Dean Street, Soho**, a poor area. They were often hungry. Marx pawned his coat and the family silver. Creditors banged on the door. Of their seven children, only three daughters survived to adulthood. Their son **Guido** died as a baby in 1850; their daughter **Franziska** died in 1852, and Jenny had to borrow money for her coffin; their beloved son **Edgar**, nicknamed "Musch", died at eight in **1855**. Marx wrote that he had suffered all kinds of misfortune, but only now did he know what real misfortune was.

There was also a private secret. The family's devoted housekeeper, **Helene Demuth**, gave birth to a son, Frederick, in 1851. Engels was named as the father, but most historians believe, based on later testimony, that Marx was.

Engels moved to Manchester to work in his father's firm and sent money regularly. Marx earned some income as the London correspondent of the ***New-York Daily Tribune***, writing hundreds of articles on European politics, economics, and, importantly, **India** and China.

And every day, for years, Marx walked to the **Reading Room of the British Museum**, where he read everything ever written on political economy, government reports on factories, statistics, history. He took endless notes. He was building, slowly, his great work.

### Capital

In **1867**, the first volume of ***Das Kapital*** (*Capital: A Critique of Political Economy*) was published in Germany. It was the result of nearly twenty years of research. Marx expected it to shake the world. At first, it was largely ignored. A Russian translation appeared in 1872, and the censors allowed it, thinking such a difficult book would never be read by many. They were wrong.

Marx never finished the next volumes. After his death, Engels assembled Volumes II (1885) and III (1894) from his chaotic manuscripts.

### The International and the Commune

In **1864**, Marx helped found the **International Workingmen's Association** (the "First International"), an alliance of trade unionists and socialists from many countries. He wrote its founding address and became its guiding mind.

In **1871**, the workers of Paris rose up and established the **Paris Commune**, a revolutionary city government that lasted about two months before being crushed by the French army, with thousands killed. Marx wrote *The Civil War in France*, praising the Commune as the first example of working-class government. His defence made him notorious across Europe as the "Red Terror Doctor".

### Final years

In his last decade, Marx was often ill: liver trouble, boils, bronchitis. He kept reading, now about Russia, ancient communal societies and agriculture, learning Russian in his fifties to read the sources.

His wife **Jenny** died of cancer in **December 1881**. His eldest daughter, also named Jenny, died in **January 1883**. Marx died two months later, on **14 March 1883**, in his armchair in London.

He was buried in **Highgate Cemetery**. Only around eleven people attended the funeral. At the graveside, Engels said: "His name will endure through the ages, and so also will his work."

He was right. Within a century, governments claiming to follow Marx ruled over a third of the world's population.

## The big ideas, one by one

Marx's ideas form a system. Let's build it up step by step.

### Idea 1: Materialism, how we live shapes how we think

**The idea in one line:** The way a society produces its material life (food, shelter, goods) shapes its politics, laws, religion and ideas.

Hegel had argued that history is driven by the development of **ideas**, of "Spirit". Marx, as people say, turned Hegel "on his head" (or, he would say, put him back on his feet). History, Marx argued, is driven by **material** life: how people produce the things they need to live.

He wrote, in the famous Preface of 1859:

"It is not the consciousness of men that determines their being, but, on the contrary, their social being that determines their consciousness."

What does this mean? Think about a village where everyone farms the land. The rhythms of life follow the seasons. Families are large, because children work the fields. Land is the main form of wealth, so inheritance and marriage are carefully controlled. Festivals mark harvests. Religion is full of rain gods and fertility. Respect for elders runs deep, because their knowledge of farming matters.

Now think of a big city like Bengaluru or Gurugram with IT jobs. Young people move away from family. Wealth comes from skills and salaries, not land. Families get smaller. People marry later, perhaps across caste lines. Festivals become occasions for shopping and holidays. Attitudes to gender change as women earn their own salaries.

What changed first? Marx says: the **way people make a living**. The ideas and customs followed.

Marx called the economic system the "**base**" of society, and the laws, politics, religion, art and ideas built upon it the "**superstructure**". The base shapes the superstructure (though the superstructure also reacts back on the base).

**Why it matters:** This is one of the most influential ideas in the social sciences. Historians, sociologists and economists ask, of any period or society: what was the economic system, and how did it shape everything else? Indian historians like **D.D. Kosambi**, **R.S. Sharma** and **Irfan Habib** used Marxist methods to rewrite Indian history, focusing not just on kings and dynasties but on land, crops, trade, tools, and the lives of ordinary people.

**A common misunderstanding:** Marx did not say that economics determines everything mechanically, or that people are just robots driven by money. He said the economy is the **foundation**, which sets limits and pressures on everything else. Engels later complained that some followers had turned this into crude "economic determinism", and said that Marx and he had stressed economics against their opponents more than they should have.

### Idea 2: Class struggle

**The idea in one line:** Throughout history, society has been divided into classes with opposed interests, and the struggle between them drives historical change.

The *Communist Manifesto* begins:

"The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles. Freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild-master and journeyman, in a word, oppressor and oppressed, stood in constant opposition to one another..."

A **class**, for Marx, is defined by its relationship to the **means of production**: the land, tools, machines, factories, and resources needed to produce goods. Some own the means of production; others must work for those owners.

- In **ancient** societies: slave owners and slaves.
- In **feudal** societies: lords who owned land and serfs who worked it.
- In **capitalist** societies: the **bourgeoisie** (capitalists, who own factories, companies and capital) and the **proletariat** (workers, who own nothing but their ability to work and must sell it for wages).

The interests of these classes are opposed. The owner wants to pay low wages and get long hours. The worker wants high wages and short hours. This conflict, sometimes hidden, sometimes open, in strikes, riots and revolutions, shapes politics and drives history forward.

Think of a landlord and a tenant farmer in a village. Or a factory owner and the workers at their gate. Or an app company and its delivery riders arguing about per-order pay. Their interests clash, however polite they are to each other.

**Why it matters:** Class analysis remains a powerful tool for understanding politics and society. Who benefits from a tax cut? Who bears the cost of inflation? Why do some political parties draw support from some groups and not others? Even people who reject Marxism use class as a basic category.

**A common misunderstanding:** Class is not the same as caste, though they often overlap. In India, caste and class are tangled together: historically, upper castes often owned land, and lower castes, especially Dalits, were often landless labourers. **B.R. Ambedkar** criticised Indian Marxists for ignoring caste, which he saw as a system of graded inequality that could divide workers against each other. He wrote that the caste system "is not merely a division of labour. It is also a division of labourers." A purely class-based analysis, he argued, misses this. This debate between caste and class remains central in Indian politics.

### Idea 3: Alienation

**The idea in one line:** Under capitalism, workers are cut off from the product of their work, from the work itself, from other people, and from their own human nature.

In the *Manuscripts of 1844*, the young Marx described the experience of the worker under capitalism as **alienation** (in German, *Entfremdung*, "becoming a stranger").

Think of a traditional potter in a village. She digs the clay, shapes the pot, fires it, decorates it with her own designs, and sells it at the market. She sees the whole process; the pot is **hers**, an expression of her skill and creativity.

Now think of a worker on an assembly line in a factory that makes plastic buckets. All day, he presses a button that clamps a mould. He doesn't design the bucket, doesn't own it, doesn't sell it, and may never even see a finished one. He does what the machine and the supervisor tell him. He is replaceable at any time.

Marx identified four kinds of alienation:

1. **From the product**: the worker makes things that belong to someone else, and which even seem to rule over him (the more he produces, the richer and more powerful his employer becomes).
2. **From the activity of work**: work is not an expression of himself but something forced on him, done only for the wage. He feels himself only outside work, and at work he feels outside himself.
3. **From his human nature** (what Marx called "species-being"): humans are creative, purposeful producers. Turning work into mere survival degrades this.
4. **From other people**: relationships become competitive, transactional, governed by money.

Remember Adam Smith's warning about the worker who performs a few simple operations and becomes "as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become"? Marx developed this into a full philosophy.

**Why it matters:** Alienation describes something many people feel today: in jobs where they're just a number, where their work feels meaningless, where they count the hours until the weekend. Gig workers controlled by an app, call centre employees reading scripts, coders doing endless repetitive tasks for a distant client. Modern discussions of "**burnout**", "**quiet quitting**" and "meaningful work" are, in part, conversations about alienation.

**A common misunderstanding:** Alienation isn't just "feeling unhappy at work". It's a **structural** condition: a result of how work is organised under capitalism, where workers don't own or control what they produce. A worker could feel cheerful and still be alienated in Marx's sense.

### Idea 4: The labour theory of value and surplus value

**The idea in one line:** The value of goods comes from the labour needed to make them, and profit comes from the unpaid labour of workers.

This is the economic heart of *Capital*, and it's a bit tricky. Let's take it slowly.

**Step 1: Use value and exchange value.** Every commodity (a thing made to be sold) has a **use value**, its usefulness (a shirt keeps you warm), and an **exchange value**, what it can be exchanged for in the market (one shirt = two kilos of rice = ₹500). This distinction goes back to **Aristotle**.

**Step 2: Labour creates value.** What makes a shirt and two kilos of rice exchangeable? What do they have in common? Marx, following Smith and Ricardo, answered: they both take a certain amount of **labour time** to produce. The value of a commodity is determined by the "socially necessary labour time" needed to make it (the average time, with average skill and technology).

**Step 3: Labour power is a commodity.** Under capitalism, workers sell their **labour power**, their ability to work, to capitalists, for a wage. What is the value of labour power? Like any commodity, it's the cost of producing it: the cost of the food, shelter, clothing etc. needed to keep the worker alive and able to work, and to raise the next generation of workers.

**Step 4: Surplus value.** Here comes the key insight. Suppose a worker's daily needs (food, rent etc.) take **four hours** of labour to produce. So the capitalist pays the worker a wage equal to four hours of value. But the worker works for **ten hours**. In the extra six hours, he produces value for which he is **not paid**. Marx called this **surplus value**. It goes to the capitalist as **profit**.

Let's make it concrete. A garment worker in Tiruppur is paid ₹500 a day. In a day, she stitches shirts worth, say, ₹3,000 after deducting the cost of cloth, thread, electricity and machine wear. Where does the difference of ₹2,500 go? To rent, interest, and the owner's profit. Marx says that difference is created by her labour, but taken by others.

**Step 5: Exploitation.** Marx called this **exploitation**. Not necessarily in the sense of cruelty, though often it was cruel, but in a technical sense: the worker produces more value than she receives. Exploitation is built into the system, even when the employer is kind and wages are "fair" by market standards.

This is also why, Marx says, capitalism looks fair on the surface. The worker freely agrees to a wage, the capitalist pays it, both seem to get a fair deal. The exploitation is hidden inside the working day.

He described capital itself in a vivid image: "Capital is dead labour, that, vampire-like, only lives by sucking living labour, and lives the more, the more labour it sucks."

**Why it matters:** Whatever its technical problems, this theory raises a question every society must face: **who creates wealth, and who gets it?** Debates about minimum wages, working hours, CEO pay versus worker pay, gig workers' rights and profit sharing are all, in a sense, debates about surplus value.

**A common misunderstanding:** Most modern economists **reject** the labour theory of value. They explain prices through supply and demand, and value through what people are willing to pay (marginal utility). They also point out that capital, entrepreneurship, risk-taking, organisation and technology contribute to production. So Marx's technical theory is widely seen as flawed. But his broader question, about power and distribution in production, remains alive.

### Idea 5: Commodity fetishism

**The idea in one line:** In capitalism, relationships between people appear as relationships between things, especially money and commodities.

When you buy a phone, you see a **thing** with a price. You don't see the miners in Congo digging cobalt, the factory workers in China or India assembling it, the designers, the shipping crews. The price tag hides all of these human relationships. It seems as if the phone simply "has" a value, like it has a colour or weight.

Marx called this **commodity fetishism**. A "fetish" was an object that people believed had magical powers. Under capitalism, he said, we treat commodities and money as if they had powers of their own. We say "the market decided", "the price of oil rose", "the economy needs", as if markets were gods rather than the result of human actions and relations.

Think of how people talk about the stock market, the **Sensex**: "the market is nervous", "the market punished the company". The market seems like a person with moods. In fact, it's millions of people buying and selling, with real effects on real lives.

**Why it matters:** Commodity fetishism helps us see what's hidden behind prices and products. Movements for "**fair trade**", ethical sourcing, and transparency about supply chains (for example, concerns about child labour in mica mines in Jharkhand, which supply minerals for cosmetics and electronics) are attempts to reveal the human relationships behind commodities.

**A common misunderstanding:** Commodity fetishism doesn't mean people love shopping too much, or worship brands. It's subtler: it's the way a market society makes social relations look like natural facts about things.

### Idea 6: Ideology, the ruling ideas

**The idea in one line:** The dominant ideas in a society tend to be the ideas that serve its ruling class.

In *The German Ideology*, Marx and Engels wrote:

"The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, i.e. the class which is the ruling material force of society, is at the same time its ruling intellectual force."

Why? Because the ruling class controls the means of producing and spreading ideas: in their time, the press, the universities, the churches; in ours, media companies, social media platforms, advertising, think tanks and much of education.

So ideas that support the existing order come to seem like "**common sense**", natural, obvious, eternal. In feudal times, it seemed natural that the lord ruled by right of birth. In capitalism, it seems natural that the owner of a company gets the profits, that wages are set by the market, that poverty is caused by laziness.

Marx called such ideas **ideology**: ways of thinking that make a particular social system seem natural and inevitable, hiding its contradictions.

Think of the phrase "**bootstraps**": "If you work hard, you'll succeed." There's truth in it, but repeated endlessly, it also implies that poor people are poor because they didn't work hard enough, which ignores the role of inherited wealth, caste, gender, schools, connections and luck. Marx would say this idea serves those at the top, by making their position look deserved.

In the Indian context, think about how caste hierarchy was justified for centuries by religious ideas of karma and dharma: your position in life reflects your deeds in past lives. Marx (and, in a different way, Ambedkar and Periyar) would see this as ideology: a set of ideas making an unjust social order appear natural and divinely ordained.

**Why it matters:** The concept of ideology is one of Marx's most important legacies. It teaches us to ask, of any widely held idea, **whose interests does this serve?** The Italian Marxist **Antonio Gramsci** later developed this into the concept of "**hegemony**": the ruling class rules not mainly by force but by making its worldview the common sense of society.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ideology doesn't mean deliberate lies or conspiracies. The ruling class often sincerely believes its own ideas. And not every idea is ideology; Marx thought science, including his own, could see through ideology.

### Idea 7: Religion as "the opium of the people"

**The idea in one line:** Religion is both an expression of real suffering and a protest against it, and a comfort that helps people endure, and accept, oppression.

This is one of Marx's most quoted, and most misunderstood, lines. Here is the full passage, from 1843:

"Religious suffering is, at one and the same time, the expression of real suffering and a protest against real suffering. Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of soulless conditions. It is the opium of the people."

Notice the tone. Marx isn't just mocking religion. He's saying religion arises from real suffering. It's a "sigh", a "heart in a heartless world". In his time, opium was used as a painkiller. Religion soothes real pain.

But that's the problem. Painkillers don't cure the disease. If people find comfort in the promise of heaven, or accept their suffering as fate or karma, they may not try to change the conditions causing their suffering. Marx wanted to abolish the conditions that make people need such comfort: "The demand to give up the illusions about its condition is the demand to give up a condition which requires illusions."

**Why it matters:** Marx's analysis influenced debates about the role of religion in politics across the world. In India, Marxists often clashed with religious movements, and also with Gandhi, whose politics were deeply spiritual. Yet religion has also fuelled movements for justice: **Liberation theology** in Latin America combined Christianity with Marxist analysis; the **Bhakti** saints like Kabir and Ravidas used religious language to attack caste; Ambedkar turned to **Buddhism** as a religion of liberation.

**A common misunderstanding:** Marx didn't propose to abolish religion by force. That was done later by some communist regimes, with brutal results. Marx thought religion would fade away naturally once the conditions that produce it, oppression and suffering, were removed.

### Idea 8: Capitalism, revolutionary and doomed

**The idea in one line:** Capitalism is the most productive system in history, but its own contradictions will eventually destroy it.

Here's something that surprises people: the *Communist Manifesto* contains some of the most glowing praise of capitalism ever written. Marx and Engels wrote that the bourgeoisie, "during its rule of scarce one hundred years, has created more massive and more colossal productive forces than have all preceding generations together". It has built railways, canals, factories, cities; connected the world through trade; dissolved ancient superstitions and feudal ties.

"All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned, and man is at last compelled to face with sober senses his real conditions of life, and his relations with his kind."

Capitalism, they said, is constantly **revolutionising** itself: new technologies, new products, new markets. Think of how, in just a few decades, smartphones, the internet, UPI payments and e-commerce have transformed life in India. That's capitalism's dynamism.

But Marx also argued capitalism has deep **contradictions** that would eventually destroy it:

1. **Crises of overproduction.** Capitalists compete to produce more and cheaper, while keeping wages low. But if workers are paid little, who will buy all the goods? This leads to periodic **crises**: booms and busts, recessions, unemployment.
2. **Concentration.** Competition drives smaller firms out; capital becomes concentrated in fewer and fewer hands: monopolies.
3. **Falling rate of profit.** As capitalists replace workers with machines, and if profit comes from labour, the rate of profit tends to fall over time (this is a controversial theory).
4. **Polarisation.** Society splits into a small class of very rich owners and a vast mass of workers.
5. **Globalisation.** Capitalism spreads across the world, seeking new markets and cheap labour, "it creates a world after its own image".

Eventually, Marx predicted, the workers, organised and conscious of their shared interests, would overthrow capitalism. "The proletarians have nothing to lose but their chains. They have a world to win. Working Men of All Countries, Unite!"

**Why it matters:** Marx's analysis of capitalism's dynamism and its crisis tendencies remains influential even among economists who reject his conclusions. After the **2008 financial crisis**, sales of *Capital* rose, and even mainstream newspapers ran articles asking whether Marx had been right about capitalism's instability. Debates about inequality, especially after **Thomas Piketty**'s *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013), revived Marxian questions about the concentration of wealth.

**A common misunderstanding:** Marx did not hate capitalism in a simple way. He admired its power and saw it as a necessary stage of history that would develop technology and wealth enough to make a better society possible. He just thought it couldn't last, and that its benefits were unfairly shared.

### Idea 9: Making history, but not as we please

**The idea in one line:** People make history through their actions, but within conditions they inherit and cannot choose.

In *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852), Marx wrote:

"Men make their own history, but they do not make it as they please; they do not make it under self-selected circumstances, but under circumstances existing already, given and transmitted from the past. The tradition of all dead generations weighs like a nightmare on the brains of the living."

This balances two ideas: **human agency** (we act, we choose, we struggle) and **structure** (we're born into economic systems, classes, cultures, histories that shape and limit our choices).

Think of a young Dalit woman from a poor family in a village. She can study, work hard, fight discrimination, and perhaps become a doctor or an officer. That's agency. But she starts with less money, worse schools, social prejudice, maybe family responsibilities that others don't have. That's structure. Her life is made by both.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the wisest sentences in social thought. It avoids two errors: believing that individuals can do anything if they try (ignoring structure), and believing that individuals are mere puppets (ignoring agency). Notice how close this is to Burke's idea of the inheritance from the dead, but with the opposite feeling: for Burke, the past is a treasure; for Marx, often a nightmare.

**A common misunderstanding:** Marx is sometimes portrayed as a pure determinist who thought history follows iron laws regardless of human action. This passage shows he believed human action mattered, though within limits.

### Idea 10: Communism, from each, to each

**The idea in one line:** After capitalism, a classless society would emerge, where the means of production are owned in common and goods are distributed according to need.

Marx wrote surprisingly little about what communism would actually look like. He thought it was unscientific to draw up detailed blueprints of the future. But some things are clear.

After a workers' revolution, there would be a transitional period, which he once called the "**dictatorship of the proletariat**". (By "dictatorship" he meant the rule of a class, in the way he thought capitalist democracies were a "dictatorship" of the bourgeoisie, not necessarily one-man rule. Unfortunately, the phrase was later used to justify actual dictatorships.)

Then, in a "first phase" of communism, people would be paid according to their work. Later, in a "higher phase", with abundance and the end of the division between mental and physical labour, society would follow the principle:

"From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs!"

The state, as an instrument of class rule, would eventually "**wither away**" (Engels's phrase), because there would be no classes to rule over.

In *The German Ideology*, Marx and Engels gave a famous picture of unalienated life: in communist society, it would be possible "to hunt in the morning, fish in the afternoon, rear cattle in the evening, criticise after dinner... without ever becoming hunter, fisherman, herdsman or critic". People would not be trapped in one narrow role their whole lives.

**Why it matters:** This vision inspired millions of people to fight for a world without exploitation. It also influenced many less radical ideas: welfare states that provide healthcare and education according to need; cooperatives; public ownership of key industries.

**A common misunderstanding:** The Soviet Union, Maoist China and other "communist" states claimed to be building Marx's communism. In reality, they created powerful one-party states, with a new ruling class of party officials, censorship, labour camps, and in several cases catastrophic famines. Whether this was a betrayal of Marx or a predictable result of his ideas is one of the great debates of modern history. Rosa Luxemburg, a Marxist, warned against it as early as 1918.

### Idea 11: Marx on India

**The idea in one line:** British rule destroyed India's old society brutally, but in doing so, Marx thought, it was also laying the foundations of a modern one.

In 1853, Marx wrote a series of articles for the *New-York Daily Tribune* on British rule in India. They are complex and controversial.

On one hand, he condemned British rule fiercely. He described how British textile imports destroyed India's handloom weavers, the "**hand-loom**" and "**spinning-wheel**", turning great textile cities like Dhaka into ruins. He described the cruelty and greed of the East India Company. Later, in **1857**, he wrote sympathetic articles about the great revolt, exposing British atrocities in its suppression.

On the other hand, he saw British rule as historically "progressive" in one sense. He described India's old village communities as stagnant, and wrote that, however idyllic they appeared, "they restrained the human mind within the smallest possible compass, making it the unresisting tool of superstition". He wrote:

"England has to fulfil a double mission in India: one destructive, the other regenerating—the annihilation of old Asiatic society, and the laying the material foundations of Western society in Asia."

He thought railways, the telegraph, a unified administration and a free press would eventually create the conditions for Indian self-rule. And he predicted that Indians would not reap the benefits "till in Great Britain itself the now ruling classes shall have been supplanted by the industrial proletariat, or till the Hindoos themselves shall have grown strong enough to throw off the English yoke altogether".

**Why it matters:** These articles are central to debates about colonialism. Some Indian scholars criticise them as Eurocentric, treating India as a society without history, waiting for Europe to wake it up. Others note that Marx's later writings, when he studied Russian and other village communities, became more sympathetic to non-Western paths of development. Either way, Marx's writings shaped generations of Indian historians and political leaders.

**A common misunderstanding:** Marx didn't simply "support" British rule. He thought it was brutal and driven by greed, but also that it would, unintentionally, destroy feudal structures and open the way to modernity. That double view is uncomfortable, and it should be.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Hegel: the dialectic turned upside down

**Hegel** taught that history moves through **contradictions**: an idea (thesis) produces its opposite (antithesis), and the conflict between them produces a higher stage (synthesis). Marx kept this "**dialectical**" method but applied it to material life: the contradiction between classes, between the forces and relations of production. "With him it is standing on its head," Marx wrote of Hegel's dialectic. "It must be turned right side up again." For Hegel, history ends in the self-realisation of Spirit in the modern state; for Marx, it would end in a classless society.

### Smith and Ricardo: the economics he took over

From **Adam Smith** and **David Ricardo**, Marx took the **labour theory of value**, the analysis of the division of labour, and the study of how capitalism grows. He respected them as serious scientists, unlike the "vulgar economists" who simply defended capitalism. But he argued that they failed to see that profit was unpaid labour, and that they treated capitalism as natural and eternal rather than as one historical stage.

### Aristotle: use and exchange

Marx admired **Aristotle**, whom he called "the great investigator". He took Aristotle's distinction between **use value** and **exchange value**, and his distinction between *oikonomia* (managing a household to meet needs) and *chrematistics* (making money for its own sake). Aristotle condemned the endless pursuit of wealth as unnatural; Marx showed how capitalism turns it into the engine of the whole economy.

### Bentham, Mill and Burke: the targets

Marx attacked **Bentham** as an "arch-philistine" whose utilitarianism simply took the English shopkeeper as the model of human nature. He dismissed **John Stuart Mill**'s attempt to reconcile capitalism with workers' interests as a "shallow syncretism". He called **Burke** a "sycophant" of the oligarchy. In each case, Marx saw thinkers who treated the capitalist present as normal and natural.

Imagine Marx and Mill debating:

*Mill:* Let us have free markets, but tax inheritances, encourage cooperatives, educate workers and extend the vote. Society can be reformed gradually.

*Marx:* You treat distribution as if it were separate from production. But as long as a few own the factories, they will dominate politics, press and everything else. Your reforms leave the foundation untouched.

*Mill:* And your revolution would put all power in the hands of the state. What happens to individual liberty then?

*Marx:* What liberty does the factory worker have now?

Both questions still hang in the air.

### Luxemburg: the faithful critic

**Rosa Luxemburg** developed Marx's economics, arguing that capitalism needs to expand into non-capitalist regions (colonies) to survive, which explains imperialism. She also warned Lenin's Bolsheviks that socialism without democracy, free elections and free speech would become a bureaucratic dictatorship. She is one of the most important voices for a democratic Marxism.

### Nietzsche: a different revolution

**Nietzsche**, writing at the same time, also attacked bourgeois values and Christian morality. But where Marx wanted equality and collective liberation, Nietzsche despised socialism as a "slave morality" of resentment and championed rare, exceptional individuals.

### Sartre: Marxism and freedom

**Jean-Paul Sartre**, the existentialist, called Marxism "the unsurpassable philosophy of our time" and spent years trying to combine Marx's analysis of social structures with existentialism's emphasis on individual freedom and responsibility.

### Ambedkar: Buddha or Marx?

In India, **B.R. Ambedkar** engaged seriously with Marx. He agreed that exploitation must be ended and that property relations matter. But in his essay ***Buddha or Karl Marx*** (written in 1956), he argued that the Buddha's way, through persuasion, moral change and democracy, was superior to Marx's way of violence and dictatorship. He also criticised Indian communists for ignoring caste. This debate shaped Indian politics deeply.

### Later voices

- **Lenin** (1870–1924) adapted Marxism to lead the Russian Revolution of 1917, creating a vanguard party.
- **Antonio Gramsci** (1891–1937) developed the theory of cultural hegemony.
- **Mao Zedong** (1893–1976) adapted Marxism to a peasant society.
- **The Frankfurt School** (Adorno, Horkheimer, Marcuse) combined Marx with Freud to analyse mass culture.
- **Karl Popper** (1902–1994) and **Friedrich Hayek** (1899–1992) were influential critics, arguing that Marxism was unfalsifiable or led to tyranny.
- In India: **M.N. Roy**, **E.M.S. Namboodiripad** (whose communist government in Kerala in 1957 was one of the first elected communist governments in the world), **Bhagat Singh** (who read Marx and Lenin in prison), and historians like Kosambi, Sharma and Habib.

## See it around you

**Gig workers and platform capitalism.** Delivery riders, cab drivers and home-service workers on apps are classed as "partners", not employees. They often have no minimum wage, no sick leave, no pension. The app decides their pay per order. Marx would see a classic case of workers who own nothing but their labour, selling it on terms set by those who own the means of production, in this case, the platform. Rajasthan passed a law in **2023** for the registration and welfare of platform-based gig workers, and the national **Code on Social Security, 2020** recognises gig and platform workers, reflecting the political struggle over these relationships.

**Strikes and unions.** When workers at a factory or a hospital go on strike, demanding higher wages or better conditions, you're seeing class struggle in Marx's sense. India's trade unions and their periodic nationwide strikes, and debates over the new labour codes, are part of this.

**Farmers' protests.** The farmers' protests of **2020–21** against three farm laws, which were eventually repealed in November 2021, can be read in many ways. A Marxian reading would focus on who owns land, who controls markets, and the power of agribusiness corporations.

**CEO pay and inequality.** Reports showing that the richest 1 per cent of Indians hold a very large share of national wealth, or that a CEO earns hundreds of times what the average employee earns, raise the question Marx asked: who creates value, and who captures it?

**Advertising and consumerism.** Ads tell you that you'll be happy, attractive, successful if you buy this phone, car or cream. Marx would see commodity fetishism at work: social desires like respect, love and belonging attached to things.

**Ideology on TV and social media.** Whenever you hear that poverty is caused by laziness, that billionaires deserve their wealth because they work hard, or that the market is always right, Marx asks: whose interests do these ideas serve? (He'd also ask: who owns the channel or platform?)

**Automation and AI.** As machines and artificial intelligence replace jobs, Marx's questions return: who owns the machines, and who benefits from the increased productivity? If AI makes production far cheaper, will that free people from toil, or just enrich the owners of AI?

**Meaningless work.** Feeling that your job has no meaning, that you're a replaceable part, that you live only on weekends: that's what Marx called alienation.

## Use it

**1. Ask "who benefits?"** Whenever you hear an idea presented as obvious common sense, about work, wealth, poverty, gender, caste, ask whose interests it serves. Sometimes it serves everyone. Often it serves some more than others.

**2. Follow the chain behind your purchases.** Pick one thing you own, your phone, your shirt, your tea. Try to trace who made it, under what conditions, and how much they earned. You'll see the human relationships behind the price tag, and you might make different choices.

**3. Look at structures, not just individuals.** When someone fails or succeeds, ask not just "did they work hard?" but "what conditions were they born into? What advantages or obstacles did they have?" Marx's "not as they please" applies to everyone, including you.

**4. Know your own position.** Are you an owner, a worker, or something in between? Understanding your class position helps you understand your interests, and helps you see why people in different positions might see things differently.

**5. Find meaning in your work, or change the conditions.** If you feel alienated at work, ask what part of your work you can make your own: learning a skill fully, seeing the whole process, connecting with colleagues. And remember that alienation is often structural, which is why workers organise together, not just suffer alone.

**6. Read history from below.** When you read about history, don't just ask about kings, battles and leaders. Ask: how did ordinary people live? What did they eat, how did they work, who owned the land? You'll understand far more.

**7. Watch for the vampire, but don't become one.** If you become a manager, employer or investor, ask whether the people working for you are getting a fair share of the value they create. Being on the other side of the relationship doesn't make Marx's question go away.

**8. Be sceptical of utopias, including Marxist ones.** Use Marx's tools to understand society, but remember what happened when people tried to impose his vision by force. A good analysis of a problem doesn't automatically mean a good solution.

## Where he went wrong

**1. The revolution didn't happen where he predicted.** Marx expected revolution in advanced industrial countries like Britain and Germany, where the working class was large. Instead, revolutions in his name happened in mostly agrarian countries like Russia and China, while in the advanced countries, workers gained the vote, unions, welfare states and rising living standards. Capitalism reformed itself, partly in response to socialist pressure.

**2. Workers didn't get steadily poorer.** Marx predicted the "immiseration" of the proletariat. In fact, in most capitalist countries, real wages rose enormously over the twentieth century. Hundreds of millions of people, including in India after 1991, escaped extreme poverty through market-based growth.

**3. The labour theory of value.** Most economists reject it. Prices are determined by supply and demand; value depends on usefulness and scarcity as well as labour. Capital, technology, organisation and entrepreneurship contribute to production. Marx's technical economics has largely been abandoned, even by many Marxists.

**4. Communism in practice.** Every state that tried to implement Marxism created a one-party dictatorship. The **Soviet Union** under Stalin, **China** under Mao, **Cambodia** under the Khmer Rouge, **North Korea**: these regimes were responsible for tens of millions of deaths through famines, purges and labour camps. Whether this was Marx's fault is debated, but his ideas of the "dictatorship of the proletariat", class war and the abolition of private property, combined with his contempt for "bourgeois" liberties, gave them easy justifications.

**5. Neglect of individual rights and democracy.** Marx dismissed liberal rights like free speech and property as "bourgeois". He underestimated how important they are for protecting people against any concentration of power, including one exercised in the name of the workers.

**6. Class isn't everything.** Marx reduced most social conflicts to class. But caste, race, religion, gender, nationality and ethnicity are powerful forces that can't simply be explained as class in disguise. Ambedkar's critique of Indian Marxists on caste is a powerful example. Workers have often been more loyal to their nation, religion or caste than to their class: in 1914, European workers marched off to kill each other for their countries.

**7. Eurocentrism.** His writings on India treated Asian societies as stagnant and in need of European "regeneration", underestimating their own histories of change, trade and resistance.

**8. Human nature.** Critics argue Marx underestimated self-interest, the desire for status, the need for incentives, and the difficulty of coordinating a complex economy without markets. Hayek argued that central planning fails because no planner can gather the dispersed knowledge contained in market prices.

**9. Personal contradictions.** Marx relied all his life on Engels's capitalist profits; he probably fathered a child with his housekeeper and let his friend take the blame; he could be harsh, intolerant of disagreement and contemptuous of rivals. None of this refutes his ideas, but it reminds us that he was a human being, not a prophet.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have seen the economy as a natural system, like the weather: prices rise and fall, some people are rich and some poor, and that's just how things are. You may have heard "Marx" as a slogan, either of revolution or of failure.

**Now,** you can see the economy as a set of human relationships: between those who own and those who work, between those who make things and those who profit from them. You can see that the things around you contain the labour of many hidden people, that common-sense ideas often serve particular interests, and that people make history, but not under conditions they choose. And you can also see the dangers of a theory that promised liberation and was used to justify tyranny.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A price tag** hides a chain of workers you'll never meet.
- **A salary slip** raises a question: how much value did I create, and how much did I receive?
- **"That's just common sense"** prompts you to ask: whose common sense?
- **A delivery rider** in the rain becomes a worker without a factory, governed by an app.
- **A boring, meaningless job** gets a name: alienation, and a structural cause.
- **A billionaire's fortune** makes you think about who else helped create it.
- **A promise of paradise on earth** reminds you of what happened when people tried to force it.

Karl Marx pawned his coat to feed his children and spent decades in a library chair, building an analysis of the system that was making the modern world. He got many predictions wrong, and terrible things were done in his name. But he taught the world to ask, of every fortune and every poverty, of every "natural" idea: *how did this come to be, and who benefits?* That question has never gone away.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Karl Marx (1818–1883), German philosopher, economist, journalist and revolutionary; lived in exile in London from 1849; lifelong partner of Friedrich Engels.
- **Context:** the Industrial Revolution, factory misery beside unprecedented wealth, the revolutions of 1848, European empire including in India.
- **Materialism:** the way people produce their living shapes their society and ideas (base and superstructure).
- **Class struggle:** history is driven by conflict between owners and workers.
- **Alienation:** workers are estranged from their product, their work, their humanity and each other.
- **Surplus value:** profit comes from labour the worker isn't paid for; this is exploitation.
- **Commodity fetishism and ideology:** markets hide human relationships; the ruling ideas are the ideas of the ruling class.
- **Capitalism:** incredibly productive and revolutionary, but prone to crisis and concentration.
- **Communism:** a classless society, "from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs".
- **On India:** condemned British plunder, but saw colonial rule as unintentionally modernising.
- **Criticisms:** failed predictions, a rejected theory of value, tyranny in his name, neglect of rights, caste and nation, and Eurocentrism.

**Key terms**

- **Means of production:** the land, tools, machines and resources used to produce goods.
- **Bourgeoisie / proletariat:** the class that owns the means of production / the class that must sell its labour.
- **Historical materialism:** Marx's theory that material conditions drive historical change.
- **Base and superstructure:** the economy, and the laws, politics and ideas built on it.
- **Alienation:** estrangement of workers from their work and their humanity.
- **Labour power:** the capacity to work, which workers sell for wages.
- **Surplus value:** the value workers produce beyond what they're paid.
- **Commodity fetishism:** treating relations between people as if they were relations between things.
- **Ideology:** ideas that make a social system seem natural and inevitable.
- **Dialectic:** development through contradiction and conflict.
- **Dictatorship of the proletariat:** Marx's term for the transitional rule of the working class after revolution.
