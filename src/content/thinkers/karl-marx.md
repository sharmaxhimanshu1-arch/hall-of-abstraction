## Life

Karl Marx was born in 1818 in Trier, in the Prussian Rhineland, into a family descended from a long line of rabbis. His father, a lawyer, converted to Lutheranism to keep his profession under Prussian law. Marx studied law at Bonn and Berlin, but in Berlin he was drawn into the circle of **Young Hegelians**, radical students of Hegel's philosophy who turned it against religion and the Prussian state. He received a doctorate in 1841 for a dissertation on the ancient atomists Democritus and Epicurus.

Barred from an academic career by his radical views, Marx became a journalist. He edited the liberal *Rheinische Zeitung* in Cologne until the government suppressed it in 1843. That year he married Jenny von Westphalen, the daughter of a Prussian aristocrat, and moved to Paris. There he began studying political economy and met **Friedrich Engels**, the son of a textile manufacturer, who became his lifelong collaborator, friend and financial supporter.

Expelled from Paris, Marx moved to Brussels, where he and Engels wrote *The Communist Manifesto*, published in February 1848 on the eve of revolutions across Europe. When those revolutions failed, Marx was expelled again and settled in 1849 in **London**, where he would remain for the rest of his life.

The London years were marked by poverty, illness and grief: three of the Marxes' children died young. Marx worked as a correspondent for the *New York Tribune* and spent years in the British Museum reading room on his critique of political economy. He helped lead the International Workingmen's Association, the First International, founded in 1864. The first volume of *Capital* appeared in 1867. Volumes two and three were edited by Engels from Marx's manuscripts after his death. Jenny died in 1881, and Marx died in London in 1883. Eleven people attended his funeral at Highgate Cemetery.

## Key ideas

**Alienation.** In his early manuscripts Marx argued that under capitalism workers are alienated: from the products of their labour, which belong to someone else; from the activity of work, which becomes a mere means of survival; from other people; and from their own creative human nature.

**Historical materialism.** Marx turned Hegel's dialectic "right side up". History is driven not by ideas but by material conditions: by how human beings produce their means of life. Each society's **forces of production** and **relations of production** form its economic base, which shapes its politics, law, religion and ideas, its "superstructure".

**Class struggle.** "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles." In capitalism the central conflict is between the **bourgeoisie**, who own the means of production, and the **proletariat**, who own only their labour power.

**Surplus value and exploitation.** Building on Adam Smith and David Ricardo, Marx argued that labour is the source of value. Workers are paid a wage covering the cost of their subsistence, but they produce more value than that. The difference, **surplus value**, is appropriated by the capitalist as profit. Exploitation is built into the system even when exchange is fair by market standards.

**Commodity fetishism.** In a market economy, social relations between people appear as relations between things: prices, money, commodities. That hides the human labour behind them.

**Crisis and revolution.** Capitalism revolutionises production and creates unprecedented wealth, as the *Manifesto* admiringly describes, but it is prone to recurring crises. Marx expected the proletariat to overthrow it and, after a transitional period, establish a classless communist society governed by the principle "From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs!" He wrote little about what such a society would look like.

## Influence and legacy

Few thinkers have had a greater impact on world history. Marx's ideas inspired socialist and labour movements worldwide, and in the twentieth century revolutionary parties claiming his name ruled a third of humanity, in the Soviet Union, China, Eastern Europe, Cuba and elsewhere. Those regimes' repression, famines and economic failures are central to judgements of his legacy, though scholars debate how far they followed from his own ideas.

As a theorist, Marx helped found modern sociology alongside Weber and Durkheim. His analysis of class, ideology, economic power, globalisation and crisis continues to influence economics, history, political science and cultural theory. Each new crisis of capitalism tends to bring a revival of interest in his work.
