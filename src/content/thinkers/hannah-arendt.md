## Start here

Imagine a government clerk. He is polite, punctual, hardworking. He loves his family. He has no particular hatred for anyone. He wants a promotion. Every day he goes to his office, reads files, signs papers, arranges schedules, and makes sure the trains run on time. He is good at logistics.

Now imagine that the trains he schedules carry millions of people to their deaths.

In **1961**, in a glass booth in a courtroom in **Jerusalem**, sat a man who had done exactly this. **Adolf Eichmann** had organised the transport of Jews from all over Europe to the Nazi death camps. The world expected to see a monster: a fanatic, a sadist, a demon. A German-Jewish philosopher who had herself escaped the Nazis came to watch the trial and report on it for an American magazine.

What she saw shocked her. Eichmann was not a monster. He was **ordinary**. He spoke in clichés and official jargon. He seemed to have no deep hatred, no ideas of his own, no ability to imagine what his actions meant to others. He had simply, as he put it, done his job and obeyed the law. She coined a phrase for it that has haunted the world ever since: **"the banality of evil"**.

Her name was **Hannah Arendt**. She had been arrested by the Gestapo, lived as a stateless refugee for fourteen years, escaped from an internment camp in France, and arrived in New York with almost nothing. She went on to write some of the most important books of the twentieth century on how totalitarian regimes destroy freedom, what it means to have rights, what power really is, and why thinking, the simple habit of stopping to ask "what am I doing?", may be our best protection against evil.

After 2016, as many democracies worried about lies, polarisation and strongmen, sales of her book *The Origins of Totalitarianism* surged again. By the end of this page, you'll understand why, and you'll never hear "I was just doing my job" in the same way again.

## The world she was born into

### Germany between empire and catastrophe

Arendt was born in **1906** in the German Empire. She grew up in **Königsberg**, in East Prussia, the city where **Immanuel Kant** had lived and taught. Her family were secular, educated, liberal Jews, part of a German-Jewish community that had become deeply rooted in German culture, and believed itself fully German.

Her childhood saw the **First World War** (1914–18), Germany's defeat, and revolution. In **1919**, the socialist revolutionary **Rosa Luxemburg** was murdered in Berlin; Arendt's mother, a Luxemburg admirer, had taken her daughter to political meetings. The **Weimar Republic**, Germany's first democracy, followed: a time of brilliant culture, hyperinflation, street violence between communists and fascists, and deepening antisemitism.

### The age of totalitarianism

In **1933**, **Hitler** came to power. Within months, Germany's democracy was gone. Jews were expelled from universities and professions, stripped of citizenship (**1935**), attacked in the **Kristallnacht** pogrom (**1938**), and from **1941**, systematically murdered. Six million Jews were killed in the Holocaust.

At the same time, in the Soviet Union, **Stalin**'s regime sent millions to labour camps (the **Gulag**) and killed millions more through famine and terror.

### The age of refugees

The two world wars and the collapse of empires produced something new: millions of **stateless people**, refugees whom no country would accept as citizens. Russians fleeing the revolution, Armenians fleeing massacre, Jews stripped of German citizenship. They had no passport, no country, no government to protect them. Arendt became one of them.

## The life, as a story

### A clever, defiant girl

Johanna "Hannah" Arendt was born on **14 October 1906** in Linden, near Hanover, and grew up in **Königsberg**. Her father died when she was seven. Her mother, **Martha**, raised her to be proud and independent. If a teacher made antisemitic remarks, Hannah was to stand up, leave the class and go home, and her mother would write a letter of complaint.

She was intellectually precocious. By her mid-teens, she had read Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* and Kierkegaard. When she was expelled from her school for leading a boycott of a teacher who had insulted her, she studied on her own and passed the university entrance exam early.

### Heidegger and Jaspers

In **1924**, at eighteen, she went to the University of **Marburg** to study with **Martin Heidegger**, a brilliant young philosopher who was becoming famous. He was thirty-five and married. They began a secret love affair. It ended after a year or so, but the relationship would shadow her life, especially after Heidegger joined the Nazi Party in **1933**.

She moved to **Heidelberg** to study with **Karl Jaspers**, a humane existentialist philosopher who became her lifelong friend and mentor. Her doctoral dissertation, completed in **1929**, was on **the concept of love in Saint Augustine**.

### Arrested

When Hitler took power in **1933**, Arendt decided she could no longer be a spectator. "If one is attacked as a Jew," she later said, "one must defend oneself as a Jew." She agreed to help a Zionist organisation collect evidence of antisemitic propaganda from libraries.

She was caught and arrested by the **Gestapo**, along with her mother. She was held for about **eight days**. A young police officer, who liked her, helped her get released. She immediately fled Germany, without papers, crossing the border into Czechoslovakia through the house of a sympathetic family, then on to Geneva and **Paris**.

### Paris and the camp

In Paris, she worked for organisations helping Jewish refugees and **Youth Aliyah**, which sent Jewish children to Palestine. She met the German writer **Walter Benjamin**, and a self-taught former communist, **Heinrich Blücher**, who became her second husband.

In **1937**, Germany stripped her of her citizenship. She was now **stateless**.

In **May 1940**, as Germany invaded France, the French government interned German refugees, including Jews who had fled the Nazis, as "enemy aliens". Arendt was sent to **Gurs**, a camp in the south of France. In the chaos after France's defeat, she walked out with a few other women. Those who stayed were later handed over to the Nazis and deported to the death camps.

She found Blücher, and they made their way across Spain to **Lisbon**. Walter Benjamin, trying to escape by the same route, killed himself at the Spanish border when he was told he would be sent back. He had given Arendt a manuscript to carry to safety.

### New York

In **May 1941**, Arendt and Blücher arrived in **New York** with very little money and almost no English. She learned the language, worked as a journalist and an editor, and worked for organisations rescuing Jewish cultural treasures from Europe. In **1951**, after fourteen years of statelessness, she became a **United States citizen**. The same year, she published ***The Origins of Totalitarianism***, which made her famous.

She taught at universities including Chicago, Princeton and the New School in New York, and published ***The Human Condition*** (**1958**) and ***On Revolution*** (**1963**).

### Jerusalem

In **1960**, Israeli agents captured **Adolf Eichmann** in Argentina, where he had been living under a false name, and brought him to **Jerusalem** for trial. Arendt asked *The New Yorker* to send her to report on it.

Her reports, published as ***Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil*** (**1963**), caused a firestorm. Many Jews were furious at the subtitle, and especially at her brief, sharp criticism of the Jewish councils that the Nazis had forced to help organise deportations. Old friends broke with her. The scholar **Gershom Scholem** accused her of lacking love for the Jewish people. She replied that she had never in her life loved any people or collective, only her friends; the only kind of love she knew was love of persons.

### Judging

In her last years, she worked on ***The Life of the Mind***, a study of **thinking**, **willing** and **judging**. She completed the first two parts. On **4 December 1975**, while having dinner with friends in her New York apartment, she had a heart attack and died, aged sixty-nine. In her typewriter was a sheet of paper with the title of the third part, **"Judging"**, and two quotations. The rest was blank.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Totalitarianism is something new

**The idea in one line:** Nazi Germany and Stalin's Soviet Union were not ordinary tyrannies but a new form of government that sought total domination of every aspect of human life, through ideology and terror.

People had known tyrants for thousands of years: kings, dictators, emperors who ruled by fear. Arendt argued that **totalitarianism** was different.

- A tyrant wants obedience. A totalitarian regime wants **total domination**: not just your actions but your thoughts, your friendships, your memories, your very identity.
- A tyrant uses terror against opponents. A totalitarian regime uses terror against **anyone**, including loyal supporters, because terror is not a means but the essence of the system.
- A tyrant has interests. A totalitarian regime is driven by an **ideology**, a single idea, about race or class or the laws of history, that claims to explain everything and must be proved true, whatever the cost.

The ultimate laboratory of totalitarianism, she said, was the **concentration camp**, where human beings were reduced to bundles of reactions, stripped of their identity, their choices, their individuality. The goal was to make human beings **superfluous**.

**Why it matters:** Arendt showed that the horrors of the twentieth century were not just "more of the same" cruelty, but a new danger that grew out of modern society itself: mass movements, propaganda, bureaucracy and technology.

**A common misunderstanding:** Arendt did not say every authoritarian government is totalitarian. She used the word strictly, for regimes like Hitler's and Stalin's. Using it loosely for every bad government, she would say, blurs important differences.

### Idea 2: Loneliness and lies

**The idea in one line:** Totalitarian movements feed on lonely, uprooted people, and work by destroying the shared sense of what is real and true.

Who joins totalitarian movements? Not, Arendt said, mainly the fanatics. They draw on **masses** of people who feel uprooted, lonely and superfluous: unemployed, cut off from community, with no stake in the common world. Such people are drawn to a movement that gives them belonging and a simple explanation of everything.

And the movement works by destroying the **common world** of facts. It tells lies so huge and so often that people give up trying to tell truth from falsehood. They believe everything and nothing. They become cynical and gullible at the same time. As she wrote: **"The ideal subject of totalitarian rule is not the convinced Nazi or the convinced Communist, but people for whom the distinction between fact and fiction and the distinction between true and false no longer exist."**

**Why it matters:** This is why Arendt's book became a best-seller again in the age of social media, "fake news" and "post-truth" politics. When a WhatsApp rumour about child-kidnappers led to the lynching of innocent strangers in several Indian villages in **2018**, it showed how quickly a crowd that cannot tell fact from fiction can turn deadly.

**A common misunderstanding:** Arendt did not blame lonely people for totalitarianism. She saw loneliness, being cut off from others and from a shared world, as a social condition that modern societies produce, and that dictators exploit.

### Idea 3: The right to have rights

**The idea in one line:** Human rights mean nothing to people who belong to no political community; the most basic right is the right to belong somewhere, to be a citizen.

The great declarations of the eighteenth century spoke of the "rights of man": rights that belong to every human being by nature. Arendt, a stateless refugee, saw the bitter truth. When millions of people lost their citizenship in the twentieth century, they found that these "natural" rights protected them not at all. No government would enforce them. A stateless person had only the rights that someone chose to give them.

The paradox, she wrote, is that the moment human beings were only human, with no nationality, no citizenship, nothing but their bare humanity, was exactly the moment they lost all their rights. So the most fundamental right is **"the right to have rights"**: the right to belong to a political community in which one's actions and opinions count.

**Why it matters:** This idea is central to debates about refugees and statelessness today. India knows these questions intimately: the millions uprooted by **Partition** in **1947**; Tibetan refugees living in India since **1959**; Rohingya refugees from Myanmar; and people in **Assam** left off the final **National Register of Citizens** in **2019**, about 1.9 million, whose citizenship came into question. Each case raises Arendt's question: what are rights worth without a community that recognises you?

**A common misunderstanding:** Arendt did not reject human rights. She argued that they must be anchored in political communities and international institutions, not just in declarations about human nature.

### Idea 4: Labour, work and action

**The idea in one line:** Human activity has three levels: labour to survive, work to build a lasting world, and action, speaking and acting together in public, where freedom lives.

In ***The Human Condition***, Arendt distinguishes three kinds of human activity:

1. **Labour:** what we do to stay alive: growing and cooking food, cleaning, the endless cycle of biological needs. Its products are consumed almost as soon as they are made. Labour never ends.
2. **Work:** making lasting things: houses, tools, furniture, books, roads, works of art. Work builds a durable human **world** that outlasts individual lives and gives us a common home.
3. **Action:** speaking and acting together with others in **public**: debating, organising, deciding, beginning something new. Action reveals **who** we are, not just what we can do. It is where human freedom lives.

She worried that modern society had lost sight of action, and even of work. Everything was becoming labour and consumption: we work to earn, earn to consume, and consume to keep the economy going. Politics, too, was becoming mere administration, the management of needs, rather than free citizens deciding together.

**Why it matters:** This raises a powerful question about modern life: are we living only as labourers and consumers? Do we ever **act**, in the public sense, with others, to shape our shared world?

**A common misunderstanding:** Arendt did not despise labour or the people who do it. She thought all three activities are necessary. Her worry was that a society organised entirely around labour and consumption leaves no room for freedom.

### Idea 5: Natality, the power to begin

**The idea in one line:** Every human being, by being born, is a new beginning, and so has the capacity to start something new in the world.

Most philosophers since Plato have focused on **death**: on our mortality, and how to face it. Arendt focused on **birth**. She called it **natality**.

She took the idea from **Augustine**, who wrote: "that a beginning be made, man was created". Each person is born unique, never existed before, and will never exist again. So each person can **begin** something genuinely new, something unpredictable, that no one could have foreseen.

This is why action is so powerful. A single person, or a small group, can start something that changes history: a movement, a new institution, a revolution, an idea.

**"The miracle that saves the world... from its normal, 'natural' ruin is ultimately the fact of natality."**

**Why it matters:** Natality is a philosophy of hope. However bad things are, new people are born, and with them, new beginnings. Every generation can begin again.

**A common misunderstanding:** Natality is not about having children. It is about the capacity, which every human being has because they were born, to begin something new.

### Idea 6: Power is not violence

**The idea in one line:** Power comes from people acting together; violence is what rulers use when they have lost power, and it can destroy power but never create it.

Most people think of **power** as the ability to force others to obey: guns, police, armies. Arendt argued this is a mistake.

**Power**, she said, arises whenever people **act together**. It belongs to a group, not an individual, and lasts only as long as the group holds together. A government has power only so long as people support it.

**Violence**, in contrast, uses tools, weapons, to force people. It can be used by a few against many. And it is the **opposite** of power: **"Power and violence are opposites; where the one rules absolutely, the other is absent."** When rulers turn to violence, it is a sign they are losing power. **"Violence can destroy power; it is utterly incapable of creating it."**

India's history offers a striking example. In **1919**, General Dyer ordered his troops to fire on an unarmed crowd at **Jallianwala Bagh** in Amritsar, killing hundreds. It was a display of violence. But it destroyed British power in India: it turned millions against British rule, and Rabindranath Tagore gave up his knighthood in protest. A year later, Gandhi launched the **Non-Cooperation Movement**: millions of people acting together, refusing to cooperate. That was power.

**Why it matters:** This distinction helps explain why non-violent movements so often succeed, and why regimes that rely on violence are often weaker than they look.

**A common misunderstanding:** Arendt was not a pacifist. She thought violence is sometimes justified, in self-defence or against tyranny. And she was realistic about its limits: she wrote that if Gandhi's strategy of non-violent resistance had faced Stalin's Russia or Hitler's Germany instead of Britain, the outcome would have been "not decolonization, but massacre and submission".

### Idea 7: The banality of evil

**The idea in one line:** Some of the greatest evils are done not by monsters but by ordinary people who do not think about what they are doing.

At Eichmann's trial, Arendt expected to see a demon. Instead, she saw a man who was, as she put it, "terrifyingly normal". He was not stupid, but he seemed incapable of **thinking**: of stopping to consider what his actions meant, from the point of view of the people they affected. He spoke in clichés and bureaucratic phrases. "Officialese is my only language," he told the court. He was proud of his efficiency. He had followed orders and the law, which in Nazi Germany was the will of the Führer.

This is what she called **the banality of evil**: not that the Holocaust was banal, but that one of its chief organisers was. He did evil not out of deep hatred but out of **thoughtlessness**, careerism and obedience.

**Why it matters:** This changed how we think about evil. Great crimes need not just a few monsters but thousands of ordinary clerks, soldiers and officials who do their jobs without asking questions. Psychological experiments around the same time, like **Stanley Milgram**'s studies of obedience (from **1961**), showed how easily ordinary people follow orders to harm others.

**A common misunderstanding:** "The banality of evil" does not mean evil is unimportant or that Eichmann was innocent. Arendt agreed he deserved to be executed. She meant that evil can be committed by people who are shallow rather than demonic, which makes it more dangerous, because it can be anyone.

### Idea 8: Thinking as a defence against evil

**The idea in one line:** The habit of thinking, of stopping to talk with yourself about what you are doing, may prevent you from taking part in evil.

If Eichmann's evil came from thoughtlessness, could thinking prevent it? In her last work, Arendt turned to **Socrates**. Socrates described thinking as a **silent dialogue** with oneself. And he said he would rather suffer wrong than do wrong, because if he did wrong, he would have to live with himself, a wrongdoer, forever. Thinking means keeping company with yourself; a person who thinks does not want to become someone they could not bear to live with.

She also drew on **Kant** to describe **judgment**: the ability to see a situation from the point of view of others, to "go visiting" in imagination, and so to judge particular cases well without a rulebook.

**Why it matters:** In a world of orders, procedures, algorithms and social pressure, the simple habit of asking "What am I really doing? Could I live with myself afterwards?" is a powerful defence against taking part in harm.

**A common misunderstanding:** Arendt did not think professional philosophers are better people. Thinking, in her sense, is open to everyone; and many brilliant intellectuals, including her teacher Heidegger, failed to think when it mattered.

### Idea 9: Forgiveness and promises

**The idea in one line:** Because actions cannot be undone and their results cannot be predicted, human beings need two powers: forgiveness, to release the past, and promises, to bind the future.

Action is powerful but dangerous. Once we act, we cannot undo it (**irreversibility**), and we cannot know where it will lead (**unpredictability**).

Arendt said humanity has two remedies:

- **Forgiveness** releases people from the consequences of what they have done, so that they and others can start again. Without it, we would be trapped forever by a single act.
- **Promising** creates islands of certainty in an uncertain future. Contracts, treaties, constitutions, marriages and vows are promises that let people act together over time.

**Why it matters:** These are the foundations of every relationship, every community and every political order: the ability to forgive and the ability to keep one's word.

**A common misunderstanding:** Forgiveness, for Arendt, is not forgetting or excusing. And she thought some crimes, like those of the Holocaust, go beyond what can be forgiven or punished.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Augustine: love and beginning

Arendt's first book was on **Augustine**'s concept of love. She asked how a Christian who loves God above all can still love their neighbour in this world. Later she took from him the idea of natality: a human being is a beginning. Augustine looked to the City of God; Arendt cared most for the earthly, public world that people share.

### Socrates: the thinker in the marketplace

**Socrates**, who thought in public, questioned everyone, and would rather suffer wrong than do it, was Arendt's model of thinking. She saw him not as a man with doctrines but as a "gadfly" and a "midwife", helping others to think for themselves.

### Kant: judgment

Arendt grew up in Kant's city and read him as a teenager. In her late lectures, she found in his ideas about aesthetic taste a model for political judgment: thinking from the point of view of others, and judging particular cases without rigid rules.

### Marx: labour is not everything

Arendt took **Marx** seriously but argued that he made **labour** the essence of human life, and so had no room for **action**, the free public life of citizens. A society of liberated labourers, she warned, would still be a society of consumers, not of free citizens.

### Luxemburg: the revolution and freedom

Arendt admired **Rosa Luxemburg**, whom her mother had followed, and wrote an essay about her. She shared Luxemburg's faith in the spontaneous councils that ordinary people form in revolutions, and Luxemburg's warning that a revolution without freedom of debate would become a dictatorship.

### Sartre and Fanon: the glorification of violence

In ***On Violence*** (1970), she criticised **Jean-Paul Sartre**'s preface to Frantz Fanon's *The Wretched of the Earth*, which praised violence as a way for the colonised to remake themselves. Violence, she replied, can achieve short-term goals, but it cannot create power, and its means overwhelm its ends.

### Gandhi: non-violent power

Arendt saw **Gandhi**'s movement as a great example of power, people acting together, defeating an empire. But she doubted it could work against a totalitarian regime. Imagine them talking:

*Gandhi:* Non-violence is the greatest force at the disposal of mankind. It can melt the hardest heart.

*Arendt:* It worked against Britain, which had a parliament, a press and a conscience to appeal to. Against Hitler or Stalin, it would have met massacre.

*Gandhi:* Then the Jews of Germany should have offered satyagraha, even unto death.

*Arendt:* I have seen what the camps did. They were designed to destroy the very possibility of a meaningful sacrifice.

(Gandhi did in fact advise German Jews to practise non-violent resistance, in **1938**; many Jewish thinkers, including the philosopher **Martin Buber**, replied that he did not understand what they faced.)

### Heidegger: the teacher who failed

Arendt's relationship with **Martin Heidegger**, her teacher and lover, who joined the Nazi Party, is one of the most debated stories in philosophy. After the war, she resumed contact with him and helped publish his work in America. Some see this as forgiveness; others as a blind spot.

### Later voices

- **Jürgen Habermas** built on her ideas of the public sphere and communicative power.
- **Václav Havel** and dissidents in communist Eastern Europe drew on her ideas of truth and action.
- After **2016**, journalists and scholars around the world returned to her work to understand populism, propaganda and post-truth politics.

## See it around you

**"I was just doing my job."** Officials who carry out unjust orders, employees who sign off on dangerous products, soldiers who follow unlawful commands: the banality of evil in everyday clothes.

**WhatsApp rumours and mob violence.** When people can no longer tell fact from fiction, rumours can kill. Arendt saw this as the fertile ground of tyranny.

**Refugees and the stateless.** From Partition's refugees to Tibetans, Rohingya and those left off citizenship registers, the right to have rights is still a live question.

**Non-violent movements.** From the Salt March to Chipko to peaceful protests worldwide, people acting together show Arendt's idea of power.

**Violence as weakness.** When a government turns to firing on crowds, internet shutdowns or mass arrests, Arendt would say it is showing not strength but a loss of power.

**Labour and consumption.** A life of work-earn-spend-scroll, with no time to act in public, is the life Arendt worried modern society produces.

**Gram sabhas and town halls.** Village assemblies, student councils and residents' associations are small public spaces where citizens can act together.

**New beginnings.** Every young person who starts a movement, an organisation or a new idea is an example of natality.

**Apologies and promises.** Truth and reconciliation commissions, peace treaties, constitutions: forgiveness and promising at a national scale.

## Use it

**1. Stop and think.** Before you follow an instruction at work, in a group or online, ask: what am I actually doing? Could I live with myself afterwards?

**2. Guard the line between fact and fiction.** Check before you forward. Refuse to give up on truth, even when it is tiring.

**3. Act with others.** Find a public space, a residents' association, a panchayat, a club, a campaign, where you can speak and act together with others.

**4. Remember you can begin.** Natality means you can start something new, at any age. What new thing could you begin?

**5. Don't confuse violence with strength.** In families, offices and politics, people who shout, threaten or hit are often showing weakness. Real power comes from people choosing to work together.

**6. Visit other points of view.** Before judging, imagine the situation from the standpoint of everyone affected.

**7. Forgive, and keep promises.** Release others, and yourself, from past wrongs where you can. And make your promises count.

**8. Watch out for loneliness.** Isolated people are vulnerable to extremism and manipulation. Reach out to those who seem cut off.

## Where she went wrong

**1. The Jewish councils.** Her brief, sharp criticism of Jewish leaders who cooperated with the Nazis, in *Eichmann in Jerusalem*, was seen by many as unfair, cold and ignorant of the impossible choices they faced. Even admirers think her tone was harsh.

**2. Was Eichmann really "banal"?** Later historians, studying documents that came to light after the trial, including recordings Eichmann made in Argentina, have shown that he was a committed antisemite who was proud of his role. He may have played the dull bureaucrat in court. Arendt may have been deceived.

**3. Little Rock.** In **1959**, she wrote an essay criticising the forced desegregation of schools in the American South, arguing that children should not be put on the front line of political battles. Black writers like **Ralph Ellison** replied that she did not understand the experience of Black families, and she later admitted she had misjudged.

**4. Blind spots about Africa and colonialism.** In *The Origins of Totalitarianism*, her descriptions of African peoples draw on racist colonial sources. Critics argue that she treated colonialism mainly as a forerunner of European catastrophe, rather than as a catastrophe for the colonised.

**5. The private and the social.** Her sharp division between public action and private life has been criticised by feminists: by placing household labour and the body outside politics, she seemed to dismiss issues, like domestic violence, poverty and care work, that are deeply political.

**6. Heidegger.** Her continued loyalty to a teacher who supported the Nazis troubles many readers.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought that great evil is done by monsters, that power means guns and armies, that human rights belong to everyone automatically, and that politics is something governments do. You may have known Arendt only for the phrase "the banality of evil".

**Now,** you can see that evil can be done by ordinary people who stop thinking; that totalitarianism feeds on loneliness and the collapse of truth; that rights depend on belonging to a community; that power is people acting together, and violence its opposite; that each of us, by being born, can begin something new; and that thinking, judging, forgiving and promising are the habits that keep a shared world alive. And you can see her blind spots: harsh judgments, a possibly mistaken reading of Eichmann, and a narrow vision of what counts as political.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **"I was just following orders"** becomes a warning sign, not an excuse.
- **A rumour on your phone** becomes a test of whether you can still tell fact from fiction.
- **A refugee in the news** raises the question of the right to have rights.
- **A government firing on a crowd** looks like a loss of power, not a show of strength.
- **A residents' meeting or a gram sabha** becomes a small space of freedom.
- **A newborn** becomes a reminder that the world can always begin again.

A girl from Kant's city who was arrested by the Gestapo, lived without a country, walked out of a camp, and arrived in New York with nothing, spent her life trying to understand how ordinary people could do unimaginable evil. Her answer was not despair but a call to think, to judge, to act together, and to begin.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Hannah Arendt (1906–1975), German-born American political thinker; theorist of totalitarianism, action and the banality of evil.
- **Context:** Weimar Germany; the rise of Nazism and Stalinism; the Holocaust; mass statelessness; the Cold War.
- **Life:** grew up in Königsberg; studied with Heidegger and Jaspers; dissertation on Augustine (1929); arrested by the Gestapo and fled (1933); stateless (1937–51); interned at Gurs and escaped (1940); New York (1941); *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951); *The Human Condition* (1958); *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963); died in New York (1975).
- **Totalitarianism:** total domination through ideology and terror, a new form of government.
- **Loneliness and lies:** the masses and the collapse of the line between fact and fiction.
- **The right to have rights:** rights depend on belonging to a political community.
- **Labour, work, action:** survival, world-building, and free public action.
- **Natality:** every birth is a new beginning.
- **Power vs violence:** power is acting together; violence destroys it and cannot create it.
- **Banality of evil:** evil done by thoughtless, ordinary people.
- **Thinking and judging:** the silent dialogue with oneself; seeing from others' standpoints.
- **Forgiveness and promises:** remedies for irreversibility and unpredictability.
- **Criticisms:** the Jewish councils, Eichmann's real antisemitism, Little Rock, racist sources on Africa, the public–private divide, Heidegger.

**Key terms**

- **Totalitarianism:** a regime seeking total domination of all aspects of life through ideology and terror.
- **Statelessness:** having no citizenship in any country.
- **The right to have rights:** the right to belong to a political community.
- **Labour, work, action:** Arendt's three forms of human activity.
- **Natality:** the human capacity to begin something new, rooted in birth.
- **Plurality:** the fact that people, not one "Man", live on the earth, each unique.
- **Power:** the human ability to act together.
- **Banality of evil:** evil committed by ordinary people through thoughtlessness rather than deep malice.
- **Judgment:** the ability to assess particular situations from the standpoint of others.
