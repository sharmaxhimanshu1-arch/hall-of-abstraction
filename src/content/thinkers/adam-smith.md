## Life

Adam Smith was baptised in Kirkcaldy, a small port on the Firth of Forth in Scotland, in June 1723; his father, a customs official, had died months before. At fourteen he went to the University of Glasgow, where the moral philosopher **Francis Hutcheson** made a deep impression on him. He then spent six largely wasted years at Balliol College, Oxford, where, he later wrote, most professors had "given up altogether even the pretence of teaching."

After giving successful public lectures in Edinburgh, he became professor of logic and then of moral philosophy at Glasgow in the early 1750s. These years were among the happiest of his life. He became the closest friend of **David Hume**, and in 1759 published *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, which made his reputation.

In 1764 he resigned his chair to become travelling tutor to the young Duke of Buccleuch, a post that paid far better. In France he met Voltaire and the leading French economists, the Physiocrats François Quesnay and Anne-Robert-Jacques Turgot. Returning to Kirkcaldy, he spent about ten years writing *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*, published in March 1776, the year of the American Declaration of Independence. In 1778 he was appointed Commissioner of Customs in Edinburgh. He never married, lived much of his life with his mother, and was famously absent-minded. Shortly before his death in 1790 he had his friends burn sixteen volumes of unfinished manuscripts.

## Key ideas

**Sympathy and the impartial spectator.** *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* begins from our natural capacity for **sympathy**, imaginatively entering into the feelings of others. We learn to judge our own conduct by imagining how an "impartial spectator" would see it. Morality grows from this social mirror, not from self-interest alone.

**The division of labour.** *The Wealth of Nations* opens with a pin factory. One worker alone might make barely one pin a day, but ten workers, each specialising in a few operations, could make 48,000. The division of labour, limited by the extent of the market, is the great engine of productivity.

**Self-interest and markets.** In exchange, we address ourselves "not to their humanity but to their self-love." Prices in a competitive market signal what people want and coordinate the work of millions. An individual pursuing his own gain is "led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention." The phrase appears only once in the book.

**Against mercantilism.** National wealth is not a stockpile of gold and silver but the flow of goods that people can consume. Tariffs, monopolies and colonial trade restrictions mostly benefit merchants and manufacturers at the public's expense.

**A role for government.** Smith was no simple advocate of laissez-faire. The state must provide defence, justice and public works, including roads, canals and public education. He warned that business interests constantly conspire to raise prices, and that repetitive factory work could make workers "as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become" unless education counteracted it.

## Influence and legacy

*The Wealth of Nations* founded economics as a discipline. David Ricardo, Thomas Malthus, John Stuart Mill and Karl Marx all built on or argued with it. Smith's case for free trade shaped British policy in the nineteenth century, and he became an icon for free-market thinkers such as Friedrich Hayek and Milton Friedman.

Recent scholarship stresses that Smith the economist and Smith the moral philosopher are one thinker, concerned throughout with sympathy, justice and the condition of the poor. His portrait appeared on the Bank of England £20 note from 2007 to 2020.
