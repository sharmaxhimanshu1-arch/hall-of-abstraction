## Start here

Have you ever said, "That's just how I am"?

*I'm shy, I can't speak in public.* *I'm bad at maths, always have been.* *I have a short temper, it runs in the family.* *I'm not the creative type.* *I had no choice, my parents decided.* *What could I do? The boss told me to.*

These sentences feel natural. They describe us. They also do something sneaky: they let us off the hook. If I'm "just" shy, I don't have to try speaking up. If I "had no choice", I'm not responsible.

Now imagine a philosopher who looks at you and says, gently but firmly: **"No. You are not 'just' anything. You are not a table or a stone, with a fixed nature. You are a free being. You chose, and you go on choosing, every day, to be shy, to avoid maths, to lose your temper, to obey. You could choose otherwise. And you are responsible for what you make of yourself."**

That might sound harsh. It might also sound strangely exciting. If I'm not fixed, I can change. If I made myself, I can remake myself.

That philosopher was **Jean-Paul Sartre**, the most famous intellectual of the twentieth century. In 1945, in a packed lecture hall in liberated Paris, where people fainted in the crush, he declared that there is no human nature given in advance, no God who designed us, no fixed essence that tells us what to be. "**Existence precedes essence**": first we exist, and then, through our choices, we make ourselves.

He said we are "**condemned to be free**": we didn't choose to be born, but once here, we can't escape choosing. Even not choosing is a choice. And we're responsible for all of it.

He wrote novels, plays, enormous philosophical books and political essays; he argued with presidents and revolutionaries; he marched with students, refused the Nobel Prize, and lived his whole adult life in cafés and hotel rooms, in an open partnership with Simone de Beauvoir. When he died, some fifty thousand people followed his coffin through Paris.

By the end of this page, you'll understand why "existentialism" became a word everyone knew, why Sartre's idea of freedom is both liberating and terrifying, and why you might never again say "that's just how I am" without a second thought.

## The world he was born into

### France between the wars

Sartre was born in **Paris** in **1905**. He grew up in the shadow of the **First World War** (1914–18), which killed some 1.4 million French soldiers and left a generation shattered. The old certainties, faith in progress, in civilisation, in God, in the nation, had been badly shaken.

Paris between the wars was the cultural capital of the world: artists, writers, musicians, jazz, surrealism. But beneath the excitement lay anxiety: economic depression in the 1930s, the rise of fascism in Italy and Germany, political violence in France itself.

### Occupation and Resistance

Then came the defining experience of Sartre's generation: the **Second World War**. In **1940**, Germany defeated France in six weeks. The north, including Paris, was **occupied** by the Nazis; the south was governed by the collaborationist **Vichy regime** under Marshal Pétain.

For four years, every French person faced daily choices. Collaborate with the occupiers, or resist? Inform on your neighbours, or hide them? Keep your head down, or risk your life? Jews were rounded up and deported to death camps, often with the help of French police. Some people joined the **Resistance**; most tried to survive.

Sartre later wrote, in a famous essay, that "we were never more free than under the German occupation". He meant: when every small act, every word, every silence could be a choice between collaboration and resistance, people felt the full weight of freedom and responsibility. The occupation made existentialism feel urgent and real.

### After the war

When Paris was liberated in **August 1944**, there was an explosion of hope, guilt and argument. Who had collaborated? What kind of France should be built? Then came the **Cold War** between the United States and the Soviet Union, and the wars of **decolonisation** as France fought to keep **Indochina** (Vietnam) and **Algeria**. Every intellectual had to take sides. Sartre took many, loudly.

### Phenomenology

Intellectually, Sartre's tools came from German philosophy, especially **phenomenology**, developed by **Edmund Husserl** and **Martin Heidegger**. Phenomenology tries to describe experience exactly as it appears to consciousness, without assumptions. Not "what is the world really made of?" but "what is it like to perceive, feel, imagine, choose?" Sartre used it to describe human existence from the inside.

## The life, as a story

### The little boy in the library

Jean-Paul Charles Aymard Sartre was born on **21 June 1905**. His father, a naval officer, died of fever when Jean-Paul was fifteen months old. His mother, **Anne-Marie**, moved back with her parents. His grandfather, **Charles Schweitzer**, a language teacher (and uncle of the famous doctor and humanitarian Albert Schweitzer), dominated the household.

The boy grew up surrounded by books, adored and spoiled. In his brilliant autobiography of childhood, ***The Words*** (1964), Sartre describes how he began his life "as I shall no doubt end it: among books". He decided early that he would be a writer, that writing would save him and justify his existence.

At about three, an infection left him nearly blind in his right eye, which wandered outward (strabismus). He was small (about five feet two) and, he felt, ugly. When his mother remarried in 1917, he disliked his stepfather and felt pushed aside. He was bullied at school in La Rochelle. He learned that he could win people over with his wit and intelligence.

### The École Normale and Beauvoir

Sartre studied at the elite **École Normale Supérieure** in Paris, where he was famous for pranks, songs and brilliant arguments. In **1928**, he **failed** the *agrégation*, the competitive national exam for philosophy teachers, apparently because he tried to be too original.

In **1929**, preparing to retake it, he met a brilliant young student named **Simone de Beauvoir**. They studied together. This time Sartre came **first**; Beauvoir came **second** (some examiners reportedly debated giving her first place). She was twenty-one, the youngest person ever to pass.

They became partners for life, but on unusual terms. They agreed that their love was "**essential**", but that they would also be free to have "**contingent**" love affairs with others, and would tell each other everything. They never married, never lived together, and never had children. They addressed each other formally as *vous*. Their partnership lasted fifty-one years, until Sartre's death.

### Teacher and traveller

Sartre taught philosophy at a lycée in **Le Havre**, a port town he found dull. A famous story: in **1933**, at a café, his friend **Raymond Aron**, just back from Germany, pointed to his apricot cocktail and said that a phenomenologist could make philosophy out of that cocktail. Sartre, Beauvoir recalled, turned pale with excitement. This was exactly what he wanted: philosophy about real, concrete experience.

He went to **Berlin** in **1933–34** to study Husserl, in the year Hitler came to power, though he paid surprisingly little attention to politics at the time.

### Nausea

In **1938** he published his first novel, ***Nausea***. Its hero, **Antoine Roquentin**, a lonely historian in a provincial town, is overwhelmed by a strange feeling: **nausea** at the sheer, meaningless existence of things. In the most famous scene, he sits in a park staring at the root of a **chestnut tree**, and suddenly sees it as it is: a black, knotty mass, existing for no reason, "**de trop**" (too much, superfluous). Everything exists without reason, without necessity, without justification. Including himself.

It's a novel about discovering that existence has no built-in meaning. It made Sartre's name.

### War and prison camp

In **1939**, Sartre was conscripted as a meteorologist in the army. In **June 1940**, he was captured by the Germans and spent nine months in a **prisoner-of-war camp** at Trier (Marx's birthplace, as it happens). There he wrote and staged a play for his fellow prisoners, taught philosophy, and experienced, he said, a kind of solidarity he had never known. Released in **1941** (posing as a civilian with poor eyesight), he returned to Paris, tried to start a resistance group, which fizzled out, and went back to teaching and writing.

### Being and Nothingness

In **1943**, under the occupation, he published his philosophical masterwork, ***Being and Nothingness***: over 700 pages of dense, brilliant analysis of consciousness, freedom, bad faith and relations with others. Legend says some Parisians bought copies because the book weighed exactly one kilo, useful for weighing goods on scales when official weights had been melted down for war materials.

The same year, his play ***The Flies*** was staged in occupied Paris, a retelling of the Greek myth of Orestes, which audiences read as a call to resistance and freedom. In **1944** came ***No Exit***, with its famous line, "Hell is other people."

He wrote much of this sitting in Paris cafés, especially the **Café de Flore** in Saint-Germain-des-Prés, which was warm when his hotel room wasn't.

### The existentialist craze

After the liberation, Sartre became suddenly, astonishingly famous. On **29 October 1945**, he gave a public lecture, "**Existentialism Is a Humanism**", at the Club Maintenant in Paris. The crowd was so huge that people fought to get in, chairs were broken, and some fainted. The lecture, published the next year, became the most widely read introduction to existentialism.

"Existentialism" became a fashion. Young people in black polo necks gathered in Saint-Germain cafés and jazz cellars, calling themselves existentialists. Newspapers wrote about the "existentialist" lifestyle. Sartre was both amused and annoyed.

In **1945**, he and Beauvoir founded the journal ***Les Temps modernes*** (Modern Times), which became one of the most influential publications in France.

### Engagement

After the war, Sartre argued that writers must be "**engaged**" (*engagé*): they must take political responsibility, using their words to act in the world. He threw himself into politics:

- He moved closer to **Marxism** and, for some years in the early 1950s, became a "fellow traveller" of the French Communist Party, defending the Soviet Union, though he never joined.
- He had a famous public **break with Albert Camus** in **1952**, after Camus's book *The Rebel* criticised revolutionary violence and Soviet camps. Their friendship ended.
- He broke with the Communists after the Soviet invasion of **Hungary** in **1956**.
- He fiercely opposed France's war in **Algeria**, supporting Algerian independence. His apartment was bombed twice by French right-wing extremists in 1961–62.
- He wrote a famous preface to **Frantz Fanon**'s *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), a key text of anti-colonial thought.
- He opposed the American war in **Vietnam** and took part in the **Russell Tribunal** on war crimes.
- In **May 1968**, he supported the student uprisings. When he was arrested for distributing a banned Maoist newspaper, President **de Gaulle** reportedly said: "One does not arrest Voltaire."

### Critique and Flaubert

He tried to combine existentialism and Marxism in the huge ***Critique of Dialectical Reason*** (1960), writing in its introductory essay that Marxism was "the philosophy of our time", which could not be surpassed while the conditions that produced it remained. He spent years on a vast, unfinished, multi-volume study of the novelist **Gustave Flaubert**, *The Family Idiot*, trying to understand how a person becomes who they are.

### The refused Nobel

In **1964**, Sartre was awarded the **Nobel Prize in Literature**. He **refused** it. He said that a writer should not allow himself to be turned into an institution, and that accepting such honours would compromise his independence. He had refused France's Legion of Honour too.

### Blindness and death

By **1973**, Sartre was almost completely **blind**, after a series of strokes and the loss of sight in his good eye. He could no longer read or write. He continued to give interviews and talk. He had smoked, drunk heavily, and taken amphetamines for years to keep writing.

He died on **15 April 1980**, in Paris. About **fifty thousand** people followed his funeral procession through the streets. Beauvoir was buried beside him six years later, in **Montparnasse Cemetery**.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Existence precedes essence

**The idea in one line:** Human beings have no fixed nature or purpose given in advance; we first exist, and then define ourselves through our choices.

This is the core of existentialism. Sartre explains it with a simple example in *Existentialism Is a Humanism*.

Think of a **paper-knife** (a knife used to cut open pages or envelopes). Before it is made, the craftsman has an idea of it: what it's for, how it should look. The idea, the **essence** (what it is, its purpose), comes **first**. Then the knife is made to fit the idea. For a paper-knife, **essence precedes existence**.

For centuries, many people thought humans were like that too. God, like a craftsman, had an idea of human nature in his mind, and created us according to it. Our purpose was given in advance. Even many atheist philosophers kept the idea of a fixed "**human nature**" shared by all people.

Sartre says: if there is no God, there is no such idea, no design, no fixed human nature. Man "first of all exists, encounters himself, surges up in the world, and defines himself afterwards". At first, he is nothing. "He will not be anything until later, and then he will be what he makes of himself."

"**Man is nothing else but what he makes of himself.**"

Think of how people say "he's a born leader", "she's naturally caring", "they're lazy by nature", "boys will be boys", "women are naturally emotional", "people from that community are like this". Sartre says: no. There is no "born" anything. What someone is, is the total of what they do. A coward is someone who acts in cowardly ways, and could choose otherwise. A hero is someone who acts heroically, and could still choose to stop.

Compare this with the ideas of **svabhava** and **svadharma** in the Bhagavad Gita, the idea that each person has an inborn nature and a duty fitting it, which was later tied, in many traditions, to caste and birth. Sartre's idea is radically opposed: nothing about your birth fixes what you must be. Ambedkar's insistence that a person's worth must be judged by conduct and not by birth sits much closer to Sartre than to that reading of svadharma.

**Why it matters:** This idea is liberating. It means no one is trapped by "nature", destiny or birth. You can change. It also places enormous responsibility on you: you can't blame God, nature, genes or society for what you make of yourself.

**A common misunderstanding:** Sartre didn't deny that we have bodies, histories, social conditions and limitations. He called these our "**facticity**": the facts of our situation that we didn't choose (our birth, body, past, country). But he insisted that we always **interpret** and **respond** to our facticity freely. Being born poor or disabled is a fact; what you make of it is your choice. (Later, Beauvoir and Sartre himself would admit that some situations limit freedom much more than he first allowed.)

### Idea 2: Condemned to be free

**The idea in one line:** We cannot escape freedom; we are always choosing, and we are responsible for our choices.

"**Man is condemned to be free.** Condemned, because he did not create himself, yet is nevertheless at liberty, and from the moment that he is thrown into this world he is responsible for everything he does."

Why "condemned"? Because freedom isn't something we chose. We're thrown into it. And we can't escape it. Even when we refuse to choose, we've chosen not to choose. Even when we obey orders, we've chosen to obey. Even suicide is a choice.

Sartre took this to extreme lengths. He argued that even in prison, under torture, a person is free: free to decide how to respond, whether to give in or resist. He said that the soldier who goes to war has chosen the war, in a sense, since he could have deserted or killed himself. These claims sound extreme, and he later modified them. But the core point is powerful: **you are always choosing**, even when you tell yourself you aren't.

Think of a common excuse: "I had no choice; my parents forced me to study engineering." Sartre would say: you had a choice. It might have been very hard; you might have faced anger, loss of support, conflict. But you chose to accept, because the alternative seemed worse. That was your choice, and it's yours to own.

**Why it matters:** This idea changes how we think about excuses. It makes us take responsibility for our lives. It's also a powerful tool against injustice: officials who say "I was only following orders" (as Nazi officials did at the Nuremberg trials) are not let off the hook. They chose to follow orders.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Condemned to be free" doesn't mean everyone has equal options. A billionaire's son and a child labourer face very different situations. Sartre's point is that within whatever situation you face, you still choose how to respond. Critics, including Beauvoir, argued he underestimated how much oppression limits freedom. Sartre's later work took this more seriously.

### Idea 3: Anguish, abandonment and despair

**The idea in one line:** Freedom brings three feelings: anguish at our responsibility, abandonment without God, and despair at what we can't control.

In *Existentialism Is a Humanism*, Sartre names three emotions that come with freedom.

**1. Anguish.** When I choose, I'm not only choosing for myself. Sartre says that "in choosing for himself he chooses for all men". By choosing how to live, I am creating an image of what a human being should be. If I marry, I'm saying marriage is good for humans. If I lie, I'm saying lying is acceptable. So every choice carries a huge weight. Sartre asks you to imagine: "What would happen if everyone did what I am doing?" That weight produces anguish. (Notice how this echoes Kant's universal law, though Sartre has no fixed rules to rely on.)

**2. Abandonment** (or "forlornness"). Without God, there's no one to tell us what's right. No commandments written in heaven, no guaranteed values. Sartre quotes Dostoevsky's character: "If God did not exist, everything would be permitted." That's the starting point of existentialism, he says. We are alone, without excuses.

Sartre gives a famous example. During the war, one of his students came to him with a dilemma. The young man's brother had been killed by the Germans; his father was a collaborator; he lived with his mother, who depended on him entirely. He wanted to escape to England to join the **Free French** forces and fight. But if he left, his mother would be devastated and alone.

What should he do? Christian ethics says love your neighbour, but which neighbour: his mother, or his country? Kant says treat people as ends, but if he stays, he treats the fighters as means; if he goes, his mother. No ethical system decides it.

Sartre's answer to the student: "You are free, therefore choose, that is to say, invent." No rule can decide for you. Even if you seek advice, you choose which adviser to ask, knowing more or less what they'll say. You must decide, and you're responsible.

**3. Despair.** We can only act on what's within our power. We can't control the future, other people, or the results of our actions. So we must act without hope of guarantees. Sartre says this means acting without illusions: "one need not hope in order to undertake one's work".

This connects to the **Bhagavad Gita**'s teaching: "You have a right to your action, but never to its fruits." Act fully, without depending on results. The Gita grounds this in a cosmic order; Sartre in its absence. Yet both teach action without attachment to guaranteed outcomes.

**Why it matters:** These three emotions describe what many people feel in modern life: the weight of decisions, the absence of clear guidance, the uncertainty of outcomes. Sartre doesn't offer comfort. He offers honesty, and the dignity of acting anyway.

**A common misunderstanding:** Existentialism is often thought of as gloomy, all anguish and despair. Sartre insisted the opposite: it's an **optimistic** philosophy, because it says human beings can always change, that destiny is in their hands. "It is a doctrine of action."

### Idea 4: Bad faith, the lie we tell ourselves

**The idea in one line:** We often deceive ourselves about our freedom, pretending we're fixed things in order to avoid the anguish of choice.

This is one of Sartre's most brilliant and useful ideas. **Bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*) is a kind of **self-deception**: lying to yourself about your own freedom.

Sartre gives two famous examples in *Being and Nothingness*.

**The waiter.** Sartre watches a café waiter. His movements are a little too precise, too quick, too eager. He bends forward a little too much, balances his tray with a little too much skill. He is **playing at being a waiter**. He's trying to turn himself into a "waiter", as if being a waiter were his fixed essence, like a stone is a stone. But he's not a waiter the way an inkwell is an inkwell. He is a free person who **chooses**, every morning, to get up and play this role. He could walk out. By losing himself in the role, he avoids facing his freedom.

Think of people who become their job title: "I'm a manager, managers don't do that." "As a doctor, I must..." "I'm a mother, I have no life of my own." These can be ways of hiding from freedom.

**The woman on a date.** A woman goes out with a man. She knows, at some level, what he wants. He takes her hand. If she leaves it there, she's accepting; if she withdraws it, she's refusing. To avoid deciding, she leaves her hand there but acts as if she hasn't noticed: she talks about lofty intellectual matters, treating her hand as if it were a thing, not part of her. She avoids choosing by pretending she's not choosing.

(Modern readers have found this example uncomfortable. The point Sartre was making is about evading choice, but the example also reflects his own gendered perspective, which Beauvoir would later analyse.)

Bad faith takes many forms:

- **"That's just how I am."** Treating your character as fixed, like a thing.
- **"I had no choice."** Denying your freedom.
- **"Everyone does it."** Hiding in the crowd.
- **"It's my destiny / my karma / my stars."** Blaming fate for your choices.
- **"I was only following orders."** Blaming authority.
- Or the opposite: **"I'm so free, my past doesn't matter."** Denying your facticity, the real facts of your situation.

**Why it matters:** Once you understand bad faith, you see it everywhere, in others and, uncomfortably, in yourself. It's a powerful tool for honesty. It also helps explain why so many people defend unjust systems: it's easier to say "that's how things are" than to accept that we're choosing to support them.

**A common misunderstanding:** Bad faith is not the same as lying to others. It's lying to yourself, about yourself, and it's subtle: you half-know the truth but refuse to face it. It's also not simply "being in a role". We all play roles. The bad faith lies in pretending we **are** the role, and have no choice.

### Idea 5: Being-in-itself and being-for-itself

**The idea in one line:** Things simply are what they are; human consciousness is never just what it is, but always reaching beyond itself.

This is the technical heart of *Being and Nothingness*. Sartre distinguishes two kinds of being:

- **Being-in-itself** (*être-en-soi*): the being of **things**. A stone, a table, a tree is simply what it is. It's solid, complete, full, without lack. It doesn't question itself or choose.
- **Being-for-itself** (*être-pour-soi*): the being of **consciousness**, of human beings. Consciousness is never simply what it is. It's always aware of itself, questioning, projecting toward a future, aware of what it lacks. It's a kind of "**nothingness**" at the heart of being: a gap, an openness.

Think of it this way. A stone doesn't want anything, doesn't imagine anything. But you are always "not yet" what you'll be, and "no longer" what you were. You're a student who's becoming a professional; a person who's hoping, planning, regretting. Your being is never complete. That incompleteness is your freedom.

Sartre says we long to be both at once: to have the solidity and security of a thing, and the freedom of consciousness. To be a free being that is also complete and justified. That, he says, is the desire to be **God**. And it's impossible. So "man is a useless passion".

**Why it matters:** This explains why bad faith is so tempting: we want to be solid things, with fixed identities, to escape the restless openness of consciousness. It also explains the endless restlessness of human desire: no achievement ever makes us complete.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Nothingness" doesn't mean emptiness or despair. It means the gap in consciousness that makes freedom possible: the ability to step back from any situation and ask, "what if it were otherwise?"

### Idea 6: The look, and hell is other people

**The idea in one line:** Other people's gaze turns us into objects, judging and defining us, so our relationships are full of conflict.

Sartre gives a vivid example in *Being and Nothingness*. Imagine you are crouching at a **keyhole**, peeping into a room, out of jealousy or curiosity. You're absorbed in what you see. You aren't thinking about yourself at all.

Suddenly you hear **footsteps** behind you. Someone is looking at you.

In that instant, everything changes. You feel **shame**. You suddenly see yourself as the other sees you: a peeping Tom, a snoop. You've become an **object** in someone else's world, defined by their gaze. You can't control how they see you.

Sartre called this "**the look**" (*le regard*). Other people's gaze turns us into objects, gives us an "outside" we can't control. This is the source of shame, pride, embarrassment, self-consciousness. And we do the same to them: we try to turn them into objects in our world.

So, Sartre argued, relations between people are fundamentally a **conflict** of freedoms: each trying to define the other, neither able to escape being defined. This is his version of Hegel's struggle between master and slave.

This idea is dramatised in his play ***No Exit*** (1944). Three dead people, a man and two women, find themselves in **hell**. But there are no flames or torturers, just a room with three ugly sofas. Slowly, they realise that **they** are each other's torturers: each one needs the others to see them a certain way, and each one is denied it. At the end, the man, Garcin, says: "**Hell is other people!**"

**Why it matters:** Think of social media: the constant awareness of being seen, liked, judged, commented on. People craft images of themselves for others' gaze, and feel crushed by others' judgement. Think of the pressure of **"log kya kahenge"**, what will people say: a whole society organised around the look. Sartre's analysis explains why being seen can feel like a prison.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Hell is other people" doesn't mean other people are bad or that we should avoid them. Sartre explained that he meant: if our relations with others are twisted, if we depend entirely on their judgement for our sense of ourselves, then others become hell. The play's characters are in hell because they can't stop depending on others' opinions. The way out is to take responsibility for ourselves.

### Idea 7: Engagement, freedom for all

**The idea in one line:** Freedom is not only personal; to be free, we must want others to be free too, and act in the world to bring that about.

At first, Sartre's philosophy seemed very individualistic. But during and after the war, he came to emphasise that freedom involves **responsibility for others** and for the world.

In *Existentialism Is a Humanism*, he wrote that when I choose, I choose for all, and that "freedom, in respect of concrete circumstances, can have no other end and aim but itself". And once I've recognised that, "I am obliged to will the liberty of others at the same time as my own".

In *What Is Literature?* (1948), he argued that writers must be "**engaged**": they must use words as actions, to reveal the world and change it. Writing is never neutral; silence is also a choice.

Think of artists, journalists and public figures who choose to speak out against injustice, at risk to themselves, and those who choose to stay silent. Sartre said both are choosing. There's no neutral position.

**Why it matters:** This idea underlies the modern figure of the "public intellectual" who takes political stands. It also challenges each of us: in a world with poverty, war and oppression, what is your silence choosing?

**A common misunderstanding:** Engagement doesn't mean that art must be propaganda. Sartre thought good literature reveals the world honestly, inviting the reader's freedom. But he did think that pretending to be above politics was itself a political choice, usually in favour of the status quo.

### Idea 8: Existentialism meets Marxism

**The idea in one line:** Individual freedom is always exercised within material, social and historical conditions, and these conditions must be changed.

In his later work, especially the ***Critique of Dialectical Reason*** (1960), Sartre tried to combine existentialism with **Marxism**. He had come to see that his early idea of freedom was too abstract. A worker in a factory, a colonised person, a poor woman, are free in principle, but their freedom is crushed by material conditions: scarcity, exploitation, the structures of society.

He introduced ideas like "**scarcity**", which shapes all human history and makes people into potential threats to each other; "**seriality**", the way people in mass society are isolated like a queue of strangers at a bus stop, each just one more number; and the "**group-in-fusion**", the moment when isolated individuals suddenly unite in common action, like the crowd that stormed the Bastille.

Think of people waiting at a bus stop: each is alone, interchangeable, thinking only of their own journey. That's seriality. Now imagine the bus doesn't come for two hours, and people start talking, then decide together to protest to the transport department. They become a group with a shared purpose. That's a group-in-fusion. Sartre saw history as moving between these states.

**Why it matters:** Sartre's later thought tried to answer the question every reader of his early work asks: "But what about people whose situation leaves them very little choice?" It's an important attempt to hold together individual freedom and social structure.

**A common misunderstanding:** Sartre never became an orthodox Communist. He criticised Marxism for ignoring the individual, and Marxists criticised him as a "bourgeois" philosopher. He wanted a Marxism that took human freedom seriously.

### Idea 9: Anti-colonialism and the violence question

**The idea in one line:** Colonialism dehumanises both coloniser and colonised, and the colonised have the right to free themselves.

Sartre was one of the most prominent European voices against **colonialism**. In essays on Algeria, he argued that colonialism was a "**system**" that inevitably produced racism, exploitation and violence. The coloniser, to justify domination, must see the colonised as less than human, and this degrades the coloniser too.

In his preface to Frantz Fanon's ***The Wretched of the Earth*** (1961), Sartre went further, endorsing anti-colonial **violence** as a way for the colonised to reclaim their humanity. These passages are among the most controversial he ever wrote.

**Why it matters:** For Indian readers, this connects to our own history. The Indian freedom struggle included both Gandhi's non-violent mass movement and revolutionary violence (Bhagat Singh and others). Gandhi insisted that violent means corrupt the ends: a freedom won by violence produces more violence. Sartre, in his preface, took a very different view. The debate between them, about whether the oppressed may use violence, remains one of the hardest questions of political ethics.

**A common misunderstanding:** Sartre didn't glorify violence in general. He argued that colonialism itself was a system of violence, and that the colonised were responding to it. But critics, including **Camus** (who was born in Algeria), argued that no cause justifies killing innocents, and that Sartre's rhetoric was reckless.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Descartes: the thinking subject

Like **Descartes**, Sartre began with consciousness: "I think, therefore I am". But where Descartes found a thinking **thing**, a substance, Sartre found no thing at all: consciousness is a **nothingness**, pure openness and freedom, always directed beyond itself to the world. Sartre also admired Descartes's emphasis on freedom of the will.

### Kierkegaard and Nietzsche: the forerunners

From **Kierkegaard**, Sartre took **anxiety**, the lonely individual, and the necessity of choice. But he removed God. From **Nietzsche**, he took the **death of God** and the challenge of creating values. Sartre's existentialism is, in a sense, Kierkegaard's individual living in Nietzsche's godless world.

### Hegel and Marx

From **Hegel** came the master-slave dialectic, which Sartre turned into the conflict of "looks". From **Marx** came the later attempt to understand freedom within history and material conditions. Sartre called Marxism "the philosophy of our time", which could not be surpassed while the circumstances that gave rise to it continued.

### Beauvoir: the partner and critic

**Simone de Beauvoir** was his closest intellectual partner. Each read and criticised everything the other wrote. She pushed him to recognise how the **situation**, body, gender, class, history, limits freedom; that a woman in a harem, for example, does not have the same freedom as a man in a café. Her *The Second Sex* applied existentialism to the oppression of women in ways Sartre never did. Many scholars now think she influenced him more than was long recognised.

### Camus: the friend who broke away

**Albert Camus** was often called an existentialist, though he rejected the label. He wrote about the "**absurd**", the clash between our need for meaning and the universe's silence. For a few years after the war, Camus and Sartre were close friends. But in 1952, after Camus's *The Rebel* criticised revolutionary violence and Soviet labour camps, Sartre's journal published a harsh review, and Sartre wrote a brutal reply. Camus thought Sartre was excusing murder for the sake of history; Sartre thought Camus was a moralist keeping his hands clean while others suffered.

Imagine the argument:

*Camus:* I will not justify killing innocent people for a future utopia. The ends do not justify any means.

*Sartre:* Your refusal to dirty your hands leaves the existing violence untouched. You choose the side of the oppressor by default.

*Camus:* And you choose the side of the camps.

History has, on the question of Soviet camps, largely sided with Camus.

### Heidegger: the German master

Sartre drew heavily on **Martin Heidegger**'s *Being and Time* (1927), especially his ideas of authenticity, being-toward-death, and "thrownness". But Heidegger rejected Sartre's humanism, and wrote a famous *Letter on Humanism* (1947) distancing himself from Sartre's existentialism. (Heidegger also had a shameful history as a member of the Nazi party, which complicates any comparison.)

### Later voices

- **Frantz Fanon** (1925–1961) used existentialist ideas, along with psychiatry, to analyse colonial racism, in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952).
- **Structuralists** like **Claude Lévi-Strauss** and later **Michel Foucault** attacked Sartre's emphasis on the free individual subject, arguing that people are shaped by language, culture and power structures. By the 1960s, French intellectual fashion had turned against him.
- **Existential psychotherapy** (Irvin Yalom, Rollo May and others) drew on Sartre's ideas of freedom, responsibility and bad faith.

## See it around you

**"That's just how I am."** Every time someone uses this to excuse behaviour, a short temper, laziness, rudeness, bad faith is at work. Notice how often you say it too.

**"I was only following orders."** Officials who carry out unjust policies, employees who do unethical things because "the company decided", soldiers who commit abuses. Sartre says: you chose.

**Career choices.** The student who becomes an engineer "because my parents wanted it", and the one who becomes an artist against their wishes: both chose. Owning that choice changes how you live it.

**Social media and the look.** Curating your image, worrying about likes and comments, feeling crushed by a negative remark: Sartre's analysis of the gaze describes the inner experience of online life. And the pressure of "log kya kahenge" is a whole society of looks.

**Roles and identities.** People who lose themselves in a role, the perfect bahu, the devoted employee, the strict father, the always-busy executive, and forget that they're choosing it every day.

**Fate, horoscopes and karma as excuses.** "It was in my stars." "It's my karma." "God willed it." Sartre would see these, when used to avoid responsibility, as bad faith. (He would also note that many religious traditions actually emphasise responsibility for one's actions, so this isn't the only way to understand karma.)

**Public intellectuals and silence.** When writers, actors and sportspeople speak out on public issues, or choose not to, Sartre's idea of engagement reminds us that silence is also a choice.

**Refusing honours.** When writers and artists return awards in protest, or refuse them to stay independent, they echo Sartre's refusal of the Nobel.

## Use it

**1. Catch your "that's just how I am".** Each time you say "I'm just not a morning person / good at maths / a people person", replace it with "I've been choosing not to...". Notice how different it feels. Then decide whether you want to keep choosing it.

**2. Own your choices out loud.** Instead of "I had to", try "I chose to, because...". "I chose to take this job because security mattered more to me than passion right now." It's more honest, and it gives you back your power.

**3. Ask "what if everyone did this?"** Before an important choice, ask what kind of world you're creating by it. You're not just choosing for yourself; you're showing what a human being can be.

**4. When stuck, invent.** Like Sartre's student, you'll face dilemmas no rulebook can solve. Gather what information you can, listen to advice, then choose, and accept that the choice is yours. Don't wait for certainty.

**5. Step out of the role.** Once in a while, step outside your role, as student, employee, son or daughter, spouse, and ask: who am I, apart from this? What would I do if I weren't playing this part?

**6. Free yourself from the look, but not from others.** Notice when you're living for others' gaze: likes, approval, what relatives will say. Ask what you'd do if no one were watching. At the same time, remember that others are free too; don't reduce them to objects in your story.

**7. Act without guarantees.** You can't control results. Do what's within your power, fully, and let go of the outcome. (The Gita and Sartre meet here.)

**8. Choose your silence carefully.** When you see injustice and say nothing, you've made a choice. Sometimes silence is wise; sometimes it's complicity. Know which one you're choosing.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Freedom overstated.** Sartre's early claim that we are totally free, even in prison or under torture, seemed absurd to many critics, and insensitive to the real power of oppression, poverty and trauma. A starving labourer, an abused child, a prisoner in a camp do not have the same freedom as a Parisian intellectual in a café. Beauvoir pushed back, and Sartre himself later admitted that his early view was too abstract.

**2. Too bleak about others.** His view that human relations are fundamentally conflict, that the look always objectifies, ignores love, friendship, cooperation and care, which are not always struggles for domination. Many critics think this reflects his own personality more than human nature.

**3. Political misjudgements.** Sartre defended the Soviet Union for years, minimised the existence of labour camps, and later praised Maoism and the Chinese Cultural Revolution, which caused immense suffering. He endorsed political violence in rhetoric that critics found reckless. For a philosopher of freedom, his political choices were often on the side of regimes that crushed it.

**4. The Camus quarrel.** On the question of whether revolutionary ends justify violent means, many now think Camus was right and Sartre wrong.

**5. Treatment of women.** Despite his partnership with Beauvoir, Sartre's relationships with younger women, some of them former students of Beauvoir's, have been criticised as manipulative and exploitative. His philosophical examples (like the woman on the date) reveal a masculine perspective he rarely questioned.

**6. Obscurity.** *Being and Nothingness* and the *Critique* are long, dense and repetitive. Many readers find them nearly impenetrable. His ideas often travel better in his plays, novels and short essays.

**7. The individual and structure.** Structuralists and later thinkers argued that Sartre's free, self-creating individual is an illusion: we are shaped by language, culture, unconscious drives and power structures to a far greater degree than he admitted.

**8. Can we create values from nothing?** If there are no values given in advance, and each person invents their own, what stops someone from choosing cruelty? Sartre said that choosing freely implies willing others' freedom. But critics argue this doesn't follow from his premises, and that his ethics never quite got off the ground (he never completed the ethics he promised at the end of *Being and Nothingness*).

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought of your character, your nature and your path in life as mostly fixed, by birth, by family, by fate, by personality. You may have used phrases like "that's just how I am" or "I had no choice" without thinking twice. You may have thought existentialism was just gloomy talk about meaninglessness.

**Now,** you can see that you are not a thing with a fixed nature, but a free being who makes yourself through your choices, and that this is both a burden and a gift. You can see bad faith, the subtle lie that hides our freedom from ourselves, in excuses, roles and appeals to fate. You can see that others' gaze shapes us, and that we need not be imprisoned by it. And you can see the limits too: that freedom is real but unequal, and that a philosophy of freedom must care about the conditions that crush it.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **"That's just how I am"** sounds like an excuse you can choose to drop.
- **"I had no choice"** becomes "I chose, and here's why".
- **A job title or family role** looks like something you play, not something you are.
- **The fear of what people will say** loses some of its grip.
- **A hard dilemma** becomes a moment of invention, not a test you might fail.
- **Your silence about an injustice** reveals itself as a choice.
- **Every person you meet** is someone still making themselves, not a fixed "type".

Jean-Paul Sartre was a small, half-blind man who wrote in cafés, refused the Nobel Prize, and argued with everyone. He told a generation shattered by war that there was no God to blame and no nature to hide behind, only their freedom, and their responsibility to use it. That message still stands at the door of every choice you make.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980), French philosopher, novelist, playwright and political activist; the leading figure of existentialism; refused the Nobel Prize in 1964.
- **Context:** the aftermath of the First World War, the Nazi occupation of France, the Cold War, the wars of decolonisation.
- **Life:** raised among books; partnership with Simone de Beauvoir; *Nausea* (1938); prisoner of war; *Being and Nothingness* (1943); the 1945 lecture; political engagement; blindness and a huge funeral.
- **Existence precedes essence:** no fixed human nature; we make ourselves through choices.
- **Condemned to be free:** we cannot escape choosing and are responsible for all we do.
- **Anguish, abandonment, despair:** the emotions of freedom without God or guarantees.
- **Bad faith:** self-deception about our freedom (the waiter, "that's just how I am").
- **In-itself and for-itself:** things are what they are; consciousness is a restless nothingness.
- **The look:** others' gaze objectifies us; "Hell is other people" (*No Exit*).
- **Engagement and Marxism:** freedom requires responsibility for others and changing the world.
- **Criticisms:** overstated freedom, a bleak view of human relations, political misjudgements, obscurity, and an unfinished ethics.

**Key terms**

- **Existentialism:** the philosophy that existence comes before essence, and individuals create themselves.
- **Existence precedes essence:** humans have no predetermined nature; they define themselves through action.
- **Facticity:** the given facts of our situation that we did not choose.
- **Bad faith (mauvaise foi):** self-deception that denies our freedom (or our facticity).
- **Being-in-itself / being-for-itself:** the solid being of things / the open, free being of consciousness.
- **Nothingness:** the gap in consciousness that makes freedom possible.
- **Anguish:** the feeling of total responsibility in choosing.
- **The look (le regard):** the gaze of others that makes us aware of ourselves as objects.
- **Engagement:** a writer's or person's commitment to act in the world.
- **Seriality / group-in-fusion:** isolated individuals in mass society / individuals united in shared action.
