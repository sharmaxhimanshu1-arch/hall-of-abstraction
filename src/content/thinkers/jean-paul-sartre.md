## Life

Jean-Paul Sartre was born in Paris in 1905. His father, a naval officer, died when he was fifteen months old, and he grew up in the home of his maternal grandfather, Charles Schweitzer, a language teacher and uncle of the theologian Albert Schweitzer. Surrounded by books, the boy decided early that he would be a writer, a childhood he later dissected in his autobiography *The Words*.

At the elite École Normale Supérieure he met **Simone de Beauvoir** in 1929. That year he came first in the national philosophy examination, the *agrégation*, and she came second. They became lifelong partners, never marrying or living together, in an "essential" relationship that allowed for other "contingent" loves. Sartre taught philosophy at a lycée in Le Havre, and in 1933–34 studied in Berlin, where he absorbed the phenomenology of Edmund Husserl and the work of Martin Heidegger. His first novel, *Nausea* (1938), depicts a man overwhelmed by the sheer, meaningless contingency of existence.

Drafted in 1939, Sartre was captured by the Germans in 1940 and spent nine months in a prisoner-of-war camp. Back in occupied Paris he published his philosophical masterpiece, *Being and Nothingness* (1943), and the plays *The Flies* and *No Exit*. After the Liberation he became a celebrity. His October 1945 lecture "Existentialism Is a Humanism" drew a crowd so large that people fainted. With Beauvoir and Maurice Merleau-Ponty he founded the journal *Les Temps modernes*.

Sartre believed intellectuals must be politically "engaged". He was close to the French Communist Party in the early 1950s before breaking with it over the Soviet invasion of Hungary in 1956. He campaigned against the French war in Algeria, and his apartment was bombed twice by right-wing extremists. He opposed the Vietnam War and supported the students of May 1968. In 1964 he declined the Nobel Prize in Literature, saying a writer should not allow himself to be turned into an institution. Nearly blind in his last years, he died in 1980. Some fifty thousand people followed his funeral procession through Paris.

## Key ideas

**Existence precedes essence.** A paper-knife is made according to a concept that defines its purpose. Human beings are not. There is no God who designed human nature, so "man first of all exists, encounters himself, surges up in the world, and defines himself afterwards." We are nothing but what we make of ourselves.

**Being-in-itself and being-for-itself.** In *Being and Nothingness* Sartre distinguishes the solid, self-identical being of things (**in-itself**) from consciousness (**for-itself**), which is always directed beyond itself, never coinciding with itself, a kind of "nothingness" at the heart of being. This gap is our freedom.

**Condemned to be free.** We cannot escape choosing; even not choosing is a choice. We are "condemned to be free" and wholly responsible for our actions and, in choosing, for the image of humanity we project. This brings **anguish**.

**Bad faith.** To escape anguish we deceive ourselves, pretending that we are fixed things determined by our roles, characters or circumstances. Sartre's example is a café waiter whose movements are a little too perfect, playing at being a waiter as if that were all he is.

**The look.** Under the gaze of another person, I suddenly experience myself as an object, judged and defined from outside. Relations with others are therefore marked by conflict, and in *No Exit* three characters trapped together in the afterlife discover that "hell is other people".

**Existentialism and Marxism.** In his later work, especially the *Critique of Dialectical Reason*, Sartre sought to unite existentialist freedom with Marxist analysis of history, scarcity and class, calling Marxism "the unsurpassable philosophy of our time."

## Influence and legacy

In the decade after the Second World War, Sartre was the most famous intellectual in the world, and "existentialism" became a cultural movement as much as a philosophy, with its own cafés, fashions and jazz clubs. His ideas of freedom, responsibility and authenticity shaped literature, theatre, psychotherapy and political activism. Structuralists and post-structuralists of the 1960s reacted against his philosophy of the free subject, and his political judgements, including his long indulgence of Soviet and Maoist regimes, have been sharply criticised. Yet his challenge, that we are what we do and cannot hide behind excuses, retains its force.
