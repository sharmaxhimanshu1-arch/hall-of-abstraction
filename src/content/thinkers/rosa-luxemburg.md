## Life

Rosa Luxemburg was born in 1871 in Zamość, in the part of Poland ruled by the Russian Empire, the youngest child of a cultured Jewish timber merchant's family. A childhood hip ailment left her with a lifelong limp. In Warsaw she excelled at school and joined an underground socialist group while still a teenager. In 1889, facing arrest, she fled to Switzerland.

At the University of Zurich, one of the few in Europe that admitted women, she studied philosophy, natural science and economics, earning a doctorate in 1897 with a dissertation on the industrial development of Poland. With her comrade and long-time partner **Leo Jogiches** she co-founded the party that became the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland and Lithuania. Unusually for a Polish socialist, she opposed the goal of Polish independence, arguing that workers' interests transcended national borders.

In 1898 she moved to Germany, acquiring citizenship through a marriage of convenience, and joined the Social Democratic Party (SPD), then the largest socialist party in the world. She immediately made her name by attacking Eduard Bernstein's "revisionist" argument that socialism could be achieved gradually through reforms. During the Russian Revolution of 1905 she went to Warsaw to take part and was imprisoned. From 1907 she taught economics at the SPD's party school in Berlin.

When the SPD voted in August 1914 to support Germany's war effort, Luxemburg was devastated. With Karl Liebknecht and others she formed the anti-war group that became the **Spartacus League**, and she spent most of the war in prison, where she continued to write. Freed by the German Revolution of November 1918, she co-founded the **Communist Party of Germany** at the turn of 1919. Days later, during the Spartacist uprising in Berlin, which she had considered premature, she and Liebknecht were captured by Freikorps paramilitaries. On 15 January 1919 they were murdered. Her body was thrown into the Landwehr Canal and not recovered until months later.

## Key ideas

**Reform or revolution.** Against the revisionists, Luxemburg argued that reforms within capitalism, though worth fighting for, could never abolish the system's basic contradictions. The goal must remain the conquest of political power by the working class.

**The mass strike and spontaneity.** Drawing on 1905, she argued that revolutions are not made by party decree. They grow from the spontaneous self-activity of the masses, in which workers learn through struggle. The party's role was to give political direction, not to command.

**Against Leninist centralism.** In 1904 she criticised Lenin's model of a tightly disciplined party of professional revolutionaries, warning that it would stifle the initiative of the working class: "Historically, the errors committed by a truly revolutionary movement are infinitely more fruitful than the infallibility of the cleverest Central Committee."

**Democracy and socialism.** Writing in prison in 1918, she greeted the Russian Revolution but sharply criticised the Bolsheviks for dissolving the Constituent Assembly and suppressing opposition. "Freedom only for the supporters of the government, only for the members of one party, however numerous they may be, is no freedom at all. Freedom is always and exclusively freedom for the one who thinks differently." Without free elections, a free press and free assembly, she predicted, public life would wither and a bureaucracy would rule.

**Imperialism.** In *The Accumulation of Capital* she argued that capitalism needs to keep expanding into non-capitalist regions and markets to survive, which drives colonialism, militarism and war.

**Socialism or barbarism.** The First World War showed that humanity faced a stark choice: "either transition to socialism or regression into barbarism."

## Influence and legacy

Luxemburg became a martyr of the left. The East German state honoured her while keeping her critique of the Bolsheviks out of sight, and her insistence on democracy made her a heroine to dissidents, the New Left of the 1960s and democratic socialists. Her economic theory of imperialism influenced later theories of world systems and globalisation. Each January, thousands of people still march in Berlin in memory of her and Liebknecht.

Her letters from prison, full of tenderness toward birds, plants and friends, revealed a warm and poetic side that has made her one of the most humanly compelling figures in the history of socialism.
