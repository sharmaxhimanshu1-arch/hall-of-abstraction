## Start here

Imagine a revolution succeeds. The old, corrupt rulers are gone. A new government, led by people who sincerely want to help the poor, takes power. They promise land for the farmers, bread for the hungry, power for the workers.

Then the new government faces enemies: the old rulers' supporters, foreign armies, saboteurs, critics. So it says: "For now, we must suspend elections. We must close the newspapers that spread lies. We must arrest those who organise against the revolution. It's only temporary. Once we're safe, freedom will return."

Do you agree?

Think carefully. The intentions are good. The enemies are real. Wouldn't it be foolish to let them destroy the revolution in the name of freedom?

Now ask: who decides who is an "enemy"? Who decides when it's "safe"? What happens to people inside the revolution who disagree with the leaders, who think the leaders are making mistakes? Are they enemies too?

In **1918**, a small, limping woman sitting in a German prison cell asked these questions about the Russian Revolution, which had just happened. She was a revolutionary herself, a lifelong Marxist who wanted capitalism overthrown. She admired the courage of Lenin and the Bolsheviks. And she wrote, in a manuscript smuggled out of prison:

"Freedom only for the supporters of the government, only for the members of one party—however numerous they may be—is no freedom at all. Freedom is always and exclusively freedom for the one who thinks differently."

She warned that without free elections, a free press and free debate, the revolution would turn into the rule of a small group of bureaucrats. Within a few years, Stalin proved her right.

Her name was **Rosa Luxemburg**. She was one of the most brilliant thinkers in the history of socialism, an economist who explained why capitalism needs empires, a fearless speaker who went to prison for opposing the First World War, and a woman who loved birds, plants and poetry as passionately as she loved revolution. She was murdered at forty-seven.

By the end of this page, you'll understand why she matters to anyone who wants to change the world, and anyone who fears what happens when people who want to change the world get power.

## The world she was born into

### Poland: a nation without a state

Rosa Luxemburg was born in **1871** in **Zamość**, a town in the part of **Poland** ruled by the **Russian Empire**. There was no independent Polish state at the time. Poland had been divided between Russia, Prussia (Germany) and Austria in the late eighteenth century. Poles in Russian Poland faced attempts to suppress their language and culture, especially after failed uprisings in 1830 and 1863.

She was also **Jewish**, in an empire where Jews faced legal discrimination, restrictions on where they could live and study, and periodic violent attacks called **pogroms**.

And she was a **woman**, in a world where women could not vote, were mostly excluded from universities and professions, and were expected to stay in the home.

So from the start, she was an outsider three times over: a Pole under Russian rule, a Jew in an anti-Semitic society, and a woman in a man's world. Later, she added a fourth: a revolutionary.

### The rise of socialism

In the late nineteenth century, **socialism** grew from small groups of exiles into mass movements. Workers formed **trade unions** and political **parties**. The most powerful was the **Social Democratic Party of Germany** (SPD), founded in 1875 and officially committed to Marx's ideas. By 1912 it was the largest party in the German parliament, with a million members, its own newspapers, schools, choirs, sports clubs and libraries. It was the model for socialists everywhere.

But there was a question at the heart of the movement: how would socialism actually arrive? Through **revolution**, the violent overthrow of capitalism? Or through **reform**, winning elections, passing laws, gradually improving workers' lives until capitalism became socialism by stages?

### Empires and war

Meanwhile, European powers were carving up the world. Britain ruled India and much of Africa; France ruled Indochina and North and West Africa; Germany grabbed colonies in Africa and the Pacific; Belgium's King Leopold turned the Congo into a slave plantation where millions died. The rivalry between these empires, combined with an arms race, was pushing Europe toward catastrophe. In **1914**, it came: the **First World War**, which killed some fifteen to twenty million people.

## The life, as a story

### A limping girl who read everything

Rosa Luxemburg was born on **5 March 1871**, the youngest of five children in a cultured, middle-class Jewish family. Her father was a timber merchant who loved Polish and German literature. The family moved to **Warsaw** when she was small.

At about five, she developed a hip condition and spent a year in bed. She was left with a **permanent limp**, and she was very small, under five feet tall. But she was quick, funny and astonishingly intelligent. She learned Polish, Russian, German, French and later more, and read voraciously.

At school in Warsaw, a Russian-language girls' gymnasium (Jews were admitted only in small numbers), she joined a secret revolutionary socialist group while still a teenager. She graduated near the top of her class but was denied a gold medal for her "rebellious attitude".

### Escape to Zurich

In **1889**, at eighteen, with the police on the trail of her group, she was smuggled out of Poland, according to a well-known story, hidden under straw in a peasant's cart.

She went to **Zurich**, Switzerland, one of the few places in Europe where universities admitted women. Zurich was full of exiled revolutionaries from Russia and Poland. She studied natural sciences, mathematics and then **political economy** and law, and in **1897** received a doctorate with a thesis on ***The Industrial Development of Poland***. It was published as a book and praised by economists. She was one of the first women in Europe to earn a doctorate in economics.

### Leo Jogiches

In Zurich, she met **Leo Jogiches**, a secretive, wealthy, intense young revolutionary from Vilnius. They became lovers and political partners for fifteen years. Together they founded and led a small revolutionary party, the **Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland** (later of Poland and Lithuania), in **1893**.

Their relationship was passionate and difficult. Their surviving letters, hundreds of them from her side, show her longing for a normal life with him, a home, perhaps a child, alongside her total commitment to the cause. He was often cold and controlling. They separated as lovers around 1907, but remained political comrades until the end.

The party they led took a controversial position: it **opposed Polish independence** as a goal. Rosa argued that Polish workers' liberation would come through international socialist revolution with Russian, German and Austrian workers, not through a new Polish nation-state led by Polish capitalists and nobles. This put her in conflict with Polish nationalists, and later with Lenin, who supported the "right of nations to self-determination".

### To Germany, and a marriage of convenience

In **1898**, she moved to **Berlin**, the heart of the European socialist movement, to work in the German SPD. To get German citizenship, she entered a **marriage of convenience** with Gustav Lübeck, the son of a friend. They parted at the registry office and divorced a few years later.

She quickly became famous in the German party, as a writer, editor and, above all, a brilliant, fiery **speaker**. She was a woman, a foreigner, a Jew, small and limping, in a party dominated by German men, many of whom found her arrogant and abrasive. She didn't care. She argued with everyone, including the party's leaders.

### The great debate: reform or revolution

In 1898–99, a leading SPD intellectual, **Eduard Bernstein**, argued that Marx was wrong in key ways: capitalism was not heading for collapse, the middle class was not disappearing, and workers' conditions were improving. Socialists, he said, should give up the dream of revolution and focus on gradual reforms through parliament and unions. "The movement is everything," he wrote; "the final goal is nothing."

Rosa answered with ***Social Reform or Revolution?*** (1899), which made her name. Reforms, she argued, are essential, but if you give up the goal of transforming society, you'll end up just managing capitalism, making it a little gentler while leaving its basic injustice intact. The struggle for reforms is the **means**; the social revolution is the **aim**.

### 1905: revolution in Russia

In **1905**, revolution broke out across the Russian Empire, with mass strikes, uprisings and mutinies. Rosa rushed to **Warsaw** under a false name to take part. She was arrested in **March 1906** and imprisoned in the Warsaw Citadel, where she faced the possibility of execution. Released on bail after some months, partly through pressure and money from the German party, she fled.

The experience transformed her thinking. She had seen workers, without leaders' instructions, organising themselves through **mass strikes**: spontaneous waves of strikes spreading from factory to factory, city to city, combining economic demands with political ones. She wrote ***The Mass Strike, the Political Party and the Trade Unions*** (1906), arguing that revolutionary energy comes from the masses themselves, not from orders handed down by party leaders.

### Teacher

From **1907** to **1914**, she taught **economics and economic history** at the SPD's party school in Berlin. Her students remembered her as a superb teacher: clear, challenging, funny, never condescending. Out of her lectures came her major economic work, ***The Accumulation of Capital*** (1913), which tried to explain **imperialism**.

### Against the war

On **4 August 1914**, as the First World War began, the SPD deputies in the German parliament voted **for war credits**, money to fund the war. Socialists across Europe, who had promised for decades that workers of all countries would never fight each other for their capitalist rulers, lined up behind their own governments.

Rosa was devastated. She reportedly considered suicide. Then she fought back. With a small group, including **Karl Liebknecht**, the only SPD deputy who voted against further war credits in December 1914, she formed what became the **Spartacus League**, named after the leader of a slave revolt in ancient Rome.

She spent most of the war in **prison**, from 1915 to November 1918, with a short break, convicted for inciting soldiers to disobey and then held in "protective custody" without trial. In prison she wrote the ***Junius Pamphlet*** (1916), a ferocious attack on the war and on the socialist leaders who supported it. "Socialism or barbarism", she said, was the choice before humanity.

### Letters from prison

In prison, she also wrote letters that have become classics. She wrote to her friends about the birds she watched from her cell window, the plants she pressed in her herbarium, the clouds, the poetry of Goethe. She wrote, in one famous letter to **Sophie Liebknecht** in December 1917, about watching soldiers beat a team of exhausted buffaloes, captured in Romania, that were pulling a cart into the prison yard. One buffalo had a bleeding wound and looked, she wrote, like a child who had been punished and did not know why. She wept, she said, for the buffalo as if for her own brother.

In another letter she wrote that being human means being ready to throw your whole life "on the scales of destiny" when necessary, while rejoicing in every bright day and every beautiful cloud. For her, revolution and tenderness were not opposites.

### The Russian Revolution, from a cell

In **1917**, the Russian Revolution overthrew the Tsar, and in November the **Bolsheviks**, led by **Lenin** and **Trotsky**, seized power. Rosa welcomed it as the first great break in the war's madness. She praised the Bolsheviks for having dared.

But in her cell in Breslau, in **1918**, she wrote a manuscript, ***The Russian Revolution***, which criticised them sharply: for dissolving the elected **Constituent Assembly**, for suppressing other parties and newspapers, for using terror. She warned that without democracy, the revolution would degenerate into a dictatorship "of a handful of politicians". She also criticised their land policy and their slogan of national self-determination.

She never published it. Her friends thought it would harm the revolution. It appeared only in **1922**, after her death.

### Revolution in Germany and murder

In **November 1918**, Germany lost the war. Sailors mutinied, workers' and soldiers' councils sprang up, the Kaiser abdicated, and a republic was proclaimed. Rosa was released from prison on **8 November**. She went to Berlin, white-haired and ill after years in prison, and started a newspaper, ***Die Rote Fahne*** (The Red Flag).

The new government was led by the **SPD**, her former party, under **Friedrich Ebert**. It allied with the army and right-wing paramilitary units called the **Freikorps** to restore order.

On **1 January 1919**, the Spartacists and others founded the **Communist Party of Germany** (KPD). Rosa wrote its programme, which rejected terror and said the party would take power only with the clear will of the great majority of workers. She also argued, and lost the vote, that the party should take part in the coming elections to the National Assembly.

In **January 1919**, an uprising broke out in Berlin, the so-called **Spartacist uprising**. Rosa thought it premature, but once it started, she would not abandon the workers. The government crushed it with the Freikorps.

On **14 January**, she wrote her last article, "**Order Prevails in Berlin**", mocking the government's claim to have restored order:

"'Order prevails in Berlin!' You foolish lackeys! Your 'order' is built on sand. Tomorrow the revolution will 'rise up again, clashing its weapons,' and to your horror it will proclaim with trumpets blazing: I was, I am, I shall be!"

The next evening, **15 January 1919**, she and Karl Liebknecht were arrested by Freikorps soldiers and taken to the Hotel Eden. Rosa was struck with a rifle butt, shot in the head, and her body was thrown into Berlin's **Landwehr Canal**. It was not found until **31 May**. The government of her former party had tolerated, if not encouraged, the killing. Most of her killers were never seriously punished. Leo Jogiches, investigating the murders, was himself killed in March.

She was forty-seven.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Reform as means, revolution as aim

**The idea in one line:** Reforms are vital, but only as part of a struggle to transform society; reforms without that aim end up stabilising capitalism.

Bernstein said socialists should drop revolution and focus on reforms: better wages, shorter working days, social insurance, voting rights, through parliament and unions.

Rosa agreed reforms were vital. She wrote:

"Between social reforms and revolution there exists for the social democracy an indissoluble tie. The struggle for reforms is its means; the social revolution, its aim."

But she argued that reforms alone can't change the basic structure of capitalism: the private ownership of the means of production, the exploitation of labour. Unions can win better wages, but the power to hire, fire, invest and close factories stays with the owners. Parliament can pass laws, but capitalist economic power shapes what laws are possible.

She used a vivid image: the unions' struggle is like the "labour of Sisyphus", the Greek myth of a man condemned to roll a rock up a hill forever, only for it to roll down again. Workers win gains in good times, lose them in crises, and have to fight again.

If socialists give up the aim of transformation, she warned, they'll become managers of capitalism, administering it more humanely, but never changing it. Their movements will be absorbed into the system.

Think of it like this. A doctor treats a patient's symptoms: fever, pain, cough. That's necessary. But if the doctor never asks what disease is causing them, the patient will keep getting sick. Reforms treat symptoms; Rosa wanted to cure the disease.

**Why it matters:** This debate, between **reformists** and **radicals**, runs through every movement for change. In India, it appears in debates between those who want gradual improvements within the system (more welfare schemes, better implementation, incremental policy) and those who say the system itself (caste, capitalism, patriarchy) must be fundamentally transformed. Ambedkar's thought contained both: he worked within the Constitution and wrote much of it, while saying that political democracy without social and economic democracy would be "a life of contradictions".

**A common misunderstanding:** Rosa wasn't against reforms or parliament. She fought for both and wanted socialists to take part in elections. Her point was about the **relationship** between reforms and the larger goal. History gave both sides evidence: Bernstein's path led to European social democracy and welfare states, which improved workers' lives enormously; Rosa's fears were confirmed when the SPD voted for war in 1914 and then allied with the army against the workers.

### Idea 2: The masses learn by struggling

**The idea in one line:** Revolutionary change comes from the self-activity of ordinary people, not from orders handed down by a party leadership.

After 1905, Rosa saw that the great strikes in Russia hadn't been planned by party leaders. They grew spontaneously: a strike over wages in one factory spread to others, became political, retreated, flared up again. Workers who had never been political learned through action, organised themselves, made demands. She called this the **mass strike**.

From this she drew a principle: the masses are not raw material to be shaped by a party of experts. They learn and develop political consciousness through their own **experience** of struggle. A party's role is to explain, encourage, and give political direction, not to command.

This led to her famous clash with **Lenin**. In 1902–04, Lenin argued for a tightly disciplined, centralised party of **professional revolutionaries** who would lead the workers. Without such a "**vanguard**", he said, workers would develop only "trade union consciousness", demands for better wages, not revolutionary consciousness.

Rosa answered in **1904** that this was dangerous. A super-centralised party would stifle the initiative of the workers and subject them to the will of a central committee. She concluded:

"Historically, the errors committed by a truly revolutionary movement are infinitely more fruitful than the infallibility of the cleverest Central Committee."

Think of a school where teachers dictate everything, students memorise and obey, and nobody is allowed to make mistakes. The students may perform well in exams, but they never learn to think for themselves. Rosa wanted a movement where people learned to govern themselves, even if that meant making mistakes.

**Why it matters:** This idea of change from below runs through many social movements: the **Chipko** movement in the Himalayas in the 1970s, where village women hugged trees to stop logging; the **Narmada Bachao Andolan**; the **farmers' protests** of 2020–21, which were organised largely by farmers' unions themselves, without a single party or leader. Rosa would have recognised such movements as the masses learning through action.

**A common misunderstanding:** Rosa is often accused of believing in pure "spontaneity", as if parties and organisation didn't matter. In fact, she spent her whole life building parties and organisations. She believed in organisation, but organisation that grows from and serves the self-activity of the masses, rather than replacing it.

### Idea 3: Imperialism, capitalism's hunger for outside lands

**The idea in one line:** Capitalism cannot survive on its own; it must constantly expand into non-capitalist areas, which drives imperialism, colonialism and war.

This is the central argument of ***The Accumulation of Capital*** (1913), her most important economic work. It's a bit technical, but let's get to the heart of it.

Capitalists produce goods to make a profit. Part of that profit is reinvested to produce more goods, growing ever larger. This is called **accumulation**. But here's the puzzle: who buys all these extra goods? Workers are paid less than the value they produce, so they can't buy everything. Capitalists can't simply keep buying from each other forever.

Rosa's answer: capitalism needs **non-capitalist** buyers and resources from **outside** the capitalist system: peasant economies, traditional communities, colonies. It breaks into these societies, sells them goods, takes their land, raw materials and labour, and converts them into capitalist markets. When it has swallowed everything, it will have nowhere left to grow.

That, she argued, explains **imperialism**: the scramble of European powers for colonies in Asia and Africa. And it explains the **violence** of imperialism, because traditional societies resist.

She gave examples in the book, including **India**. She described how British rule broke up India's traditional village communities, imposed new forms of land property and taxation, destroyed handicraft industries, and forced Indian peasants into the cash economy, often with catastrophic results like famine. She also discussed the destruction of peasant economies in Algeria, the opium wars in China, and the dispossession of Black Africans in South Africa.

Think of it like a fire that must keep spreading to new fuel. When it runs out of fuel, it dies. Capitalism, she said, must keep consuming new territories, new markets, new resources.

**Why it matters:** Rosa's theory was one of the first to place **colonialism** at the centre of the story of capitalism. It anticipated later theories of "**dependency**" and "**world systems**", which argued that the wealth of rich countries is connected to the underdevelopment of poor ones. Indian thinkers like **Dadabhai Naoroji**, with his "**drain theory**", made related arguments about how British rule drained wealth from India.

Today, some writers apply her idea to new "outside" areas capitalism enters: forests and tribal lands opened up for mining (think of struggles in **Niyamgiri** in Odisha, where Dongria Kondh Adivasi communities, after a Supreme Court-ordered consultation in 2013, voted against a bauxite mining project), public services turned into private markets, personal data turned into a commodity, informal economies drawn into formal finance. The geographer **David Harvey** calls this "**accumulation by dispossession**".

**A common misunderstanding:** Most economists, including many Marxists, think her technical argument was flawed: capitalism can, in theory, keep growing by itself, through investment and rising wages. Lenin and others criticised her math. But her broader point, that capitalism has historically expanded by absorbing and transforming non-capitalist societies, often violently, is widely seen as powerful and true.

### Idea 4: Socialism or barbarism

**The idea in one line:** Humanity faces a choice: either move forward to socialism, or fall back into barbarism.

In the ***Junius Pamphlet***, written in prison in 1915 and published in 1916, Rosa looked at the First World War, millions of workers in uniform slaughtering each other for their rulers, and wrote:

"Bourgeois society stands at the crossroads, either transition to socialism or regression into barbarism."

(She attributed the idea to Engels.)

Before the war, many socialists believed that history was on their side, that capitalism would inevitably collapse and socialism would inevitably follow. Rosa said: **nothing is inevitable**. History can go backward as well as forward. If humanity doesn't consciously choose a better path, it could descend into mass killing, destruction and savagery. She described the war in terrible terms: the destruction of culture, the mass death of workers, "a sea of blood".

She also attacked the socialist leaders who supported the war, for betraying the promise that workers of all nations would never fight each other. The old slogan "Workers of the world, unite!" had become, she said bitterly, a call to unite in peacetime and cut each other's throats in war.

**Why it matters:** Her prophecy proved terribly accurate. Within a generation came **fascism**, **Nazism**, the **Holocaust**, the **Second World War**, and the atom bomb. "Socialism or barbarism" became a famous slogan. More broadly, the idea that progress is not automatic, that civilisation can collapse, that humanity faces real choices, is central to thinking about climate change, nuclear weapons, and the rise of authoritarianism today.

**A common misunderstanding:** By "barbarism" she didn't mean any particular non-European people (a common nineteenth-century use of the word). She meant the collapse of civilisation into mass violence, which she saw happening in the heart of "civilised" Europe.

### Idea 5: Freedom for the one who thinks differently

**The idea in one line:** Socialism without democracy, free elections, a free press and the right to dissent will degenerate into a bureaucratic dictatorship.

This is her most famous idea, and it's worth reading the full passage from ***The Russian Revolution***:

"Freedom only for the supporters of the government, only for the members of one party—however numerous they may be—is no freedom at all. Freedom is always and exclusively freedom for the one who thinks differently. Not because of any fanatical concept of 'justice' but because all that is instructive, wholesome and purifying in political freedom depends on this essential characteristic, and its effectiveness vanishes when 'freedom' becomes a special privilege."

And her prediction of what would happen without freedom:

"...without general elections, without unrestricted freedom of press and assembly, without a free struggle of opinion, life dies out in every public institution, becomes a mere semblance of life, in which only the bureaucracy remains as the active element."

She went on to describe exactly the kind of system that later emerged: a few dozen party leaders direct and rule; an elite of workers is invited to meetings to applaud the leaders' speeches and approve their resolutions unanimously. "A dictatorship, to be sure," she wrote, "not the dictatorship of the proletariat, however, but only the dictatorship of a handful of politicians."

Why does freedom of dissent matter so much? Rosa's reason wasn't the liberal idea of individual rights alone. It was **practical**: socialism is a new kind of society, which has never existed before. Nobody knows exactly how to build it. It must be worked out through experience, experiments, mistakes and corrections, by millions of people. Without free debate and criticism, the leaders will make mistakes and nobody will be able to correct them. Socialism, she said, "cannot be decreed or introduced by ukase" (a Tsar's decree).

Think of a company where nobody can criticise the boss. Every meeting is full of applause. All reports say things are going well. Then one day the company collapses, and everyone says, "We knew there were problems, but nobody dared to say so." Rosa saw that a whole society could work like that.

**Why it matters:** Her prediction came true. In the Soviet Union, under **Stalin**, the party became a bureaucratic dictatorship that killed millions in purges and famines. Similar patterns appeared in other one-party states. Her words became a rallying cry for dissidents in communist countries. In **East Germany** in **January 1988**, protesters at the official annual commemoration of her death carried banners with her line "Freedom is always the freedom of those who think differently" and were arrested.

The idea also applies far beyond communism. Any movement, party, organisation or government that silences internal dissent risks the same fate. And it echoes John Stuart Mill's argument that silencing any opinion robs us of the chance to correct our errors.

**A common misunderstanding:** Rosa was not a liberal in the ordinary sense. She did not defend capitalist "bourgeois democracy" as an ideal; she thought it was limited by economic inequality. She wanted a **socialist democracy**: a society where economic power was shared and political freedom was wider than under capitalism, not narrower. She thought the Bolsheviks made the wrong choice by throwing away democracy instead of deepening it.

### Idea 6: Internationalism and the national question

**The idea in one line:** Workers' liberation is international; nationalism tends to divide workers and serve their rulers.

Rosa was a fierce **internationalist**. She believed that workers of different countries had more in common with each other than with their own capitalists. The war of 1914 confirmed for her the danger of nationalism: workers who had promised solidarity marched off to kill each other under their national flags.

She was so committed to this that she opposed the demand for **Polish independence**, even though she was Polish. She argued that an independent Polish state would just be a state for Polish landlords and capitalists, and that Polish workers' future lay in a joint revolution with Russian and German workers. She also criticised Lenin's slogan of "**the right of nations to self-determination**", which she thought would encourage nationalism and break up a revolutionary state into small nations controlled by local elites.

**Why it matters:** The debate between internationalism and nationalism is still central. On one hand, nationalism powered anti-colonial movements, including India's freedom struggle, which liberated hundreds of millions. On the other, it has also fuelled wars, ethnic cleansing and the persecution of minorities. Rosa's warning, that national flags can be used to make workers fight each other instead of their rulers, remains relevant.

**A common misunderstanding:** On this point many think Rosa was **wrong**. National feeling turned out to be far stronger than she expected, and national independence was a genuine liberation for many colonised peoples. Poland became independent in 1918, and Poles, including Polish workers, largely welcomed it. India's freedom movement shows that national liberation can be a step toward wider human liberation, not only a tool of elites.

### Idea 7: Tenderness and revolution

**The idea in one line:** A true revolutionary must be fully human, loving life, nature and people, not a cold machine of struggle.

This isn't a "theory" in her books, but it's central to who she was and why she still moves people. Her letters show a woman who loved birds, plants, music, poetry and her cat Mimi; who wept for an abused buffalo; who wrote to friends about clouds and the songs of tits and nightingales outside her prison window.

She was impatient with revolutionaries who saw people merely as tools for the cause. She wrote to a friend that she felt at home in the whole world, wherever there were clouds and birds and human tears.

**Why it matters:** Many movements for justice have turned cold, cruel and inhuman, treating people as means to an end. Rosa reminds us that the point of changing the world is to make life better for real human beings, and that a person who has lost tenderness has lost the reason for the struggle. Think of Gandhi's insistence that means must be as pure as ends, or the Buddha's compassion, or Tagore's union of politics with beauty and feeling.

**A common misunderstanding:** Rosa's tenderness did not make her soft. She was sharp, combative, often sarcastic, and unafraid of conflict. She believed revolution would sometimes require force. But she also believed that a socialism built on terror and contempt for human beings would not be worth having.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Marx: master, not idol

**Karl Marx** was her teacher, but she never treated him as infallible. In *The Accumulation of Capital*, she argued openly that Marx's own schemes of how capital reproduces itself were incomplete, because they assumed a closed capitalist economy. She extended his analysis to explain imperialism. She also shared Marx's belief that the emancipation of the working class must be the act of the working class itself, and took that belief more seriously than many who called themselves Marxists.

### Hegel and the dialectic

Like Marx, she inherited **Hegel**'s idea that history moves through contradictions, and that people learn through struggle and conflict, not through being taught in advance. Her trust in the masses learning from their own mistakes is deeply dialectical.

### Smith and the classical economists

In *The Accumulation of Capital*, Rosa traced the problem of how capital reproduces itself through the history of economics, criticising **Adam Smith** and his successors for missing key parts of the process. She respected them as serious thinkers, as Marx did, but argued they couldn't see the whole picture because they treated capitalism as natural.

### Lenin: comrades and critics

**Lenin** and Rosa were allies in many fights: against the war, against reformism, for revolution. They respected each other. But they disagreed about the party (centralism vs. mass self-activity), about national self-determination, about her theory of accumulation, and finally about democracy after the revolution.

Imagine them arguing in 1918:

*Lenin:* We are surrounded by enemies. White armies, foreign invaders, saboteurs. If we allow free elections and a free press, the revolution will be destroyed.

*Rosa:* And if you abolish them, the revolution will destroy itself. Without free debate, life dies out in every institution. Only the bureaucracy will remain.

*Lenin:* These are temporary measures.

*Rosa:* The danger is that you make a virtue of necessity, and turn temporary emergency measures into a permanent system, and recommend them to the whole world.

After her death, Lenin called her an eagle who sometimes flew lower than hens, but remained an eagle. (He also, in the same breath, listed her "errors".)

### Bernstein and Kautsky: the reformists

**Eduard Bernstein** was her chief opponent on reform. **Karl Kautsky**, the "pope of Marxism" and her friend for years, broke with her around 1910 over the mass strike and tactics; she came to see him as a timid centrist who used Marxist words to justify doing nothing.

### Mill: an unexpected ally

**John Stuart Mill** was a liberal; Rosa was a revolutionary socialist. Yet her defence of "freedom for the one who thinks differently" is remarkably close to Mill's argument in *On Liberty* that silencing any opinion robs humanity, because no one is infallible and we learn from clashing views. Both saw dissent as the way a society corrects its mistakes. The difference is that Rosa thought true freedom required economic equality too.

### Later voices

- **Hannah Arendt** (1906–1975) wrote an admiring essay on Rosa, praising her independence of mind and her commitment to freedom and spontaneous political action.
- **Dissidents in communist countries** used her words against the regimes that claimed her legacy.
- **The New Left** of the 1960s, disillusioned with Soviet communism, found in Rosa a model of democratic, humane socialism.
- **Feminists** have claimed her as a pioneer, though she herself rarely wrote about women's issues separately, believing women's liberation would come through socialist revolution. Her close friend **Clara Zetkin** led the socialist women's movement and proposed International Women's Day.

## See it around you

**Dissent inside organisations.** Every political party, company, family and institution faces Rosa's question: is there space for people who disagree with the leadership? When a party expels members for criticising its leaders, when an employee is punished for raising concerns, when a student is silenced for questioning a teacher, Rosa's warning applies: without dissent, the organisation stops learning.

**Movements from below.** The farmers' protests of 2020–21, women's self-help groups, Chipko, students' movements: these show what Rosa called the self-activity of the masses. Notice how such movements often surprise political parties and experts, who didn't plan them.

**Reform vs. transformation.** Debates about whether India's problems, poverty, caste discrimination, gender inequality, can be solved by better schemes and laws, or require deeper structural change, are debates Rosa joined in 1899.

**Mining, forests and Adivasi land.** Struggles over the opening of forests and tribal lands to mining and industry, from Niyamgiri to Hasdeo Arand in Chhattisgarh, echo Rosa's theory of capitalism expanding into non-capitalist areas, often at the cost of the communities living there.

**Nationalism and war.** When a war breaks out and everyone is expected to support it, when those who question it are called traitors, think of Rosa in 1914. And think of the soldiers from poor families who die while the rich stay home.

**Hope against despair.** "Socialism or barbarism" reminds us that the future isn't guaranteed. Climate change, nuclear weapons and pandemics show that civilisation can go backward. The choices we make matter.

**Kindness in activism.** When activists treat opponents with contempt, or treat ordinary people as stupid, or forget joy and beauty in the struggle, Rosa's letters offer another model: fierce and tender at once.

## Use it

**1. Protect the person who disagrees.** In your group, team, class or family, notice who is being silenced. Make space for the person who thinks differently, even when you think they're wrong. Rosa's line, "freedom for the one who thinks differently", is a test of whether you really value freedom or only your own opinions.

**2. Prefer errors that teach over orders that silence.** If you lead anything, a team, a project, a club, let people try things, make mistakes and learn. A group that learns from its errors is stronger in the long run than one that obeys a perfect plan.

**3. Keep the big goal in view.** When working on small improvements, in your career, your community, your cause, keep asking what the bigger goal is. Small reforms can be steps toward it or substitutes for it. Know which ones you're taking.

**4. Learn by doing.** You learn politics, organising, leadership and courage not from books alone but from taking part, in a union, a resident association, a protest, a campaign, a student council. Rosa believed people become capable through action.

**5. Look for who's paying for "growth".** When you hear of a new mine, dam, factory or market, ask: who was there before? What happens to their land and way of life? Who benefits?

**6. Refuse to be divided against your neighbours.** When politicians or media try to set you against people of another religion, caste, region or country, ask Rosa's question: who benefits from us fighting each other?

**7. Stay tender.** Whatever your fight, for justice, for your family, for your career, keep loving the small beautiful things: a bird, a plant, a cloud, a friend's laugh. They're not a distraction from the struggle; they're what it's for.

**8. Have courage.** Rosa went to prison for saying the war was wrong when almost everyone, including her own party, said it was right. Ask yourself: when everyone around me supports something I believe is wrong, will I say so?

## Where she went wrong

**1. The economics.** Most economists, including many Marxists, consider her theory of accumulation technically flawed. Capitalism has continued to grow through rising productivity, investment and expanding domestic consumption, not only through absorbing non-capitalist areas. Lenin and Bukharin criticised her arguments in her own time.

**2. Underestimating nationalism.** Her opposition to Polish independence and to national self-determination ignored the deep, genuine desire of peoples for their own states. Anti-colonial movements in India, Africa and elsewhere showed that nationalism could be a force for liberation, not only a tool of elites.

**3. Too much faith in spontaneity?** Her trust in the masses' spontaneous action sometimes underestimated the importance of organisation, leadership and strategy. The Spartacist uprising of January 1919, which she thought premature, was poorly organised and easily crushed, with fatal consequences for her and many others.

**4. Ambiguity about democracy and force.** While she defended democratic freedoms, she also supported a revolutionary "dictatorship of the proletariat" and accepted that revolution would involve force. Critics ask whether her democratic socialism could ever have worked in practice, or whether she simply never had power long enough to face the hard choices Lenin did.

**5. Dismissing reform's achievements.** Bernstein's reformist path led to European social democracies, welfare states and enormous improvements in workers' lives. Rosa's fear that reformism would merely stabilise capitalism was partly right, but she underestimated how much reform could achieve.

**6. Little on gender.** Though she was a woman in a male world and faced constant sexism, she wrote relatively little about women's oppression as a distinct issue, believing it would be solved by socialism. Later feminists found this inadequate.

**7. A revolution that never came.** She expected a revolution in Germany and Western Europe that would rescue the Russian revolution from isolation. It never happened. Instead, Germany eventually turned to Nazism.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought of revolutionaries as either heroes or villains, and of the struggle for justice as a choice between quietly reforming the system or violently overthrowing it. You may have thought that freedom of speech was a "liberal" idea, and that people who want radical change don't care about it.

**Now,** you can see that one of history's most committed revolutionaries made freedom for dissenters the test of any movement, and predicted, with terrible accuracy, what happens when people who want to change the world silence those who disagree. You can see capitalism's expansion into forests, villages and colonies as part of its basic logic. You can see that history has no guaranteed direction: it can lead to justice or to barbarism. And you can see that the deepest commitment to struggle can live alongside tenderness, for a bird, a buffalo or a friend.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A meeting where nobody disagrees with the boss** looks less like unity and more like danger.
- **A movement or party that silences critics** loses your trust, however good its cause.
- **News of a new mine or project in a forest** makes you ask who was living there first.
- **Small reforms** raise the question: steps toward something, or substitutes for it?
- **War fever** makes you think of the poor who will fight and die.
- **Your own mistakes**, in a team or a movement, become lessons rather than shames.
- **A bird outside your window** reminds you what all the struggle is for.

Rosa Luxemburg was small, limping and murdered at forty-seven, her body thrown into a canal. But her voice outlasted the men who killed her and the regimes that misused her name. Every time someone stands up for the right of the person who thinks differently, her words come back: "I was, I am, I shall be!"

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Rosa Luxemburg (1871–1919), Polish-Jewish-German Marxist economist, writer, teacher and revolutionary; leader of the Spartacus League; co-founder of the German Communist Party; murdered in 1919.
- **Context:** Russian-ruled Poland, discrimination against Jews and women, the rise of mass socialist parties, European imperialism, the First World War and the Russian Revolution.
- **Reform or revolution:** reforms as means, social transformation as aim (against Bernstein).
- **Mass strike and spontaneity:** workers learn through their own struggles; against Lenin's centralised vanguard party.
- **Accumulation of capital:** capitalism must expand into non-capitalist areas, explaining imperialism; she used India as one example.
- **Socialism or barbarism:** history has no guaranteed direction; the First World War showed civilisation could collapse.
- **Freedom for dissenters:** "Freedom is always and exclusively freedom for the one who thinks differently"; without democracy, socialism becomes bureaucratic dictatorship.
- **Internationalism:** opposed nationalism and even Polish independence.
- **Humanity:** prison letters about birds, plants and an abused buffalo.
- **Criticisms:** flawed economics, underestimated nationalism and reform, too much faith in spontaneity, little on women's oppression as such.

**Key terms**

- **Reformism:** the belief that socialism can be achieved gradually through reforms within capitalism.
- **Mass strike:** a wave of strikes spreading across industries and regions, combining economic and political demands.
- **Spontaneity:** the self-organised action of ordinary people, not directed by leaders.
- **Vanguard party:** Lenin's idea of a disciplined party of professional revolutionaries leading the workers.
- **Accumulation of capital:** the reinvestment of profit to expand production.
- **Imperialism:** the expansion of powerful states and their capital into other lands, often by force.
- **Socialism or barbarism:** her slogan that humanity faces a choice between progress and collapse.
- **Spartacus League:** the anti-war revolutionary group she co-founded during the First World War.
- **Freikorps:** right-wing paramilitary units that crushed revolutionary uprisings and murdered her.
- **Internationalism:** the belief that workers of all nations share common interests above national loyalties.
