## Start here

Say the word "accha" aloud.

Now think about how many things it can mean. "Accha" with a nod means *okay, fine*. "Accha?" with raised eyebrows means *really? is that so?* "Acchaaa..." stretched out means *now I understand*. "Accha, accha" said quickly means *yes, yes, I heard you, stop talking*. "Accha!" with a frown can even mean *oh, so that's your game*.

Same word. Six different meanings. And you, if you grew up hearing it, never get confused. You don't look it up in a dictionary. You know what it means from **how it is being used**: the tone, the face, the situation, the conversation around it.

This simple fact, that meaning lives in use, sounds obvious. But it overturned an idea that philosophers had held for over two thousand years: that every word is a **name** for some thing, and that its meaning is the thing it names. And the man who overturned it had, as a young man, built the most rigorous version of that old idea himself, then spent the second half of his life tearing it down.

His name was **Ludwig Wittgenstein**. He was born into one of the richest families in Europe and gave all his money away. He wrote one short, strange book that he believed had solved all the problems of philosophy, and then went to teach in a village school. He came back to Cambridge, where the economist John Maynard Keynes announced his arrival with the words: "Well, God has arrived." He thought philosophy was a kind of illness of thinking, and that his job was to cure it.

Many philosophers consider him the most important philosopher of the twentieth century. His ideas shaped how we think about language, meaning, the mind, mathematics, rules, and even artificial intelligence. By the end of this page, you'll understand why a man who thought most philosophy was nonsense became one of its greatest heroes, and how his ideas can help you notice when you are being tricked by your own words.

## The world he was born into

### Vienna at its height

Wittgenstein was born in **Vienna** in **1889**. It was the capital of the **Austro-Hungarian Empire**, a vast, ageing empire of many nations and languages ruled by the Habsburg emperor **Franz Joseph**.

Vienna around 1900 was one of the most creative cities in history. **Sigmund Freud** was inventing psychoanalysis. **Gustav Mahler** was conducting at the opera. **Gustav Klimt** was painting his golden portraits (including one of Wittgenstein's sister). Composers like **Arnold Schoenberg** were breaking the rules of music. Architects were stripping away decoration. Writers like **Karl Kraus** were attacking the empty, pompous language of newspapers and politics. Many Viennese felt that their society was full of hypocrisy and fake ornament, hiding emptiness underneath, and that the cure was **honesty, clarity and simplicity**.

### The Wittgenstein family

Ludwig's father, **Karl Wittgenstein**, was a steel magnate, one of the richest industrialists in Europe, often compared to the American Andrew Carnegie. The family's palace in Vienna had seven grand pianos, and musicians such as **Johannes Brahms** were guests.

But the family was unhappy. Karl was a demanding, domineering father who wanted his sons to become businessmen. Three of Ludwig's four older brothers **took their own lives**. Another brother, **Paul**, became a concert pianist, lost his right arm in the First World War, and kept playing with his left hand; the composer **Maurice Ravel** wrote his *Piano Concerto for the Left Hand* for him.

The family were of Jewish descent but had converted to Christianity; Ludwig was baptised a Catholic. Under the Nazis' racial laws, decades later, they would be classified as Jews.

### Logic in revolution

Meanwhile, in philosophy, a quiet revolution was happening. The German mathematician **Gottlob Frege** and the English philosopher **Bertrand Russell** had invented a powerful new **mathematical logic**. They hoped to show that all of mathematics could be built from logic alone. And they began to think that this new logic could reveal the hidden structure of language and thought, and so solve old philosophical problems.

Into this world came a restless young engineer from Vienna.

## The life, as a story

### The youngest son

Ludwig Josef Johann Wittgenstein was born on **26 April 1889**, the youngest of eight children. He was educated at home until fourteen, then sent to a school in **Linz**. By a strange coincidence, a boy named **Adolf Hitler** attended the same school at around the same time, though they were in different years and there is no evidence they knew each other.

Ludwig loved machines. As a boy, he built a working sewing machine out of wood and wire. He studied **mechanical engineering** in Berlin, then went to **Manchester** in England in **1908** to do research on aeronautics. He designed a new kind of propeller.

But designing propellers required mathematics, and mathematics led him to the question: what **is** mathematics? What are its foundations? He read Russell's *The Principles of Mathematics*, and was gripped.

### "Should I become an aeronaut?"

In **1911**, he went to see Frege in Germany, who advised him to study with Russell in **Cambridge**. Wittgenstein turned up at Russell's rooms unannounced and began attending his lectures, arguing with him relentlessly afterwards.

At the end of his first term, he asked Russell: "Will you please tell me whether I am a complete idiot or not? If I am, I shall become an aeronaut; but if not, I shall become a philosopher." Russell asked him to write something over the vacation. When Wittgenstein brought it, Russell read just the first sentence and told him: "No, you must not become an aeronaut."

Within a year, the student was teaching the teacher. Russell described him as "perhaps the most perfect example I have ever known of genius as traditionally conceived, passionate, profound, intense, and dominating."

He was also exhausting. He would pace Russell's rooms for hours in silence. Once Russell asked him, "Are you thinking about logic or about your sins?" "Both," he replied.

### A hut in Norway

Cambridge felt too social and false to him. In **1913**, he went to a remote village called **Skjolden** in **Norway** and built a small wooden hut above a lake, to think about logic in solitude.

That same year, his father died, leaving him one of the richest men in Europe.

### War and a book

When the **First World War** broke out in **1914**, he volunteered for the Austrian army as an ordinary soldier, though a medical condition could have excused him. He asked to be posted to the most dangerous positions. He was decorated several times for bravery.

In a bookshop in Galicia, he found **Leo Tolstoy**'s short version of the Gospels, *The Gospel in Brief*, and carried it everywhere, until his fellow soldiers called him "the man with the Gospels". The war, and Tolstoy, turned him towards questions of ethics, death and the meaning of life.

All through the war, he wrote philosophy in notebooks. By **1918**, he had finished a book. In November, he was captured by the Italian army and spent months as a prisoner of war in a camp at **Cassino**, below the abbey where Thomas Aquinas had been sent as a boy. From there, he sent the manuscript to Russell.

The book was published in **1921**, and in English, with Russell's introduction, in **1922**, under a Latin title suggested by the philosopher G. E. Moore: ***Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus***. In the preface, Wittgenstein wrote that he believed he had solved the problems of philosophy, "in essentials", once and for all.

### Giving it all away

So, having solved philosophy, he left it.

In **1919**, he gave away his entire fortune to his brothers and sisters, refusing to keep anything. (He would not give it to the poor, he said, because it would corrupt them; his siblings were already rich.) He trained as a schoolteacher and, from **1920** to **1926**, taught in elementary schools in poor villages in the mountains of rural Austria.

He was a passionate, inspiring and demanding teacher. He took children on trips to Vienna, taught them to build models and dissect animals, and wrote a spelling dictionary for village schools. But he was also impatient and harsh. He hit children who could not keep up. In **1926**, after he struck a boy who collapsed, he was investigated and left teaching. Years later, he went back to the village to apologise personally to the children he had hurt.

He worked as a gardener at a monastery. Then he spent two years designing and building a stark, precise modernist house in Vienna for his sister **Margarete**, obsessing over every detail; he once had a ceiling raised by three centimetres just before the house was finished.

### "God has arrived"

Meanwhile, the *Tractatus* had become famous. A group of philosophers and scientists in Vienna, the **Vienna Circle**, read it line by line. Some of them met him. And slowly, Wittgenstein began to suspect that his book had been wrong.

In **January 1929**, he returned to Cambridge. Keynes wrote to his wife: "Well, God has arrived. I met him on the 5.15 train."

He submitted the *Tractatus* as his doctoral thesis. His examiners were Russell and Moore. At the end of the oral examination, Wittgenstein clapped them both on the shoulder and said: "Don't worry, I know you'll never understand it."

### The teacher in the deckchair

He became a fellow and later, in **1939**, professor of philosophy at Cambridge. He did not lecture in the normal way. He sat in his bare rooms at Trinity College, with students on deckchairs, and **thought aloud**, struggling with problems in front of them, falling into long silences, sometimes exclaiming, "I'm a fool!" Students found it unforgettable. He urged many of them to give up philosophy and do honest, practical work instead.

An important influence on his new thinking was an Italian economist at Cambridge, **Piero Sraffa**. The story goes that Wittgenstein was insisting that a sentence and what it describes must share the same "logical form". Sraffa made a Neapolitan gesture of contempt, brushing his fingertips outward under his chin, and asked: "What is the logical form of **that**?" Wittgenstein said it made him feel like a tree with all its branches cut off. Decades later, the Indian economist **Amartya Sen**, a student of Sraffa's, wrote about how this friendship helped reshape twentieth-century philosophy.

During the **Second World War**, unable to bear teaching philosophy while others suffered, he worked as a **porter** at Guy's Hospital in London, carrying medicines to the wards, and then as a laboratory assistant in Newcastle.

### The poker

In **October 1946**, the philosopher **Karl Popper** gave a talk at Cambridge arguing that there are real philosophical problems, not just puzzles about language. Wittgenstein, who was holding a fireplace poker, waved it while they argued. Accounts disagree about exactly what happened next. Popper later claimed that when Wittgenstein demanded an example of a moral rule, he replied: "Not to threaten visiting lecturers with pokers." Wittgenstein left the room. Philosophers have argued about the ten minutes ever since.

### "Tell them I've had a wonderful life"

He resigned his professorship in **1947** to write. He lived for a time in a cottage on the remote west coast of **Ireland**, taming birds. He was diagnosed with cancer in **1949**. He spent his last weeks at the home of his doctor in Cambridge, writing almost until the end.

On **29 April 1951**, three days after his sixty-second birthday, he died. His last words, to his doctor's wife, were: "Tell them I've had a wonderful life."

His second great book, the ***Philosophical Investigations***, on which he had worked for sixteen years, was published two years later, in **1953**.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Language pictures the world (the early Wittgenstein)

**The idea in one line:** A meaningful sentence is like a picture of a possible fact; its parts stand for things, and the way they are arranged shows how the things are arranged.

The *Tractatus* begins: **"The world is everything that is the case."** The world, Wittgenstein says, is made up not of things, but of **facts**: things standing in certain relations. "The book is on the table" is a fact.

Then how does language connect to the world? Wittgenstein's answer is the **picture theory of meaning**. He got the idea, the story goes, from reading about a court case in Paris in which a car accident was reconstructed using toy cars and dolls. The toys stood for the real cars and people, and their arrangement showed how the accident happened.

A sentence, he said, works the same way. Its words stand for objects, and the way the words are arranged pictures how the objects are arranged. If the picture matches the world, the sentence is **true**; if not, **false**. Either way it has **sense**: it says something definite that could be checked.

Think of a map of the Delhi Metro. Each coloured line stands for a real route; each dot stands for a real station; the arrangement shows how they connect. You can use it to find your way, because its structure matches the structure of the real network.

**Why it matters:** The picture theory was a bold attempt to explain how language can be about the world at all. It inspired the **Vienna Circle** and the movement called **logical positivism**, which said that only statements that can be checked by experience (or are true by logic) have meaning.

**A common misunderstanding:** Wittgenstein did not think everyday sentences look like pictures on the surface. He thought their hidden logical structure, revealed by analysis, does.

### Idea 2: The limits of language, and what must be shown

**The idea in one line:** Language can only describe facts; ethics, the meaning of life, God and the self cannot be said, only shown, and about them we must be silent.

If meaningful sentences are pictures of possible facts, then what about sentences like "Killing is wrong", "Life has meaning", or "God exists"? They do not picture any fact that could be checked. So, strictly, they are **nonsense**: not false, but lacking sense.

**"The limits of my language mean the limits of my world,"** Wittgenstein wrote. And the book ends with its most famous line: **"Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent."**

Here is the surprise. Many readers in the Vienna Circle took this to mean: ethics and religion are meaningless rubbish; only science matters. But Wittgenstein meant almost the opposite. He wrote to a publisher that his book had two parts: the part he had written, and the part he had **not** written, and that the second part was the important one. "We feel that even if all possible scientific questions be answered," he wrote, "the problems of life have still not been touched at all." Ethics, beauty and the meaning of life are not facts in the world. They are the most important things, and they cannot be put into words. They **show themselves** in how we live.

He even admitted that the sentences of his own book were, by its own standards, nonsense. They are like a **ladder**: you climb up it, and then you must throw it away.

Indian traditions know this thought well. The Upanishads describe the ultimate reality as that "from which words turn back, along with the mind, without reaching it" (*Taittiriya Upanishad*). The Buddha kept a "noble silence" on questions he thought words could not settle. Zen masters answer questions with a slap or a silence.

**Why it matters:** The *Tractatus* draws a line between what science can say and what lies beyond it. You can read it as defending the importance of the unsayable, not dismissing it.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent" is often quoted as "shut up if you don't know what you're talking about". Wittgenstein meant something deeper: some things are too important for words.

### Idea 3: Meaning is use (the later Wittgenstein)

**The idea in one line:** A word does not get its meaning from an object it names, but from how it is used in the life of the people who speak it.

The *Philosophical Investigations* begins with a quotation from **Augustine**'s *Confessions*. Augustine describes how he learned to speak as a child: when adults named something and pointed to it, he grasped that the sound was the name of that thing.

Wittgenstein says this is a **picture of language**: every word names an object, and its meaning is that object. It was the picture behind his own *Tractatus*. And it is far too narrow.

Think about these words: "hello", "if", "ouch", "not", "five", "please", "accha". What objects do they name? None. Their meaning lies in what we **do** with them: greet, connect, cry out, deny, count, request, agree, doubt.

So Wittgenstein proposes: **"The meaning of a word is its use in the language."** If you want to know what a word means, don't ask what it stands for; ask how it is used.

He gives an example. Imagine a builder and his assistant. The builder calls "Slab!" and the assistant brings a slab. Here, "Slab!" is not a name; it means something like "Bring me a slab!" Its meaning is its role in their work.

Indian English is a wonderful laboratory for this. "Prepone", "timepass", "do the needful", "adjust karo", "I'll come and go" (meaning *I'm going now*). A dictionary written in London might call them errors. But they have perfectly clear meanings, given by how millions of people use them.

**Why it matters:** This idea changed philosophy, linguistics and even computer science. Modern AI language models, which learn the meaning of words from how they appear among other words in vast amounts of text, work on a principle close to Wittgenstein's: meaning lies in use.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Meaning is use" does not mean anything goes. Use follows shared rules and practices. If you use "accha" to mean "elephant", no one will understand you.

### Idea 4: Language games and forms of life

**The idea in one line:** Language is not one thing but many different activities, "language games", each woven into a way of living.

Wittgenstein compares language to a **toolbox**: hammer, saw, glue, screwdriver. The tools look similar from outside, but they have very different jobs. Words, too, do very different jobs.

He calls each kind of activity with words a **language game**: giving orders, describing an object, telling a story, making a joke, thanking, cursing, praying, reporting a match, solving a maths problem. Each has its own rules, like a game.

And language games are part of **forms of life**: the shared practices, habits, needs and ways of acting of a community. To understand the word "dowry", or "jugaad", or "namaste", you need to understand the form of life in which it is used.

He made a striking remark: **"If a lion could talk, we could not understand him."** Even if a lion spoke perfect Hindi, its form of life, what it cares about, fears and does, is so different from ours that its words would not mean what ours mean.

**Why it matters:** Many misunderstandings, between generations, cultures, professions and political camps, come from people playing different language games with the same words. "Freedom" in a farmer's protest, a stock market report and a love song are not the same game.

**A common misunderstanding:** Calling them "games" doesn't mean language is trivial. Games are serious, rule-governed activities, and Wittgenstein used the word to stress that language is something we **do**.

### Idea 5: Family resemblance

**The idea in one line:** Many words have no single essence shared by all the things they cover, only a network of overlapping similarities, like the resemblances in a family.

Ever since **Socrates**, philosophers have asked: what is justice? What is courage? What is knowledge? And they assumed there must be one essence, one definition, that all cases share.

Wittgenstein asks us to consider the word **"game"**. Cricket, chess, kabaddi, solitaire, ludo, hide-and-seek, a video game. What do they all have in common? Winning and losing? Not solitaire, or a child throwing a ball against a wall. Skill? Not snakes and ladders. Teams? Not chess. Amusement? Not a professional match with crores at stake.

**"Don't think, but look!"** he says. If you look, you won't find one thing common to all games, but "a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing". He calls this **family resemblance**. In a family, one child has the father's nose, another the mother's eyes, a third the grandfather's laugh and the father's eyes. No single feature is shared by all, yet they clearly belong together.

Indian law met this problem in **1968**, when the Supreme Court had to decide whether **rummy** was a game of chance (gambling) or a game of skill. There was no single essence of "gambling" to look up. The judges had to look at how the game is actually played. Similar arguments continue over online games.

**Why it matters:** Family resemblance frees us from the hunt for perfect definitions. Words like "religion", "art", "democracy", "family" and "Indian" have no single essence, and arguments that demand one often go nowhere.

**A common misunderstanding:** This doesn't mean words have no clear meaning. We use "game" with perfect competence every day. We just can't capture that competence in one neat definition.

### Idea 6: No private language

**The idea in one line:** Words for our inner experiences, like "pain", get their meaning from shared public life, not from a private inner pointing.

Since **Descartes**, many philosophers had imagined the mind as a private inner theatre. I know my own sensations directly and with certainty; other people's minds I can only guess at. And the words for sensations, like "pain", get their meaning from my private inner experience: I feel something, and I name it.

Wittgenstein argued that this picture cannot work. Imagine trying to keep a diary of a private sensation, marking "S" each day you feel it. How would you know you were using "S" correctly? Whatever seems right to you would be right, which means there is no such thing as getting it right. A rule that cannot be broken is not a rule.

He gives an image. Suppose everyone had a box with something in it, which they call a "beetle". No one can look into anyone else's box; everyone knows what a beetle is only by looking at their own. The thing in the box could be different for everyone, or constantly changing, or even nothing. It would make no difference to how the word "beetle" is used. The thing in the box "drops out of consideration".

So how do we learn the word "pain"? Wittgenstein suggests: a child hurts itself and cries; adults talk to it and teach it exclamations and, later, sentences. Words for pain **replace** the natural expression of pain. Their meaning lives in a shared public world of crying, comforting, wincing and caring.

**Why it matters:** This argument challenged the whole picture of the mind as a private inner theatre and influenced psychology, cognitive science and medicine. Doctors who use pain scales and watch patients' faces and behaviour are working with the public life of pain.

**A common misunderstanding:** Wittgenstein did not deny that people have inner experiences, or say that pain is "just behaviour". He was attacking a picture of how the **words** for experiences get their meaning.

### Idea 7: Philosophy as therapy

**The idea in one line:** Many philosophical problems are not real problems but confusions caused by language, and the job of philosophy is to dissolve them, not to answer them.

The later Wittgenstein thought philosophers get into trouble when **"language goes on holiday"**: when words are taken out of their ordinary use and made to do work they cannot do. We ask "What is time?" as if "time" names a mysterious thing, like "the river" names a river. Then we get puzzled, because we cannot find the thing.

**"Philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language,"** he wrote. And: **"What is your aim in philosophy? To shew the fly the way out of the fly-bottle."** The fly keeps buzzing against the glass, unable to see the open top. The philosopher keeps hitting the same puzzle, unable to see that the way out is to look at how the words are actually used.

So Wittgenstein's method was not to build theories but to **remind** us of how we actually use words, to give examples, invent imaginary cases, and ask questions until the puzzle dissolves. He compared philosophy to **therapy**.

Remember Augustine's famous puzzle about time: "If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it, I know not." Wittgenstein loved this passage. Augustine knew perfectly well how to use the word "time"; he only got lost when he tried to find the thing it named.

**Why it matters:** Wittgenstein's method is useful far beyond philosophy. Many arguments in politics, law, relationships and offices are not about facts at all but about words being used in different ways.

**A common misunderstanding:** Wittgenstein did not think the problems of life are illusions. He thought **philosophical** confusions about words are. The problems of how to live remained, for him, deeply real.

### Idea 8: Seeing-as and certainty

**The idea in one line:** We don't just see things; we see them **as** something, and our knowledge rests on certainties we never question, not because we proved them, but because everything else depends on them.

Wittgenstein made famous the **duck-rabbit**: a simple drawing that can be seen as a duck or as a rabbit. Nothing in the picture changes, but suddenly you see it differently. He called this **"seeing-as"** or the "dawning of an aspect".

In his last notes, published as ***On Certainty***, he explored how knowledge works. We test our beliefs against other beliefs. But some things we never test: that I have two hands, that the earth existed before I was born, that my name is what it is. These are not conclusions we reached; they are the **hinges** on which all our questioning turns. Doubt itself only makes sense against a background of certainty.

**Why it matters:** Seeing-as explains how two people can look at the same facts, the same budget, the same match, the same historical event, and see entirely different things. *On Certainty* shows why some disagreements, between people with different "hinges", cannot be settled by evidence alone.

**A common misunderstanding:** Hinge certainties are not prejudices that can never change. Over time, Wittgenstein said, the riverbed itself can shift. But they cannot all be doubted at once.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Augustine: the picture he rejected

Wittgenstein admired Augustine deeply, and called the *Confessions* possibly "the most serious book ever written". But he chose Augustine's account of learning words to open the *Philosophical Investigations*, precisely because it was a clear, natural, and wrong picture of language. The rest of the book shows why. Imagine them talking:

*Augustine:* When my elders named an object and pointed to it, I understood that the sound was its name.

*Wittgenstein:* And how did you understand that the pointing was to the object, and not to its colour, or its shape, or its number?

*Augustine:* I just knew.

*Wittgenstein:* Then you already knew a great deal of language before you learned it. Pointing works only inside a form of life.

### Socrates: the hunt for definitions

**Socrates** asked people, "What is courage?" and refused examples: he wanted the one essence common to every case. Wittgenstein called this "the craving for generality" and answered with family resemblance. Yet he shared Socrates' sense that philosophy is an activity, done in conversation, that changes the person doing it.

### Descartes: the private mind

**Descartes** began with the certainty of his own thinking and then tried to prove the existence of the outside world. Wittgenstein turned this upside down: our inner words get their meaning from a shared public world, and doubt makes sense only against a background of certainty.

### Kierkegaard and Tolstoy: the things that matter

Wittgenstein called **Kierkegaard** "by far the most profound thinker of the last century", and reread **Tolstoy** and **Dostoevsky** constantly. Like Kierkegaard, he believed the most important things in life, faith, ethics, meaning, cannot be proved but only lived, and that a person's life shows what their words cannot say.

### Russell and the Vienna Circle: misunderstood

**Bertrand Russell** made him famous, but Wittgenstein thought Russell's introduction to the *Tractatus* misunderstood it. The **Vienna Circle** thought he was one of them, a champion of science against metaphysics; he would turn his back on them and read them poetry by Rabindranath **Tagore** instead. (According to those present, at one meeting with members of the Circle, Wittgenstein turned his back on them and read Tagore's poems aloud.)

### Later voices

- **Gilbert Ryle** and **J. L. Austin** at Oxford developed "ordinary language philosophy", studying how words are used in daily life.
- **Elizabeth Anscombe**, his student, translated his work and became one of the great philosophers of the century.
- **Amartya Sen** wrote about how Sraffa's ideas helped turn Wittgenstein towards the social, public nature of language.
- **Karl Popper** argued that Wittgenstein was wrong to reduce philosophy to puzzles about language.
- **Ernest Gellner** attacked ordinary language philosophy in a fierce book, *Words and Things* (1959).

## See it around you

**WhatsApp misunderstandings.** A message saying "Ok." can mean *fine*, *whatever*, or *I'm furious*. Without tone and face, the language game breaks down. That's why we add emojis: to rebuild the use.

**Code-switching.** Indians switch between languages in a single sentence, Hindi, English, Tamil, Bengali, and understand each other perfectly. Meaning lives in shared use, not in dictionaries.

**Legal definitions.** Courts constantly face Wittgenstein's problem: what counts as a "religion", a "game of skill", "obscenity", a "family"? There is rarely a single essence; judges must look at use and context.

**Political words.** "Development", "nationalism", "secular", "freedom": each side plays a different language game with the same word, and they talk past each other.

**AI chatbots.** Modern language models learn meaning from how words are used together in vast amounts of text, a large-scale test of the idea that meaning is use.

**Customer service scripts.** "Your call is important to us" is a sentence with no real meaning in its use; everyone knows it. Wittgenstein would say: look at the use, not the words.

**Doctors and pain.** "On a scale of one to ten, how bad is the pain?" Medicine makes pain public: through scales, faces, behaviour. That's how the word works.

**The duck-rabbit everywhere.** The same cricket decision, budget or news photo seen in two completely different ways by two different people.

## Use it

**1. When an argument goes round in circles, check the words.** Ask: are we using the same word in different ways? Define your terms by examples, not abstractions.

**2. Look at use, not dictionaries.** To understand a new word, a slang term, a technical term, a word from another language, watch how people use it.

**3. Don't demand perfect definitions.** When someone says "Define religion" or "Define success" before they'll discuss it, remind them of family resemblance. Look at cases.

**4. Notice the language game.** Before you respond to a message, ask what game is being played: a joke, a complaint, a request, a test? Many fights come from mistaking one for another.

**5. Try seeing-as.** In a disagreement, try deliberately to see the situation as the other person sees it, like flipping between duck and rabbit.

**6. Be silent where words fail.** At a funeral, in grief, in awe, don't force words. Some things are shown better by presence and action than by speech.

**7. Find your hinges.** Write down a few things you are certain of without proof. Notice how they shape everything else you believe, and how they differ from other people's.

**8. Show the fly the way out.** When you're stuck on a puzzling question, "What is time?", "What is love?", ask whether the problem is the question itself.

## Where he went wrong

**1. A cruel teacher.** He hit children in the village schools, sometimes hard, and pulled girls' hair. However much he regretted it later, real children were harmed.

**2. Dismissing philosophy too quickly.** Critics like Russell and Popper argued that Wittgenstein reduced deep problems, about knowledge, mind and morality, to word games. Russell said the later Wittgenstein seemed to have "grown tired of serious thinking". Many philosophers believe real problems remain even after the words are clarified.

**3. "Philosophy leaves everything as it is."** He wrote that philosophy cannot change our practices, only describe them. Critics say this makes it conservative: if all meaning lies in existing use, how can we criticise unjust practices, or the language that props them up?

**4. Obscure and oracular.** His later writings are collections of numbered remarks, questions and puzzles, not arguments. Readers disagree endlessly about what he meant, and his followers sometimes treated his words almost like scripture.

**5. Troubling influences.** He admired **Otto Weininger**, the author of a misogynistic and antisemitic book, and in private notebooks he wrote harsh remarks about Jewish thinkers, including himself, as merely "reproductive" rather than original, reflecting the prejudices of his Vienna.

**6. Overwhelming personality.** He could be domineering, intolerant of disagreement and harsh to friends and students, discouraging some from careers they loved.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought that words are simply labels for things, that every important word has one true definition, and that every deep question has an answer if only we think hard enough. You may have thought of Wittgenstein, if at all, as an impossibly difficult philosopher of logic.

**Now,** you can see words as tools used in the life of a community, "accha" with its six meanings, "game" with its family of cases, "pain" learned through crying and comfort. You can see that many arguments are about words being used in different language games. You can see that some things cannot be said, only shown, and that some puzzles are flies in a bottle. And you can see the limits: a cruel teacher, a philosophy that can seem to leave the world as it is, and a style that invites endless argument.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A misunderstood WhatsApp message** reveals a broken language game.
- **A demand for a definition** reminds you of family resemblance.
- **A political slogan** makes you ask how the word is being used, and by whom.
- **A silence at a funeral** says more than words could.
- **A heated debate** reveals two people seeing a duck and a rabbit in the same picture.
- **A puzzling question** makes you check whether it's the question that's the problem.

A man who believed he had solved philosophy gave away his fortune, taught children in mountain villages, came back to destroy his own answer, and spent the rest of his life teaching that the clearest thinking begins with looking at how we actually speak. He died saying he'd had a wonderful life.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951), Austrian-British philosopher; one of the founders of analytic philosophy.
- **Context:** Vienna around 1900; the rich, tragic Wittgenstein family; the new logic of Frege and Russell; two world wars.
- **Life:** engineering in Berlin and Manchester; Russell's student at Cambridge (1911); hut in Norway (1913); soldier and prisoner of war; *Tractatus* (1921); gave away his fortune (1919); village teacher (1920–26); house for his sister; back to Cambridge (1929); professor (1939); hospital porter in the war; resigned (1947); died in Cambridge (1951); *Philosophical Investigations* (1953).
- **Picture theory:** sentences picture possible facts.
- **Limits of language:** ethics and the meaning of life cannot be said, only shown; the ladder.
- **Meaning is use:** words get meaning from their role in life.
- **Language games and forms of life:** language as many activities woven into ways of living.
- **Family resemblance:** no single essence, but overlapping similarities.
- **Private language argument:** words for sensations live in public life; the beetle in the box.
- **Philosophy as therapy:** showing the fly the way out of the fly-bottle.
- **Seeing-as and hinge certainties.**
- **Criticisms:** cruelty as a teacher, dismissing real problems, conservatism, obscurity, troubling prejudices, domineering personality.

**Key terms**

- **Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus:** his early book on logic, language and the limits of what can be said.
- **Picture theory:** the early view that meaningful sentences are logical pictures of facts.
- **Saying and showing:** what can be stated in words versus what can only be shown.
- **Language game:** an activity with words, governed by its own rules, part of a way of life.
- **Form of life:** the shared practices and ways of acting in which language is embedded.
- **Family resemblance:** a network of overlapping similarities without a single common essence.
- **Private language argument:** the argument that there cannot be a language whose words refer to experiences only the speaker can know.
- **Logical positivism:** the Vienna Circle's view that only empirically checkable or logical statements have meaning.
- **Hinge propositions:** certainties that are not proved but on which all our doubting and testing turn.
