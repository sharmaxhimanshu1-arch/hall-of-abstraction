## Life

Socrates was born in Athens around 470 BCE, the son of a stonemason, Sophroniscus, and a midwife, Phaenarete. He served as a hoplite in the Peloponnesian War and was praised for his courage at Potidaea and Delium. He married Xanthippe and had three sons. Otherwise he seems to have spent his life almost entirely in public: in the marketplace, the gymnasia and the houses of friends, talking.

He was famous, and to many infuriating, for that talk. He would stop a general to ask what courage is, a priest to ask what piety is, or a politician to ask what justice is, and then show through patient questioning that their confident answers fell apart. When his friend Chaerephon asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, the oracle said no. Socrates took this as a riddle. He concluded that he was wiser only in one respect: he knew that he did not know.

In 399 BCE, in a democracy still raw from military defeat and a brief oligarchic tyranny, three citizens charged him with impiety, "not believing in the gods the city believes in", and with corrupting the young. Several of his associates, including Alcibiades and Critias, had been notorious enemies of the democracy, and that probably weighed with the jury. He was convicted by a narrow margin and sentenced to death. He refused to beg for mercy, declined his friends' plan for escape, and drank hemlock in prison.

## Key ideas

Because Socrates wrote nothing, we know him through others: the comic portrait in Aristophanes' *Clouds*, the memoirs of the soldier-writer Xenophon and, above all, the dialogues of Plato. Separating the historical Socrates from Plato's literary character is one of the oldest puzzles in philosophy (the "Socratic problem"). Most scholars treat Plato's earlier dialogues as the closest guide.

**The Socratic method (elenchus).** Socrates asks for a definition ("What is courage?"), draws out the speaker's other beliefs, and shows that they contradict the definition. The result is usually *aporia*, a productive bewilderment. The goal is not to win but to purge false confidence and make real inquiry possible.

**Socratic ignorance.** Awareness of the limits of one's knowledge is the beginning of wisdom. The person who thinks they know, but does not, cannot learn.

**Virtue is knowledge.** Socrates held that no one does wrong willingly. If we truly knew what was good, we would do it, so wrongdoing is a kind of ignorance. This intellectualist view makes ethics a matter of inquiry rather than obedience.

**Care of the soul.** The most important thing a person can do is to make their soul as good as possible, which matters far more than wealth, reputation or even life. It is better to suffer injustice than to commit it.

## Influence and legacy

Almost every ancient school claimed Socrates as an ancestor. Plato made him the hero of his dialogues. The Cynics admired his indifference to comfort, and the Stoics his calm in the face of death. His trial became the archetype of the individual conscience standing against the state, invoked by thinkers from Thomas More to Martin Luther King Jr.

His method survives in law schools, in philosophy seminars and in any classroom where a teacher answers a question with a better question. Philosophy itself is often divided into "Presocratic" and after, a sign of how decisively he changed its course.
