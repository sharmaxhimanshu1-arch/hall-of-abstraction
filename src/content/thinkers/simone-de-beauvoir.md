## Start here

Watch a small child at a birthday party. Someone gives a girl a doll, a pink kitchen set, a tiny dupatta. Someone gives a boy a toy car, a cricket bat, a toy gun. When the girl climbs a tree, an aunt says, "Arre, be careful, you're a girl." When the boy cries, an uncle says, "Boys don't cry." When the girl talks loudly, she's "too much"; when the boy does, he's "confident".

By the time they're teenagers, the girl has heard thousands of times: sit properly, don't go out late, learn to cook, think of what in-laws will say, don't be too ambitious or no one will marry you. The boy has heard: be strong, earn well, don't show weakness, protect your sisters, you'll be the head of the family.

And then, as adults, people look at them and say: "See? Women are **naturally** more caring, more emotional, less interested in power. Men are **naturally** more ambitious, more rational, better leaders. It's just nature."

Is it?

A French philosopher in **1949** looked at this whole process and wrote a sentence that changed the world:

"**One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman.**"

She didn't mean that biology doesn't exist. She meant that what a society calls "being a woman", the behaviours, the roles, the limits, the expectations, the very sense of oneself, is not simply given by nature. It is **made**, through upbringing, education, culture, law, religion, myth, and countless small daily pressures. And it has been made, for most of history, in a particular way: woman as the "**Other**", defined in relation to man, never as a full human subject in her own right.

Her name was **Simone de Beauvoir**. She was a brilliant philosopher, a prize-winning novelist, a memoirist, a political activist, and for fifty-one years the partner of Jean-Paul Sartre. Her book ***The Second Sex*** was put on the Vatican's list of forbidden books, attacked as obscene, and mocked by famous men. It also became one of the most influential books of the twentieth century, a foundation of modern **feminism**.

By the end of this page, you'll understand how a "dutiful daughter" became one of the great rebels of her age, what it means to be made into "the Other", and why her ideas matter not only for women, but for anyone who has been defined by someone else.

## The world she was born into

### A bourgeois Catholic family

Simone de Beauvoir was born in **Paris** in **1908**, into a **bourgeois** (upper-middle-class) Catholic family with aristocratic pretensions. Her father, **Georges**, was a lawyer who loved the theatre and was an unbeliever; her mother, **Françoise**, was a devout, strict Catholic who supervised her daughters' reading and religious education.

In this world, a girl's future was clear: she would be educated to a certain level, kept pure, married to a suitable man with a good dowry, and become a wife and mother. Her worth would depend on her marriage.

### A family fallen on hard times

But after the **First World War**, the family lost much of its money. Her father's investments collapsed. Without a dowry, Simone and her younger sister **Hélène** would have trouble making good marriages. Her father said, with a mixture of pride and bitterness: "**Simone has a man's brain.**" She would have to work for a living.

This misfortune turned out to be her liberation. Because she couldn't count on marriage, she was allowed to pursue higher education, at a time when very few women did.

### Women in France

French women in Beauvoir's youth had few rights. They could not **vote** until **1944** (women first voted in 1945). Married women needed their husbands' permission to work or open a bank account until **1965**. Contraception was banned until **1967**; abortion was illegal until **1975**. The ideal woman was the devoted wife and mother, and the Catholic Church taught that her place was in the home.

### Existentialism and war

Like Sartre, Beauvoir lived through the **Nazi occupation** of France (1940–44), and her philosophy was forged in that experience of freedom, choice, complicity and resistance. After the war came the existentialist movement, the Cold War, and the struggles for decolonisation, all of which she engaged with.

## The life, as a story

### The dutiful daughter

Simone Lucie Ernestine Marie Bertrand de Beauvoir was born on **9 January 1908**. As a child, she was intensely devout, imagining herself as a saint or a nun. She was clever, studious and fiercely ambitious, always first in her class at the Catholic girls' school she attended.

At about fourteen, she realised she **no longer believed in God**. She kept this secret from her mother for years. Without God, she felt, the world was empty, but also hers.

Her closest childhood friend was **Élisabeth Lacoin**, known as "**Zaza**", a brilliant, lively girl. Zaza's family was even more traditionally Catholic. When Zaza fell in love with a young philosopher, Maurice Merleau-Ponty, her family opposed the match. Zaza became ill and died suddenly in **1929**, aged twenty-one. Beauvoir believed for the rest of her life that Zaza had been killed by the stifling bourgeois morality that crushed her spirit. She ends her *Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter* (1958) with Zaza's death, saying that for a long time she had believed she paid for her own freedom with Zaza's death.

### Philosophy and Sartre

Beauvoir studied philosophy at the **Sorbonne**, one of the few women there. In **1929**, she sat the competitive national **agrégation** exam in philosophy. She came **second**, behind Jean-Paul Sartre, who was taking it for the second time. At twenty-one, she was the youngest person ever to pass it. Some of the examiners reportedly hesitated over which of the two should be first.

She and Sartre had met while preparing for the exam. He gave her a nickname, "**Castor**" (French for "beaver"), from a friend's pun on her name and English "Beaver", because she was so hard-working. They began a relationship that would last until his death in 1980.

They made a famous pact: their love was "**essential**", but they would also allow each other "**contingent**" loves, and tell each other everything. They would never marry or live together. Beauvoir said marriage would have turned their relationship into an institution.

The pact gave both of them great freedom. It also caused pain, to themselves and especially to the younger people, often women, drawn into their "trios" and affairs. Beauvoir's relationships with some of her former female students, and the way she and Sartre shared and discussed their lovers, have been sharply criticised.

### Teacher and writer

In the 1930s, Beauvoir taught philosophy at girls' **lycées** in Marseille, Rouen and then Paris. She was a dazzling teacher, popular with students. In **1943**, during the occupation, she was dismissed from teaching after a complaint by a student's mother accusing her of corrupting her daughter.

The same year, she published her first novel, ***She Came to Stay***, about a couple whose lives are disrupted by a young woman who joins them, based partly on her and Sartre's real "trio" with a former student. It explores how other consciousnesses threaten one's own sense of self.

### The existentialist years

After the liberation, Beauvoir was at the centre of the existentialist movement. With Sartre, she founded ***Les Temps modernes*** in **1945**. She wrote philosophical essays: *Pyrrhus and Cineas* (1944) and ***The Ethics of Ambiguity*** (1947), which tried to develop an existentialist ethics, something Sartre promised but never completed.

### America and Algren

In **1947**, she travelled across the **United States** for four months, lecturing and observing. She wrote a travel book, *America Day by Day*. In **Chicago**, she met the novelist **Nelson Algren**, and they began a passionate transatlantic love affair that lasted several years. Her letters to him show a side of her far from the cool intellectual: tender, playful, longing. But she would not leave Paris or Sartre, and Algren eventually ended the relationship bitterly.

America also opened her eyes to **racism**: she was shocked by segregation, and her observations of how Black Americans were treated as inferior, defined by white people, helped shape her analysis of how women are made into "the Other".

### The Second Sex

In the late 1940s, Beauvoir set out to write about herself. She asked: what has it meant to me to be a woman? Sartre reportedly told her that she had been raised differently from a boy, and that she should look into it. She did, and discovered that the question was enormous.

She spent about two years researching **biology**, **psychoanalysis**, **history**, **myths**, **literature** and **women's lived experience**, from childhood to old age. The result was ***The Second Sex*** (*Le Deuxième Sexe*), published in **1949** in two volumes, about a thousand pages.

It caused a **sensation and a scandal**. The first volume sold around 22,000 copies in its first week. Its frank discussion of sexuality, lesbianism, abortion, the realities of marriage and motherhood horrified many. The Catholic writer **François Mauriac** wrote to a contributor at *Les Temps modernes* that he now knew everything about his employer's vagina. Beauvoir received obscene letters and was mocked in the street. The **Vatican** placed the book on its **Index of Forbidden Books**.

It was translated into English in **1953**, in an abridged and often inaccurate version by a zoologist, H. M. Parshley; a fuller translation appeared only in **2009**. It became a foundational text for the "**second wave**" of feminism in the 1960s and 1970s, in America, Europe and around the world.

### The Mandarins

In **1954**, her novel ***The Mandarins***, about French left-wing intellectuals after the war, won the **Prix Goncourt**, France's most prestigious literary prize. With the money she bought an apartment near Montparnasse Cemetery, where she lived for the rest of her life.

From 1952 to 1959 she lived with the young journalist **Claude Lanzmann** (who later made the great Holocaust documentary *Shoah*), the only man she ever lived with.

### Memoirs

She then wrote a series of extraordinary **memoirs**: *Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter* (1958), *The Prime of Life* (1960), *Force of Circumstance* (1963), *All Said and Done* (1972). Together, they form one of the great autobiographies of the century, and a portrait of French intellectual life.

She also wrote ***A Very Easy Death*** (1964), a moving account of her mother's death from cancer, and ***The Coming of Age*** (1970), a major study of how society treats **old people**.

### Activism

Beauvoir was politically active all her life. She opposed the Algerian war and defended **Djamila Boupacha**, a young Algerian woman tortured and raped by French soldiers, helping to bring her case to public attention. In **1971**, she signed, and is credited with writing, the "**Manifesto of the 343**", in which 343 French women, including famous actresses and writers, publicly declared that they had had **illegal abortions**, daring the state to prosecute them. It helped lead to the legalisation of abortion in France in 1975 (the "Veil law").

In the 1970s, she publicly declared herself a **feminist** and joined the women's liberation movement, which she had earlier thought would be made unnecessary by socialism.

### The end

After Sartre died in **1980**, she wrote ***Adieux: A Farewell to Sartre*** (1981), a frank, painful account of his last years, which some of his admirers resented.

She died on **14 April 1986**, six years almost to the day after Sartre, and was buried beside him in **Montparnasse Cemetery**, Paris. She was wearing the ring Nelson Algren had given her.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Woman as the Other

**The idea in one line:** In patriarchal society, man is defined as the norm, the Subject, and woman is defined only in relation to him, as the Other.

*The Second Sex* begins with a question: **What is a woman?**

Notice something odd, Beauvoir says. A man would never write a book about "the situation of the male". A man never begins by saying, "I am a man." It goes without saying. Man is the default human. Woman is the **special case**.

She gives examples. In French (as in English), "man" (*homme*) means both a male person and human beings in general, "mankind". The masculine is the neutral; the feminine is marked as different. If a woman argues with a man, he may say, "You think that because you're a woman," as if her views were distorted by her sex, while his were simply objective.

She wrote:

"He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other."

What does "the Other" mean? Beauvoir borrowed the idea from **Hegel**. Every consciousness defines itself by contrast with what it is not. "We" define ourselves against "them": the people of one village against those of the next, one nation against another, one religion against another. This is universal. But usually, the Other can turn the tables: "they" also see "us" as Other. The relation is **reciprocal**.

With men and women, Beauvoir argues, the relationship has **not been reciprocal**. Men have defined themselves as the essential, the norm, and woman as the inessential, the Other, defined in relation to them: as someone's daughter, wife, mother, sister. And women, for many reasons, have often accepted this definition.

Think of how women in many Indian families are referred to: "Sharma ji ki beti", "Gupta ji ki bahu", "Raju ki mummy". Her own name disappears. Think of how a married woman's surname traditionally changes to her husband's. Think of how a woman is often asked first about her marriage, while a man is asked about his work. She exists in relation to men.

Even philosophers defined women as deficient men. Beauvoir quotes **Aristotle**, who wrote that the female is a female by virtue of a certain lack of qualities, and **St Thomas Aquinas**, who called woman an "imperfect man", an "incidental" being.

**Why it matters:** This concept became the foundation of feminist analysis. It also applies to other groups: Beauvoir herself compared the situation of women to that of **Black Americans**, **Jews**, and the **colonised**, each defined as the Other by a dominant group. In India, we can see it in caste: dominant castes defining themselves as pure and normal, Dalits as polluted and "other". Ambedkar's struggle was, in this sense, a struggle against being made the Other.

**A common misunderstanding:** Beauvoir didn't say men are evil or that all men consciously oppress women. She described a **structure** of meaning and power, built up over millennia, in which both men and women are caught. Many men benefit from it without noticing; many women participate in it.

### Idea 2: One is not born, but becomes, a woman

**The idea in one line:** Womanhood, as society defines it, is not a natural fact but the product of upbringing, culture and situation.

Volume II of *The Second Sex*, called "Lived Experience", begins with the famous sentence:

"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."

She continues: "No biological, psychological, or economic fate determines the figure that the human female presents in society; it is civilization as a whole that produces this creature."

What does this mean in practice? Beauvoir traces the life of a girl step by step:

**Childhood.** At first, boys and girls are much the same. But soon, the girl is treated differently. She's dressed up, admired for her looks, told to be gentle and pretty. She's given dolls, and learns that she is meant to be a mother, an object to be looked at. The boy is encouraged to be active, adventurous, to climb, fight, explore. He learns that he is a subject who acts on the world. She learns that she is an object to be seen and chosen.

**Adolescence.** The girl's body changes, and she learns to feel ashamed or anxious about it: menstruation, the need to hide, the danger of men's looks. She learns that her future depends on being chosen by a man. Her ambitions shrink. She learns to wait.

**Marriage and motherhood.** Society presents these as her destiny and fulfilment. Beauvoir describes the reality: the repetitive, endless housework that produces nothing lasting ("few tasks are more like the torture of Sisyphus than housework, with its endless repetition"), dependence on a husband, the loss of one's own projects.

At every stage, Beauvoir shows how what looks like "nature" is really the result of **situation**: of what girls are taught, allowed, forbidden, expected to be.

Now, think of the scenes from our opening: the doll and the cricket bat, "be careful, you're a girl", "boys don't cry". Think of how many girls in India are still taken out of school when they reach puberty, how many are told "padhai karke kya karegi, shaadi toh karni hai" (what will you do with studies, you have to get married anyway), how many young women give up careers after marriage or childbirth. Beauvoir would say: these are not facts of nature. They are how women are **made**.

And notice: the same process makes **men**. Boys are made into "real men" through pressure not to cry, to be tough, to earn, to dominate. This also limits them.

**Why it matters:** This sentence is the root of the modern distinction between **sex** (biological differences) and **gender** (the social roles and meanings attached to them). Beauvoir herself didn't use these exact terms, but later feminists built on her idea. It means that gender roles can change, because they were made, not given.

**A common misunderstanding:** Beauvoir didn't deny biology. She devoted a whole chapter to it. Women do menstruate, get pregnant, give birth. But she argued that biology doesn't determine what these facts **mean** or what women **can do**. "The body is not a thing, it is a situation": it is experienced and interpreted within a society. In a society that values women, pregnancy can be an honour; in one that doesn't, a burden; in one with good healthcare and shared childcare, it limits a woman far less.

### Idea 3: Immanence and transcendence

**The idea in one line:** Men have been allowed to transcend themselves through creative projects in the world; women have been confined to immanence, the repetitive maintenance of life.

Beauvoir uses two existentialist terms:

- **Transcendence**: going beyond oneself, through free projects that shape the future: creating, inventing, building, exploring, fighting, making history. For existentialists, this is what makes us human.
- **Immanence**: staying within oneself and the given; repetitive activity that merely maintains life, without creating anything new.

Throughout history, Beauvoir argues, men have claimed transcendence: they hunted, fought, built, explored, ruled, wrote, created art and science. Women were confined to immanence: cooking, cleaning, child-rearing, maintaining the household, tasks that are necessary but endlessly repeated, leaving nothing behind. The meal is cooked and eaten; tomorrow it must be cooked again. The house is cleaned; tomorrow it's dirty again.

Think of the typical day of a homemaker in an Indian joint family: up before everyone to make tea, cook breakfast, pack tiffins, clean, wash, cook lunch, serve, clean, cook dinner, serve, clean. Every day the same. Much of her work is invisible; it's called "not working". Studies by organisations like the **OECD** and India's own **Time Use Survey** (2019) show that Indian women spend several times as many hours on unpaid domestic and care work as men.

Beauvoir's point isn't that domestic work is worthless; it's essential. The problem is that women were **confined** to it, denied access to transcendence, and then told this was their nature.

**Why it matters:** This analysis helps explain why women's work has been undervalued, why women have been underrepresented in science, politics, art and business, and why sharing domestic work equally is a feminist demand. If women are to have transcendence, men must share immanence.

**A common misunderstanding:** Some readers think Beauvoir looked down on mothers or homemakers. She was certainly critical of motherhood as women's **only** destiny, and some of her descriptions are harsh. But her target was the **situation** that forced women into these roles without choice, not the women themselves.

### Idea 4: The myths of woman

**The idea in one line:** Men have created myths of "the eternal feminine" that define women as mother, virgin, temptress or goddess, but never as an ordinary free human being.

In a long section of *The Second Sex*, Beauvoir examines the **myths** about women in religion, literature and popular culture. Woman appears as:

- **The mother**: life-giver, nurturing, self-sacrificing, but also terrifying, linked to birth and death.
- **The virgin**: pure, innocent, untouched.
- **The temptress**: dangerous, seductive, leading men astray (Eve, Pandora).
- **The goddess or muse**: an ideal to be worshipped, inspiring men's art.
- **The mystery**: unknowable, irrational, "the eternal feminine".

What do these myths have in common? Woman is always defined from **man's** point of view: what she means to him. She's never simply a person with her own projects. And the myths are contradictory: she's angel and devil, pure and corrupt, nurturing and destructive. That's because, Beauvoir says, she's a screen onto which men project their hopes and fears.

Beauvoir wrote: "Representation of the world, like the world itself, is the work of men; they describe it from their own point of view, which they confuse with the absolute truth."

Think of Indian myths and their uses. **Sita**, the ideal wife, who follows her husband into exile and undergoes the fire ordeal to prove her purity. **Savitri**, who wins back her husband from death through devotion. **Draupadi**, wagered in a game of dice. The **devi**, worshipped as Shakti, the mother goddess, while living women are often treated badly. The "**sanskari**" woman of TV serials, and the "**vamp**" of old Bollywood. These figures carry deep meaning, and many women find strength in them. But Beauvoir would ask: who has used these images, and for what? How often has the ideal of Sita been used to demand that real women endure suffering silently? How can a society worship goddesses and fail to protect its daughters?

**Why it matters:** This analysis influenced feminist criticism of literature, film, advertising and religion. When you notice that a film's heroine exists only to be rescued, that advertisements show women only as mothers or sex objects, that a woman politician is judged on her clothes or her family, you're using Beauvoir's lens.

**A common misunderstanding:** Beauvoir didn't say that all myths are worthless or that women can't find meaning in them. She said that myths become harmful when they are used to define and confine real women, denying them their freedom.

### Idea 5: Complicity, why women accept being the Other

**The idea in one line:** Women have sometimes accepted their status as the Other, because it offers protection and comfort, and because they lack the means to resist.

This is one of Beauvoir's most uncomfortable and honest ideas. She asks: why haven't women, who are half of humanity, overthrown their subordination?

Part of the answer is that women have lacked **solidarity**. Unlike workers in a factory or a colonised people living together, women are dispersed among men: each woman lives with her father, husband, sons, sharing their class, caste, race and religion. A rich woman identifies more with rich men than with poor women. A woman of a dominant caste may identify with men of her caste rather than with Dalit women.

But Beauvoir adds another, harder answer: **complicity**. Being the Other has certain advantages. A woman who accepts her role may be protected, provided for, spared the anxiety of making her own way, the risks of freedom. To refuse to be the Other, Beauvoir writes, would mean for women to renounce all the advantages conferred on them by their alliance with the superior caste.

This connects to existentialism's idea of **bad faith**. Freedom is frightening. Accepting a role that someone else defines for you can feel safer.

Think of a mother-in-law who was oppressed as a young bride, and then oppresses her own daughter-in-law, enforcing the same rules. Or women who strongly oppose feminism because they've built their lives within the traditional system, and changing it would threaten their security and identity. Beauvoir would see complicity at work, and also the pressure of a system that offers no easy way out.

**Why it matters:** This idea helps explain why oppression is so durable: it isn't only imposed by force, but also accepted, internalised and passed on by the oppressed themselves. It's relevant to all forms of oppression: caste, class, colonialism.

**A common misunderstanding:** Beauvoir wasn't blaming women for their oppression. She described complicity as an understandable response to a situation designed to make freedom costly. Her aim was to help women see their situation clearly, so they could choose freedom.

### Idea 6: The ethics of ambiguity

**The idea in one line:** Human beings are ambiguous, both free and limited, both subject and object; ethics means accepting this ambiguity and willing freedom for oneself and others.

Before *The Second Sex*, Beauvoir wrote ***The Ethics of Ambiguity*** (1947). Its key idea is that human existence is fundamentally **ambiguous**: we are free consciousness, but also bodies; we are subjects, but others see us as objects; we're individuals, but we live among others; we're mortal, yet we project ourselves into the future.

She wrote: "Let us try to assume our fundamental ambiguity." Many people, she says, try to **escape** ambiguity, by pretending to be pure minds or mere things, by losing themselves in a cause or an institution, by refusing to take responsibility.

She described a series of "types" who flee freedom:

- The **sub-man**, who refuses to engage with the world, living in apathy.
- The **serious man**, who sacrifices his freedom to some absolute value, a cause, an institution, a party, a nation, and will sacrifice others to it too. (Think of a bureaucrat who follows rules without question, or a fanatic who kills for an idea.)
- The **nihilist**, who decides that since nothing has absolute value, nothing matters.
- The **adventurer**, who loves action for its own sake, indifferent to its effects on others.
- The **passionate man**, who devotes himself to one obsession.

And then the key ethical point:

"To will oneself free is also to will others free."

Why? Because my freedom only has meaning in a world with others. My projects need others to recognise them, take them up, carry them on. If I oppress others, I live in a world of objects, not free beings, and my own freedom becomes empty. So genuine freedom requires working for the freedom of others: fighting oppression wherever it exists.

**Why it matters:** This is Beauvoir's answer to the critics who said existentialism leads to selfishness or "anything goes". It's an ethics of solidarity, rooted in freedom. And it gives a philosophical basis for her feminism: oppression of women is wrong because it denies their freedom, and in doing so diminishes everyone.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Ambiguity" doesn't mean vagueness or indecision. It means that human life contains real tensions that cannot be resolved once and for all, and that ethics must work with them, not deny them.

### Idea 7: The independent woman

**The idea in one line:** Liberation requires economic independence, but also freedom from the internalised sense of being the Other.

In the last part of *The Second Sex*, Beauvoir describes the "**independent woman**", the woman who works and earns her own living. Economic independence, she says, is essential: a woman who depends on a man for money can never be fully free.

But it's not enough. The independent woman still lives in a world shaped by men. She is still expected to be feminine, attractive, a good wife and mother, on top of her work. She feels torn between career and "womanhood". She lacks the self-confidence that men are given from childhood. She is judged by different standards.

Think of the working woman in India today: expected to excel at her job and also do most of the cooking, childcare and caring for elderly in-laws; to look presentable; to not seem "too ambitious"; to answer the question, "Who looks after your children?" that is never asked of her male colleagues. That's what Beauvoir called the conflict of the independent woman. Today, people call it the "**double burden**" or "**second shift**".

Beauvoir's vision of liberation was ultimately one of **reciprocity**: a world in which men and women recognise each other as equals, as free subjects, each sometimes the Other to the other, but neither permanently defined as secondary. She ended the book with the hope that men and women could "unequivocally affirm their brotherhood".

**Why it matters:** Beauvoir's analysis of the independent woman anticipated debates that are still going on: work-life balance, the gender pay gap, women's leadership, shared parenting, maternity and paternity leave. India's female labour force participation rate has been among the lowest in the world for years. Beauvoir would say this is not just about jobs, but about the whole situation women face.

**A common misunderstanding:** Beauvoir wasn't saying that all women must have careers, or that motherhood is bad. She wanted women to have real choices, and the conditions to make them freely.

### Idea 8: Old age, the other Other

**The idea in one line:** Modern society treats old people as Others too, discarding them once they are no longer productive.

Later in life, in ***The Coming of Age*** (1970), Beauvoir turned her analysis to **old age**. She argued that society treats the old as a kind of Other: useless, a burden, a source of embarrassment. People think of old age as something that happens to **other** people; we don't want to see our future selves in the old.

She showed that how old people are treated depends on society: in some, the old are respected; in modern capitalist societies, which value people mainly as producers and consumers, the old are often discarded once they stop working. She called the treatment of the elderly a scandal of society.

Think of India, where the tradition of respecting elders and the joint family is breaking down. Many elderly people are left alone as children migrate. Some are abandoned at pilgrimage sites or in old-age homes. The widows of **Vrindavan**, many of them abandoned by their families, became a symbol of this. India passed the **Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act** in **2007** to make children legally responsible for their aged parents, a sign that the old protections were failing.

**Why it matters:** As populations age around the world, Beauvoir's question becomes urgent: how do we treat people who are no longer "useful"? Her answer was that a society reveals its true values in how it treats the old, and that a society which discards them is admitting that it valued people only as tools.

**A common misunderstanding:** Beauvoir didn't romanticise old age. She described its losses frankly. Her point was that much of its misery is socially produced, and could be changed.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Hegel: the Other

**Hegel**'s analysis of how self-consciousness needs an Other, and of the struggle between master and slave, was the starting point for Beauvoir's idea of woman as the Other. But she showed that in the case of men and women, the dialectic got stuck: woman was never allowed to win recognition as an equal.

### Kierkegaard: ambiguity

From **Kierkegaard**, Beauvoir took the sense of human existence as anxious, ambiguous and risky, and the idea that ethics must face the individual's concrete situation rather than hide in universal rules.

### Sartre: partner and critic

**Sartre** and Beauvoir shared the core of existentialism: existence precedes essence, freedom, responsibility, bad faith. "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" is existentialism applied to gender.

But Beauvoir also **corrected** Sartre. In his early work, he claimed that freedom was absolute in any situation. Beauvoir argued, in conversation and in her writing, that **situations** differ hugely: a woman confined to a harem does not have the same freedom as a man. Freedom must be understood in context: the body, history, society, oppression. She later said that on this point she had argued against Sartre and that he had come to accept her view.

For decades, people saw Beauvoir as Sartre's follower. Since the 1980s, scholars have shown that she was an original philosopher, and that some ideas usually credited to Sartre may have come partly from her. She herself always insisted that he was the philosopher and she the writer, which some think was a kind of modesty, and others a strategic choice.

Imagine them talking:

*Sartre:* We are always free. Even in chains, a man chooses how to respond.

*Beauvoir:* A woman shut in a harem, who has never been taught to read, who has never imagined another life, what freedom does she have?

*Sartre:* She is free, in principle...

*Beauvoir:* "In principle" means nothing to her. Freedom needs a situation in which it can be exercised. That's why we must change situations, not just preach freedom.

### Mill: the inheritance

**John Stuart Mill**'s *The Subjection of Women* (1869) had argued that women's "nature" is "an eminently artificial thing", produced by oppression. Beauvoir carried this insight much further: from law and politics into childhood, sexuality, myth, literature, the body and daily experience.

### Marx and Engels: class isn't everything

Beauvoir took Marxism seriously. She agreed with **Engels** (Marx's partner, who wrote *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State*) that women's oppression is linked to private property and economic dependence. But in *The Second Sex*, she argued that historical materialism alone cannot explain why it was **women** who were subordinated. You also need to understand how human consciousness sets up the Other, and how women's bodies were given meaning. Socialism alone, she thought (and later confirmed by watching the Soviet Union), would not automatically liberate women.

### Aristotle and the philosophers

Beauvoir quoted **Aristotle**, **Aquinas** and others to show how philosophy itself had defined woman as defective, a lesser man. Part of her project was to expose the male bias hidden in the "universal" claims of the great philosophers.

### Later voices

- **Betty Friedan**'s *The Feminine Mystique* (1963) brought ideas similar to Beauvoir's to American housewives and helped launch American second-wave feminism.
- **Kate Millett**, **Shulamith Firestone**, and other radical feminists of the 1970s built on and argued with Beauvoir.
- **Judith Butler** (born 1956) developed Beauvoir's idea that gender is "become" into the theory that gender is "**performative**": produced through repeated acts.
- **bell hooks** and other Black feminists criticised mainstream feminism, including some of Beauvoir's analysis, for centring the experience of white, middle-class women.
- Indian feminists like **Kamla Bhasin**, **Nivedita Menon** and **Uma Chakravarti** (who analysed "Brahmanical patriarchy", the intertwining of caste and gender) both drew on and went beyond Western feminism, insisting on the specific ways caste, class and religion shape women's lives in India.

## See it around you

**"Sharma ji ki beti", "Raju ki mummy".** Notice how often women are identified through men. Notice how surprised people are when a woman keeps her own surname after marriage.

**Toys, colours and careers.** Pink for girls, blue for boys; dolls and kitchen sets versus cars and science kits. Notice the toy aisles, school "career days", the subjects girls and boys are steered toward. That's "becoming" a woman or man.

**The double burden.** Working women who come home to a "second shift" of cooking, cleaning and caring. Ask who makes the tea in your house. Who remembers the children's school events? Who takes leave when someone is ill?

**Myths in media.** TV serials with the self-sacrificing ideal bahu and the scheming vamp; advertisements in which women are always mothers, cooks or decorations; film heroines whose role is to be rescued. Beauvoir's analysis of myths applies directly.

**Moral policing.** Rules about what women can wear, when they can go out, whom they can meet, all justified as "protection". Beauvoir would ask: protection from whom, and at the cost of what freedom?

**Women's safety.** The protests after the **2012 Delhi gang rape** (the "Nirbhaya" case), and the legal reforms that followed (the Criminal Law Amendment Act, 2013), showed how deeply the question of women's freedom in public space matters. Beauvoir would note how often the response is to restrict women, rather than to change men.

**Law and equality.** Landmark cases like ***Mary Roy v. State of Kerala*** (1986), which gave Syrian Christian women equal inheritance rights; the **2005 amendment** to the Hindu Succession Act, giving daughters equal rights in ancestral property; ***Joseph Shine*** (2018), which struck down the adultery law partly because it treated a wife as her husband's property. Each is a step away from woman as the Other.

**Old age.** Elderly parents left alone in cities or villages, old-age homes, the widows of Vrindavan: Beauvoir's "other Other".

## Use it

**1. Notice the default.** In your conversations, books, films and workplace, ask: who is the default human here? Whose point of view is treated as neutral? Who is the "special case"? Once you see it, you can't unsee it.

**2. Question "natural".** When you hear "women are naturally...", "men are naturally...", ask: how do we know? Has anyone ever been raised free of these expectations? What would they be like?

**3. Share the immanence.** If you're a man, take on an equal share of cooking, cleaning, childcare and care for elders, not as "helping", but as your own responsibility. If you're a woman, notice whether you're carrying an unequal load and talk about it.

**4. Raise children differently.** If you're a parent, teacher, aunt or uncle, give children the same freedoms, toys, challenges and encouragement regardless of sex. Let boys cry and girls climb trees.

**5. Check your complicity.** Ask honestly where you accept being defined by others because it's comfortable, or where you enforce on others the rules that once confined you. Breaking the chain begins with one person.

**6. Will others' freedom.** Beauvoir said that to will yourself free is to will others free. Ask how your freedom depends on others' unfreedom: the domestic worker, the delivery person, the woman in your family who gave up her career. What could you do to widen their freedom too?

**7. Read the myths critically.** When you watch a film or read a story, ask: does the woman have her own projects, or does she exist for the hero? Is she a person or a symbol? Enjoy stories, but don't let them define real people.

**8. Respect the old.** Visit, listen to and involve elderly people. Ask what they want, not just what they need. Remember that you're looking at your own future.

## Where she went wrong

**1. A white, middle-class perspective.** *The Second Sex* mainly describes the experience of educated, middle-class, Western women. Black feminists, working-class women and women from the Global South have pointed out that their lives involve oppressions of race, class, caste and colonialism that Beauvoir's analysis doesn't fully capture. For an Indian Dalit woman, gender oppression is tangled with caste in ways Beauvoir never considered. This is why later thinkers developed **intersectionality**, the analysis of how different oppressions overlap.

**2. Negativity about the female body and motherhood.** Some of Beauvoir's descriptions of pregnancy, menstruation and motherhood are very negative, describing the female body as a burden and motherhood as a form of bondage. Later feminists argued that this accepted a male view of the body, and failed to value the experiences that many women find meaningful.

**3. Too much faith in the male model.** Beauvoir's idea of "transcendence", through projects, work and creation in the public world, seems to accept the traditional male ideal of a good life. Critics ask: why should women aspire to be like men? Shouldn't care, relationships and maintenance of life be valued in themselves?

**4. Universalism.** Beauvoir sometimes treats "woman" as a single category across history and cultures, while her own theory says womanhood is made differently in different situations. Anthropologists and historians have shown that gender roles vary enormously across societies.

**5. Her personal relationships.** Her relationships with young female students, some of whom she introduced to Sartre, and the deceptions involved in her "contingent" loves, have been criticised as exploitative, especially given her position as a teacher. Some of these women later wrote about being hurt. A philosopher of freedom and reciprocity did not always practise them.

**6. Minimising her own originality.** By insisting that Sartre was the philosopher and she was not, Beauvoir contributed to decades of underestimating her philosophical work, and arguably to the very pattern of women's work being treated as secondary that she criticised.

**7. Translation problems.** For English readers, the 1953 translation, abridged and full of errors, distorted her philosophy for over fifty years. This isn't her fault, but it shaped how she was understood.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought of the differences between men and women as mostly natural: women caring, men ambitious; women at home, men in the world. You may have taken it for granted that "man" means human, and that a woman's identity naturally comes through her father, husband or children. You may have thought feminism was about hating men, or that it was a Western idea with little to say to you.

**Now,** you can see how a girl is made into a "woman", and a boy into a "man", through thousands of small pressures, toys, words, rules and myths. You can see the structure of the Subject and the Other, in gender and also in caste, race and age, and how the Other sometimes accepts her role because freedom is frightening and costly. You can see that liberation requires not only rights and jobs, but shared housework, new stories and a new sense of self. And you can see that to will yourself free is to will others free.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A toy aisle** becomes a factory of genders.
- **"Be careful, you're a girl"** and **"boys don't cry"** become lessons you can choose not to teach.
- **Who makes the tea** in your house becomes a philosophical question.
- **A woman known as someone's wife or mother** makes you wonder what her own name and dreams are.
- **A film heroine waiting to be rescued** looks like a myth, not a mirror.
- **A rule "for women's protection"** makes you ask whose freedom is being limited.
- **An old person sitting alone** reminds you that every society has its Others, and that one day you may be one.

Simone de Beauvoir was raised to be a dutiful daughter. She chose instead to become herself, and then spent her life showing the world how women are made into the second sex, and how they might become free. Her most famous sentence is also an invitation: if we become who we are, then we can become otherwise.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986), French existentialist philosopher, novelist, memoirist and feminist; Sartre's partner for fifty-one years.
- **Context:** a bourgeois Catholic family that lost its fortune; a France where women couldn't vote until 1944; the Nazi occupation; the existentialist movement.
- **Life:** youngest person to pass the philosophy agrégation (1929); teacher; *She Came to Stay*; *The Ethics of Ambiguity*; *The Second Sex* (1949); Prix Goncourt for *The Mandarins*; memoirs; the Manifesto of the 343.
- **Woman as the Other:** man is the Subject and the norm; woman is defined in relation to him.
- **"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman":** womanhood is made by society, not by nature alone; the root of the sex/gender distinction.
- **Immanence and transcendence:** women confined to repetitive maintenance; men allowed creative projects.
- **Myths of woman:** the eternal feminine as man's projection.
- **Complicity:** women sometimes accept being the Other because freedom is costly.
- **Ethics of ambiguity:** accept our ambiguous condition; "to will oneself free is also to will others free".
- **Independent woman and old age:** economic independence is necessary but not enough; the old are society's other Others.
- **Criticisms:** a white, middle-class perspective, negativity about the body and motherhood, acceptance of a male model of transcendence, and the exploitation in her personal relationships.

**Key terms**

- **The Other:** a being defined in relation to a dominant Subject, as inessential and secondary.
- **Subject:** a free consciousness who defines itself and acts in the world.
- **Becoming a woman:** the social process by which females are shaped into "women".
- **Sex and gender:** biological differences / the social meanings and roles attached to them (terms later feminists developed from her work).
- **Immanence / transcendence:** repetitive maintenance of life / free projects that create and go beyond the given.
- **The eternal feminine:** the myth of an unchanging female essence.
- **Complicity:** the oppressed's acceptance of their own subordination.
- **Ambiguity:** the human condition of being both free and limited, subject and object.
- **Situation:** the concrete conditions (body, history, society) in which freedom is exercised.
- **Second wave feminism:** the women's movement of the 1960s–80s, deeply influenced by *The Second Sex*.
