## Life

Simone de Beauvoir was born in Paris in 1908 into a bourgeois Catholic family. Her father, a lawyer with literary tastes, lost much of his money after the First World War, and the family's decline meant Simone and her sister would have to work rather than rely on dowries. A devout child, she lost her faith at fourteen and resolved to become a writer. She studied philosophy at the Sorbonne. In 1929, aged twenty-one, she came second in the national *agrégation* examination, the youngest person ever to pass it, just behind **Jean-Paul Sartre**, who was taking it for the second time.

The two began a partnership that lasted until Sartre's death fifty-one years later. They never married, lived apart, and openly had other relationships, an arrangement that was both influential and, as her letters and memoirs reveal, often painful. Beauvoir taught philosophy in lycées in Marseille, Rouen and Paris until 1943, when she was dismissed following a complaint by a student's mother. That year she published her first novel, *She Came to Stay*, which explores the problem of other minds through a destructive love triangle.

After the war she helped found *Les Temps modernes* and was, with Sartre, at the centre of the existentialist movement. She developed an existentialist ethics in *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947). After travelling in the United States, where she began a long love affair with the novelist Nelson Algren, she published *The Second Sex* (1949). The book caused a scandal, sold tens of thousands of copies in its first week, and was placed on the Vatican's Index of Prohibited Books. Her novel *The Mandarins*, a portrait of post-war intellectuals, won the Prix Goncourt in 1954.

In the 1970s Beauvoir became an active feminist campaigner. She wrote the "Manifesto of the 343" (1971), in which prominent women declared that they had had illegal abortions, helping to bring about the legalisation of abortion in France in 1975. She also wrote groundbreaking studies of old age and death, and a multi-volume autobiography. She died in Paris in 1986 and is buried beside Sartre in Montparnasse Cemetery.

## Key ideas

**Situated freedom.** Beauvoir accepted the existentialist view that human beings have no fixed essence and must create themselves through free choices. But she stressed, more than Sartre did at first, that freedom is always **situated**: limited and shaped by the body, history, economic circumstance and the choices of others. Oppression can make it nearly impossible to exercise.

**The ethics of ambiguity.** Human existence is ambiguous: we are both free subjects and objects in the world, both individuals and members of a community. Ethics cannot escape this ambiguity, only face it honestly. Because my freedom can only be fulfilled through others' freedom, "to will oneself free is also to will others free."

**Woman as the Other.** *The Second Sex* asks, "What is a woman?" Beauvoir argued that throughout history men have defined themselves as the norm, the Subject, and women in relation to men, as the **Other**: "He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other." Women have been confined to **immanence**, the repetitive maintenance of life, while men claim **transcendence**, the projects that create the future.

**"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."** Femininity is not a natural destiny determined by biology, psychology or economics alone. It is a situation produced by upbringing, myth, law and custom, and women are raised to accept it. Beauvoir traced this process through childhood, marriage, motherhood and old age, and examined the myths of woman in literature, religion and history.

**Liberation.** Women's liberation requires economic independence, access to contraception and abortion, and the freedom to take up their own projects, and it would free men as well.

## Influence and legacy

*The Second Sex* is one of the founding texts of second-wave feminism. Feminists such as Betty Friedan, Kate Millett and Shulamith Firestone acknowledged their debt to it, and its distinction between biological sex and socially constructed womanhood paved the way for later work on gender. Philosophers have increasingly recognised Beauvoir as an original thinker in her own right, not merely Sartre's companion. Her ideas about embodiment, oppression and ethics anticipated important themes in Sartre's later work, and her studies of ageing remain pioneering. Her novels and memoirs are among the great records of twentieth-century intellectual life.
