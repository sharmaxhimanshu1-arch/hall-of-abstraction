## Life

Epictetus was born around 50 CE in Hierapolis, in Phrygia (in modern Turkey), as a slave. His name simply means "acquired". He was brought to Rome and owned by Epaphroditus, a wealthy freedman who served as secretary to the emperor Nero. Epictetus was lame. One ancient story says his master twisted his leg until it broke while Epictetus calmly warned him it would, though other sources attribute the lameness to illness.

Remarkably, Epaphroditus allowed him to study with **Musonius Rufus**, the leading Stoic teacher in Rome. At some point after Nero's death in 68 Epictetus was freed, and he began teaching philosophy himself. Around 93 CE the emperor Domitian expelled all philosophers from Rome. Epictetus moved to **Nicopolis** in north-western Greece and opened a school that attracted students from across the empire, including young Romans of the governing class. The emperor Hadrian is said to have visited him. He lived simply and alone for most of his life. Late in life, according to the commentator Simplicius, he took in a child who would otherwise have been abandoned. He died around 135 CE.

## Key ideas

Like Socrates, whom he revered, Epictetus wrote nothing. His student **Arrian**, later a notable historian and governor, took down his lectures. Four books of the *Discourses* survive, along with the *Enchiridion*, a short handbook of key teachings.

**The dichotomy of control.** The *Enchiridion* opens with the principle at the heart of his thought: "Some things are in our control and others not." Our opinions, desires, aversions and choices, in a word our *prohairesis* or faculty of choice, are up to us. Our bodies, property, reputation and positions are not. Peace and freedom come from investing our concern only in the former.

**Judgements, not events.** "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things." Death is not terrible; the judgement that death is terrible is what terrifies us. So we must learn to examine each impression before assenting to it, asking, "Is this within my control?"

**True freedom.** The free person is not the one with power or wealth but the one who cannot be compelled, because they want nothing that others control. A slave can be free, and an emperor a slave to his fears and desires. This message from a former slave carried special force.

**Roles and duties.** We each play roles, as child, parent, citizen, friend, assigned by circumstance. Our task is to play them well: "For this is your business, to act well the character assigned you; to choose it is another's."

**Philosophy as training.** Epictetus was impatient with students who could quote Chrysippus but not control their temper. Philosophy is shown in how one lives.

## Influence and legacy

Epictetus deeply shaped **Marcus Aurelius**, who thanked his teacher Rusticus for lending him the *Discourses*. Christian monks adapted the *Enchiridion* as a rule of life. Pascal, Montaigne and Adam Smith read him closely. In the twentieth century the psychologist Albert Ellis credited him as an inspiration for rational emotive behaviour therapy, a forerunner of cognitive behavioural therapy. US Navy pilot James Stockdale said Epictetus' teachings helped him survive seven years as a prisoner of war in Vietnam.
