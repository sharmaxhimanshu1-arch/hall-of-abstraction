## Start here

India is playing a World Cup final. Two friends are watching in the same room. India loses in the last over.

The first friend is destroyed. He shouts at the screen, throws the remote, refuses to eat dinner, spends the night arguing angrily on social media, and sleeps badly. The next day at work he is still irritable.

The second friend sighs, says, "What a match, though," texts his father about a brilliant catch, eats dinner and sleeps well.

Here is my question. What caused the first friend's misery?

You will probably say: India lost the match. But wait. The *same* event happened to the second friend, and he was fine. So the match alone cannot be the cause. Something else was different. What?

The difference was inside them. The first friend believed something like: "India *must* win, a loss is a disaster, this is unbearable." The second believed: "I hoped we would win, we didn't, that's sport." Same event. Different **judgement**. Completely different experience.

Nearly two thousand years ago, a lame, elderly teacher in a small Greek town put this insight in one sentence that has changed millions of lives:

"Men are disturbed not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things."

His name was **Epictetus**. He was born a slave. He owned almost nothing. He could not walk properly. He had been exiled from Rome by an emperor. And yet he became perhaps the most powerful teacher of **freedom** the ancient world produced. Not political freedom, which he never had for much of his life, but a deeper freedom that no master, no emperor and no misfortune could take away.

His teaching has been carried by a Roman emperor in his tent on the battlefield, by Christian monks in their cells, by philosophers in the Enlightenment, and in the twentieth century by an American fighter pilot who spent seven and a half years as a prisoner of war and said Epictetus helped keep him alive. It became one of the roots of modern cognitive therapy.

By the end of this page, you will understand his central idea so deeply that you will start to apply it automatically: to traffic jams, exam results, rude relatives, office politics and the fears that keep you awake at night. And you may discover that much of what you thought was "happening to you" was, all along, something you were doing to yourself.

## The world he was born into

### An empire built on slavery

Epictetus was born around 50 CE in **Hierapolis**, a city in Phrygia, in what is now south-western Turkey. At that time the whole Mediterranean world was ruled by Rome.

To understand Epictetus, you have to understand what it meant to be a slave in the Roman Empire. Slavery was everywhere. In Italy, a large share of the population was enslaved, perhaps one in five or more by some estimates. People became slaves through war, piracy, debt, or simply by being born to an enslaved mother. A slave was legally property. They could be bought, sold, beaten, separated from their families, or killed with little consequence for the owner.

But Roman slavery was also complicated. Some slaves worked in mines and fields under brutal conditions and died young. Others, especially educated ones, worked as secretaries, accountants, teachers and doctors in rich households, and could rise to considerable influence. Slaves could be freed, and freed slaves (called **freedmen**) could become rich and powerful, especially in the emperor's household. Even so, they carried the stigma of their origins all their lives.

Notice the paradox. A freedman in the emperor's palace might control huge sums of money and command senators to wait for him, while still being looked down upon as a former slave. And a senator, technically free, might live in daily terror of the emperor's mood. Who, then, was truly free?

That is the question at the heart of Epictetus' philosophy.

### Stoicism in Rome

By Epictetus' time, the Greek philosophy of **Stoicism** had become the most influential way of thinking among educated Romans. (You can read about its origins on the page about Seneca.) Its central teachings were that virtue is the only true good, that external things like wealth and status are "indifferent", and that a wise person lives in harmony with nature and reason.

The leading Stoic teacher in Rome in the mid-first century was **Musonius Rufus**, sometimes called the "Roman Socrates". Musonius was unusual. He argued that women should study philosophy just as men should, that husbands owe their wives the same faithfulness that wives owe husbands, and that philosophy must be practised through hard training of body and mind, not just discussed. He was exiled several times by emperors who found him inconvenient.

This was the man who would become Epictetus' teacher. And the fact that a slave could study with him at all tells us something remarkable about Epictetus' master.

## The life, as a story

### A slave named "acquired"

We do not know Epictetus' real name. "Epictetus" (*epiktetos* in Greek) simply means "acquired" or "gained": the kind of name you might give to something you bought. Think about that. His very name marked him as property.

He was brought to Rome and became the slave of a man named **Epaphroditus**. Epaphroditus was himself a former slave who had risen to become a powerful secretary in the household of the emperor **Nero**. He was an important figure: in 68 CE, when Nero, facing rebellion, decided to kill himself, it was Epaphroditus who helped him drive the dagger in. Years later, the emperor Domitian had Epaphroditus executed, possibly for that very act.

So the young Epictetus grew up close to the centre of imperial power, where he could see, from the bottom, how the mighty lived: their wealth, their flattery, their fear.

### The broken leg

Epictetus was lame for most of his life. There is a famous story, told by an ancient writer named Celsus and reported later by the Christian scholar Origen, about how this happened. Epaphroditus, the story goes, was twisting Epictetus' leg for some reason. Epictetus calmly said, "You will break it." His master kept twisting, and the leg broke. Epictetus said, with no change in his voice: "Did I not tell you that you would break it?"

Is the story true? We do not know. Another ancient source says his lameness came from rheumatism or illness. But the story survived because it captured something people saw in him: a man whose inner calm could not be shaken by pain inflicted from outside.

### A slave who studied philosophy

Here is the surprising part. Epaphroditus allowed, perhaps even encouraged, his slave to attend the lectures of **Musonius Rufus**. Rich Romans sometimes gave talented slaves an education, partly because an educated slave was more valuable. Whatever the reason, Epictetus became a devoted Stoic.

He later recalled Musonius' style of teaching: so searching and personal that each student felt someone had told Musonius about his own particular faults. Musonius did not entertain his students. He made them uncomfortable. Epictetus would later teach the same way.

### Freedom, and exile

At some point after Nero's death in 68 CE, Epictetus was freed. We do not know exactly when or how. He began to teach philosophy in Rome.

Then, around 93 CE, the emperor **Domitian**, suspicious of philosophers who encouraged independent thinking, expelled them all from Rome. Epictetus left and settled in **Nicopolis**, a city on the north-western coast of Greece, founded by Augustus to celebrate his victory at the battle of Actium. There he opened his own school.

The school became famous. Young men came from across the empire to study with him, many of them from wealthy Roman families preparing for careers in government. The emperor **Hadrian** is reported to have been friendly with him. Epictetus spent the rest of his long life in Nicopolis, teaching.

### A simple life

Epictetus lived with almost nothing. Ancient sources say he had only a straw mat, a simple pallet and an iron lamp. One day his lamp was stolen. He replaced it with a cheap clay one. In the *Discourses* he reflects on this: the thief became a thief for the sake of a lamp. He lost his character to gain a lamp. Who, then, lost more?

(After Epictetus died, according to the satirist Lucian, an admirer bought his clay lamp for three thousand drachmas, hoping that by reading by it he would become wise. Lucian found this ridiculous. Epictetus would have too.)

He seems to have lived alone for most of his life. Late in life, according to the later commentator Simplicius, he took in a child whose parents were going to abandon it, a common practice then, and brought in a woman to help him raise it. A former slave, rescuing a child from the fate that so many unwanted children faced. He died around 135 CE, in his eighties.

### The student who wrote it all down

Like Socrates, whom he deeply admired, Epictetus wrote nothing. We know his teaching because one of his students, a young Greek named **Arrian**, took detailed notes of his lectures, apparently close to word for word. Arrian later became a senator, a Roman governor of the province of Cappadocia and a famous historian, whose account of Alexander the Great's campaigns, including the invasion of north-western India, is one of our main sources for that history.

Arrian published two works from his notes:

- The ***Discourses***: originally eight books, of which four survive. These are lively, informal, sometimes funny, sometimes harsh conversations between Epictetus and his students and visitors.
- The ***Enchiridion*** or ***Handbook***: a short summary of key teachings, meant to be kept "at hand", like a dagger or a tool. It is only about fifty short sections and can be read in an hour. It has been one of the most widely read books of practical philosophy ever since.

## The big ideas, one by one

Epictetus is not a complicated philosopher. His power comes from taking a few ideas and pushing them relentlessly into every corner of life. If you remember only one page of this website, it might be this one, because his central idea is so practical.

### Idea 1: The dichotomy of control

**The idea in one line:** Some things are up to us and some are not, and almost all our suffering comes from confusing the two.

The *Handbook* begins without any introduction. Its very first line is:

"Some things are in our control and others not."

Then Epictetus explains. **In our control** are our opinions, our impulses to act, our desires and aversions: in a word, whatever is our own doing, our own inner choices. **Not in our control** are our body, our property, our reputation, our position, and, in a word, whatever is not our own doing.

Now let us make this concrete. Take an ordinary day.

| Up to you | Not up to you |
|---|---|
| How much you prepare for the exam | The difficulty of the paper |
| Whether you speak honestly in the meeting | Whether your boss likes what you say |
| How you treat your mother-in-law | How she treats you |
| Whether you leave early | The traffic |
| Your effort in the job interview | Whether they hire you |
| Whether you take care of your health | Whether you get ill anyway |
| What you post | How people react to it |

Look carefully at the right-hand column. These are the things most people worry about most. And none of them is fully up to us. We influence some of them, yes. But we do not control them.

Epictetus' argument is simple. If you set your heart on things that are not in your control, you will be **anxious** while you wait, **disappointed** when they go wrong, **envious** of others who get them, and a **slave** to anyone who has power over them. If instead you place your sense of good and bad in what *is* in your control, your own choices, intentions and efforts, then no one can stop you, no one can harm you, and you will be free.

**The Bhagavad Gita parallel.** If you grew up in India, you have probably heard a verse that says almost exactly the same thing. In the Gita (2.47), Krishna tells Arjuna: *karmanye vadhikaraste ma phaleshu kadachana*. You have a right to your action, but never to its fruits. Do not let the fruits of action be your motive, and do not be attached to inaction.

Epictetus never heard of the Gita, and the Gita never heard of Epictetus. Their worlds were very different: the Gita is grounded in devotion, the self and the cosmic order; Epictetus in reason and Stoic physics. But the practical teaching is strikingly close. Do your best. Release your grip on the result. That is where peace lies.

**Why it matters.** This single idea is a complete tool for daily life. Whenever you feel anxious, angry or upset, you can ask: *is this within my control?* If yes, act on it. If no, accept it and turn your energy elsewhere. Most people spend enormous energy on the right-hand column and neglect the left. Epictetus reverses that.

**A common misunderstanding.** The dichotomy of control does **not** mean "don't care about outcomes" or "be passive". The student should still study hard; that is in her control. The doctor should still try with all his skill to save the patient. Epictetus would say: give your full effort to what is yours to do, and then, whatever the result, do not let it destroy your peace or your character. An archer does everything to aim well, but once the arrow leaves the bow, the wind is not his business.

### Idea 2: Judgements, not events, disturb us

**The idea in one line:** Events are neutral; the meaning we attach to them is what makes us suffer.

This is the idea in our opening cricket story. "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things."

Epictetus gives an example that is startling: death. Death is not terrible, he says. If it were, it would have seemed so to Socrates, who met it calmly. What is terrible is our *judgement* that death is terrible. That judgement is what causes our terror.

**An example.** Two employees get the same critical feedback from their manager. One thinks, "I'm useless, I'll be fired, everyone thinks I'm a failure." She spends the weekend miserable. The other thinks, "Okay, that part of my work needs improvement. Let me fix it." She spends the weekend relaxed. Same words, same manager. The difference is the judgement each added on top of the event.

Epictetus teaches a powerful habit: **separate the event from the story you add to it.** The event: "The manager said my report had errors." The story: "I'm useless and my career is over." The event is a fact. The story is a judgement, and judgements can be examined, questioned and changed.

**Why it matters.** This is the foundation of modern **cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)**. In the 1950s, the psychologist Albert Ellis developed a therapy based explicitly on this idea, and he credited Epictetus as an important influence. Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy, which followed, works on the same principle: identify the distorted thought, test it against reality, and replace it with a more accurate one. Millions of people have been helped by therapy built, in part, on this ancient slave's insight.

**A common misunderstanding.** Epictetus does not say that bad things are not bad, or that you should pretend to be happy when you are suffering. He says the *disturbance* in your mind comes from your judgement, and so the remedy is in your hands. Real pain may still be real. But the extra layer of panic, self-pity, rage and despair that we pile on top is something we can learn to reduce.

### Idea 3: Test every impression

**The idea in one line:** Before you accept a thought, feeling or first reaction, stop and examine it.

The Stoics used the word **impression** (*phantasia*) for the way things first appear to us: the thought that pops into your head, the instant feeling when something happens. "He insulted me." "This is a disaster." "I must have that." Impressions come automatically. We cannot stop them arising.

But, said the Stoics, we can choose whether to **assent** to them: whether to accept them as true and act on them. Between the impression and our response, there is a gap. Wisdom lives in that gap.

Epictetus gives his students a little phrase to use. When a harsh impression arises, say to it: "You are but an appearance, and not absolutely the thing you appear to be." Then examine it. Ask: is this within my control or not? If it concerns something not in my control, then it is nothing to me.

**An example.** You see that a friend has read your message but not replied for hours. The impression arrives: "She's ignoring me. She's angry. She doesn't value me." You feel hurt. Now, use Epictetus' gap. "You are just an impression." What do I actually know? She saw the message. That's all. She may be busy, tired, driving, dealing with something at home. The story of rejection is something *I* added. And even if she is upset, her feelings are not in my control. What *is* in my control is how I respond: perhaps by asking kindly if everything is okay, perhaps by waiting.

**Why it matters.** In an age of constant notifications, outrage headlines and emotional manipulation, the ability to pause before reacting may be one of the most important life skills. Advertisers, politicians and propaganda all target our first impressions. Epictetus trains us to put a guard at the gate.

### Idea 4: The self that no one can touch

**The idea in one line:** Your true self is your power of choice, and that is the one thing no one can ever take from you.

Epictetus' central concept is ***prohairesis***, usually translated as "moral choice" or "will" or "volition". It is your capacity to judge, choose, desire and refuse. For Epictetus, this is what *you* really are. Your body is not you; it can be imprisoned, injured, killed. Your property is not you. Your reputation is not you. But your prohairesis, your inner power to decide how you will respond, is yours absolutely.

In the *Discourses*, he imagines a tyrant threatening him:

"I will fetter you." What did you say? Fetter me? You will fetter my leg, but my will not even Zeus himself can overpower.

"I will throw you into prison." My body, rather.

"I will behead you." Did I ever tell you that I alone had a neck that cannot be cut?

There is humour in this, but also steel. It is the voice of someone who has been a slave, who knows exactly what it is like to have no control over one's body, and who has found something underneath that no master can reach.

**An example.** Think of Nelson Mandela, imprisoned for twenty-seven years. His jailers controlled every external thing: his food, his cell, his work, his contact with family. They could not control his dignity, his refusal to hate, his decision about who he would be. Mandela was not a Stoic, but prisoners across history have discovered what Epictetus taught.

The American fighter pilot **James Stockdale** is the most famous example. Shot down over North Vietnam in 1965, he spent seven and a half years as a prisoner of war, much of it in solitary confinement, and was tortured repeatedly. He had studied Epictetus at university. As he parachuted down, he later said, he thought: I am leaving the world of technology and entering the world of Epictetus. He later credited Stoic teaching with helping him survive, and with helping him lead other prisoners to resist.

**Why it matters.** This gives every person, however powerless they appear, a core of dignity. A worker humiliated by a boss, a student bullied at school, a person facing illness: the external situation may be beyond their control. But their response, their integrity, their refusal to let the situation define who they are, remains theirs.

**A common misunderstanding.** This does not mean Epictetus thought injustice was fine because "you can still be free inside". He did not campaign against slavery, which is a real limitation we will discuss. But his point was not that oppression is acceptable. It was that oppression cannot reach the deepest part of a person unless they hand it over.

### Idea 5: Who is truly free?

**The idea in one line:** The free person is not the one with power or wealth, but the one who wants nothing that others can withhold.

Epictetus devotes a long discourse to freedom. He defines the free person as one who lives as he wishes, who cannot be compelled, hindered or forced. Then he asks: by this definition, who is free?

Is the senator free? He must flatter the emperor, fear the informers, chase honours, wait in the antechamber of powerful freedmen. He is a slave with a fancier title. Is the rich man free? He is tormented by fear of losing his money, and by desire for more. Is the man in love with a beautiful woman free? He will do anything she asks, bear any humiliation. Is the emperor himself free? He is surrounded by plots, terrified of assassination.

The only free person, Epictetus concludes, is one who has trained himself to desire only what is in his own control. Such a person cannot be blackmailed, bribed or frightened into acting against his principles, because there is nothing external he needs so badly that he would betray himself for it.

**An example.** Think about the pressures in a typical career. You need the promotion, so you stay silent when your boss asks you to cut corners. You need your colleagues' approval, so you laugh at jokes you find cruel. You need the bonus, so you work weekends you would rather spend with your children. Each "need" is a chain. Epictetus asks: what if you needed less? What if your sense of worth did not depend on these things? You would not stop working, but you would be far harder to manipulate.

### Idea 6: Roles and duties

**The idea in one line:** You know your duties by looking at your relationships: as a child, a parent, a sibling, a citizen, a friend.

"Duties are universally measured by relations," Epictetus says. If someone is your father, your duty, he says, is to take care of him, to submit to him in all things, and to receive his reproaches and correction patiently. "But he is a bad father." Is it your concern whether he is good or bad? Your duty is to be a good son.

He also describes life as a **play**. You are an actor in a drama, of whatever kind the author chooses. If the author wants you to play a poor man, play him well. If a cripple, a ruler or a private citizen, play that well too. Your business is to act well the character assigned to you; choosing it belongs to another.

**The Indian parallel.** This is very close to the Indian idea of ***svadharma***, your own duty according to your situation and relationships. The Gita says it is better to do your own dharma imperfectly than another's well. And Indian family life is full of role-duties: what it means to be a son, a daughter-in-law, an elder brother, a *mama* or a *chachi*.

But notice an important difference. Epictetus' roles come partly from nature (being a human, a citizen of the world), partly from relationships, and partly from personal choice (becoming a philosopher, choosing a profession). He did not believe one's role was fixed by birth into a social group. A slave could become a philosopher; he himself had.

**Why it matters.** Role-thinking gives practical guidance. When you don't know what to do, ask: what does my role as a parent, friend, teacher or citizen require of me here? It also frees you from a common trap. Your duty does not depend on whether others do theirs. "My brother is unfair, so I'll be unfair too" is, for Epictetus, a confusion. His unfairness is his business. Your conduct is yours.

### Idea 7: Two handles

**The idea in one line:** Every situation can be picked up in a way that helps you or in a way that hurts you; choose the right handle.

This is one of the most beautiful passages in the *Handbook*:

"Everything has two handles, the one by which it may be carried, the other by which it cannot. If your brother acts unjustly, do not lay hold on the action by the handle of his injustice, for by that it cannot be carried; but by the opposite, that he is your brother, that he was brought up with you; and thus you will lay hold on it, as it is to be carried."

**An example.** Imagine two brothers fighting over the division of their father's house. Every Indian family has heard such a story; many have lived it. One brother feels cheated. He can pick up the situation by the handle of "injustice": he is wronged, he will fight, he will go to court, he will not speak to his brother again. That handle cannot carry the situation. It will only drop it, breaking the family for years, perhaps generations.

Or he can pick it up by the other handle: "This is my brother. We grew up together. Our parents loved us both." That doesn't mean he must accept every unfairness. But it changes how he approaches it: with patience, with a willingness to talk, with a sense of what matters more than a few hundred square feet.

The Ramayana has a famous image of this choice. When Bharata was offered the throne that should have gone to his elder brother Rama, he could have taken it. Instead he went to the forest, begged Rama to return, and when Rama refused, placed Rama's sandals on the throne and ruled only as his brother's representative. He picked up the situation by the handle of brotherhood.

### Idea 8: Nothing is lost, only returned

**The idea in one line:** Everything we have is on loan, so when it goes, we should think of it as returned, not taken.

This is one of Epictetus' hardest teachings. In the *Handbook* he says: never say of anything, "I have lost it", but rather, "I have returned it". Is your child dead? It has been returned. Is your wife dead? She has been returned. Is your estate taken? That too has been returned. As long as it is given to you, take care of it, but as of something not your own, as travellers treat an inn.

He also advises a startling exercise. When you kiss your child goodnight, he says, remind yourself that you are kissing a mortal being, who may not be here tomorrow.

**How should we read this?** For many readers, this sounds cold, almost inhuman. Can a grieving parent really say "returned"? But consider what Epictetus is trying to do. He is not telling us not to love. He is telling us to love with our eyes open, aware that everything we cherish is temporary. And that awareness, he believed, makes us treasure people more, not less, and helps us survive when the loss comes.

Many traditions share this insight. The Buddhist teaching of impermanence asks us to see that everything conditioned will pass. There is a well-known story in the Buddhist tradition of Kisa Gotami, a mother maddened by grief at her child's death, who comes to the Buddha begging for medicine to revive him. He asks her to bring a mustard seed from a house where no one has ever died. She goes from house to house and finds none. Slowly she understands that death touches every family, and her grief becomes understanding.

**A gentle note.** These are teachings to prepare the mind, not commands to stop grieving. Grief is a natural expression of love. Epictetus is offering a perspective that many people have found consoling, not a rule that grief is wrong.

### Idea 9: Life as a banquet

**The idea in one line:** Behave in life as you would at a polite dinner: take your share when it comes, don't grab, and don't cling to what has passed.

In the *Handbook* he gives a simple image. Remember that you must behave as at a banquet. Is anything brought round to you? Put out your hand and take a moderate share. Does it pass by you? Do not stop it. Is it not yet come? Do not yearn after it, but wait till it reaches you.

So too with children, a spouse, office, wealth. Take them when they come, without greed. Let them go when they pass, without clinging. Don't stretch across the table for what isn't yours yet.

**Why it matters.** Think of how much unhappiness comes from reaching for things that are "not yet come" (the promotion your colleague got first, the marriage your cousin has, the house your friend bought) or from clinging to what has passed (a relationship that ended, a job you lost, your youth). The banquet image is a gentle reminder of patience and grace.

### Idea 10: Show it, don't say it

**The idea in one line:** Philosophy is proved by how you live, not by how much you talk about it.

Epictetus was harsh with students who could quote the great Stoic Chrysippus but couldn't control their tempers. Imagine a wrestler, he said, who boasts about his training manual but cannot wrestle. The point of philosophy is not to recite it but to become better.

In the *Handbook* he advises: never proclaim yourself a philosopher, nor talk much among ordinary people about your principles. Show them by your actions. At a banquet, don't lecture others on how to eat; eat as you ought to.

And he has a wonderfully practical piece of advice about criticism. If someone tells you that a certain person speaks ill of you, don't defend yourself. Reply instead: "He does not know my other faults, else he would not have mentioned only these."

Kabir has a famous couplet in the same spirit: *nindak niyare rakhiye, aangan kuti chhawaye*. Keep your critic close, build him a hut in your courtyard; without soap or water, he cleans your nature.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Socrates: the hero

Epictetus mentions **Socrates** constantly, more than any other figure. For him, Socrates was the living proof that his philosophy worked. Socrates could not be made to betray his principles by threats of death. Socrates could live with almost nothing. Socrates faced his accusers calmly, saying (in Epictetus' paraphrase) that Anytus and Meletus can kill me, but they cannot harm me.

Epictetus also borrowed Socrates' teaching method: sharp questions that expose the student's confusion. His discourses are full of little dialogues in which a student says something confident and Epictetus asks, "Is that so? Let us examine it."

### Seneca and Marcus Aurelius: the other Roman Stoics

Seneca, the millionaire senator, and Epictetus, the former slave, teach remarkably similar things, which shows that Stoicism was not a philosophy for one class only. But their styles differ. Seneca is literary, warm, and willing to confess his weaknesses. Epictetus is blunt, demanding and often funny in a harsh way. If Seneca is a kind older friend, Epictetus is a tough coach who will not let you make excuses.

**Marcus Aurelius**, the emperor, never met Epictetus. But in his *Meditations* he thanks his teacher Rusticus for lending him the "commentaries of Epictetus", and he quotes Epictetus again and again. Think about what this means: a Roman emperor, the most powerful man in the world, learning how to live from the recorded words of a former slave.

### The Buddha: the mind as the battlefield

Both Epictetus and the Buddha locate the root of suffering inside us: for Epictetus, in false judgements and attachment to what is not ours; for the Buddha, in craving and ignorance. Both teach that we cannot control the world, but we can train the mind. Both use daily practices.

There are deep differences, though. The Buddha taught that the self itself is not a fixed, permanent thing (*anatta*). Epictetus, on the other hand, made the self, the faculty of choice, the one thing that is truly ours and must be guarded. And the Buddha's goal is liberation from the cycle of rebirth; Epictetus' is living rightly as a citizen of a rational universe.

### Descartes and the Christian thinkers

When **René Descartes** wrote his *Discourse on the Method* in 1637, he set out a few rules to live by while he rebuilt his philosophy. The third was to try always to conquer himself rather than fortune, and to change his desires rather than the order of the world, since nothing is entirely in our power except our thoughts. That is Epictetus almost word for word.

Christian monks in the early Middle Ages adapted the *Enchiridion* as a guide for monastic life, simply changing a few pagan references. The French mathematician and religious thinker **Blaise Pascal** admired Epictetus greatly as someone who understood human duty, though he thought Epictetus overestimated human strength and underestimated our need for God's grace.

### The critic's case: Stoicism as a philosophy of acceptance

Here is a serious objection, which many thinkers on the political left have raised against Stoicism in general. If you teach people to accept what is not in their control, to find freedom inside rather than demand it outside, are you not teaching the oppressed to accept their oppression? A slave who is "free inside" is still a slave. A worker who accepts low wages with Stoic calm is a very convenient worker for the factory owner.

Epictetus' defenders reply that he never told anyone to stop trying to change things; he told them to put their effort where they actually have influence, and not to be destroyed by what they cannot change. Many Stoics, including Musonius, were exiled precisely because they resisted tyrants. But the objection still has force. We should be careful not to use "it's not in my control" as an excuse for not trying to change unjust systems, which, together with others, we often *can* influence.

### Modern psychology and the "serenity prayer"

You may know the "Serenity Prayer", popularised by Alcoholics Anonymous: *God, grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change, courage to change the things I can, and wisdom to know the difference.* It is usually credited to the American theologian Reinhold Niebuhr in the twentieth century. It is Epictetus' dichotomy of control in the form of a prayer.

## See it around you

**Exam season.** Every year, millions of Indian students prepare for board exams and entrance tests where a single result can shape their future. The pressure can be crushing. Epictetus would say: your preparation, your effort, your calm on the day are in your control; the paper, the marking, the cut-off and the number of seats are not. Give everything to the first list. Let go of the second.

**Social media.** Likes, followers, comments, shares: none of these are in your control. Yet many people's mood rises and falls with them. Epictetus would ask: you have handed your peace of mind to strangers. Why?

**The workplace.** Promotions, appraisals, office politics, layoffs. You can control the quality of your work, your honesty, how you treat colleagues. Your manager's mood and the company's finances are not yours. People who confuse the two burn out.

**Family conflicts.** Property disputes, in-law tensions, disagreements about marriage and careers. Epictetus' "two handles" and his teaching that your duty doesn't depend on others doing theirs can transform how you approach them.

**Traffic and waiting.** Traffic jams, delayed trains, long queues at government offices. The delay is not in your control. Your fury is. It changes nothing except your blood pressure.

**Therapy and mental health.** If you or someone you know has done CBT, you have met Epictetus. The idea that thoughts can be examined and reframed comes straight from him.

**Leadership under pressure.** Military academies and business schools teach Stoicism to future leaders, partly because of Stockdale's story. A leader who stays calm in a crisis, focusing on what can be done rather than panicking about what can't, steadies everyone around them.

**Sport.** Elite athletes talk about "controlling the controllables": training, preparation, focus. They cannot control the referee, the weather or the opponent. That is Epictetus with a jersey on.

## Use it

**1. Draw the line.** Next time you are worried, take a sheet of paper. Draw a line down the middle. On the left, write what is in your control. On the right, what is not. Then make a plan only for the left side, and practise letting go of the right.

**2. Separate the event from the story.** When something upsets you, write two sentences. "What happened:" (just the facts). "What I'm telling myself:" (the judgement). Then question the second sentence. Is it true? Is it helpful?

**3. Say "you are just an impression".** When a strong reaction arises, anger, fear, jealousy, mentally say: "You are just an impression, not the thing itself." Take one breath. Then decide how to respond.

**4. Find the right handle.** In a conflict with someone close, ask: what is the other handle? "She hurt me" is one handle. "She is my sister, and we've been through so much" is another. Pick up the one that can carry the situation.

**5. Ask what your role requires.** When you are unsure what to do, ask: what does my role as a parent, child, friend, employee or citizen require of me here? Do that, regardless of whether others are doing their part.

**6. Practise the banquet.** When you see something you want that hasn't come to you yet, the promotion, the relationship, the recognition, remind yourself: it's not my turn yet. Don't stretch across the table.

**7. Love with open eyes.** Once in a while, when you hug someone you love, remember that this moment won't last forever. Let that make you more present and more grateful, not more afraid.

**8. Answer criticism with humour.** When you hear someone criticised you, try Epictetus' reply, even just in your head: "He doesn't know my other faults, or he'd have mentioned those too." Notice how the sting fades.

**9. Do, don't announce.** If you are trying to improve yourself, do it quietly. Let your actions speak.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Accepting slavery.** Epictetus, a former slave, never called for the abolition of slavery. His teaching that freedom is internal could be used, and has been used, to suggest that external oppression doesn't really matter. That is a dangerous conclusion. External freedom matters enormously, and many oppressed people have rightly fought for it.

**2. Too much responsibility on the individual.** By teaching that our suffering comes from our judgements, Epictetus can make people feel that their pain is their own fault. But some suffering is caused by real injustice, poverty, discrimination or illness, and the right response is not only to change your judgement but to change the situation, or to demand that others change it.

**3. The coldness problem.** "Your child has been returned" can sound heartless to a grieving parent. Stoicism's emphasis on detachment can slide into emotional suppression, which modern psychology warns is harmful. Grief, love and attachment are part of a full human life.

**4. The line is not always clear.** What exactly is "in our control"? Even our judgements are shaped by upbringing, trauma, brain chemistry and circumstances. A person with severe depression cannot simply decide to judge things differently. Modern therapists who use Epictetus' ideas treat them as skills to be practised with support, not as instant choices.

**5. Collective action is missing.** The dichotomy of control is about an individual. But many things that are not in *my* control are in *our* control, together: laws, elections, working conditions, environmental policy. A philosophy that only asks "what can I control?" may miss the power of acting with others.

**6. Little room for joy and passion.** Epictetus' ideal person is calm, steady and undisturbed. But many people feel that a good life also includes passionate love, ecstatic joy, righteous anger and wild creativity. A life with no strong emotions may be peaceful, but is it full?

## The shift

**Before this page,** you probably thought that your feelings were caused by what happens to you: the match result, the boss's comment, the traffic, the exam paper, the unanswered message. If you were upset, something out there had upset you.

**Now,** you can see a gap between what happens and how you respond, and you can see that the gap belongs to you. Events are what they are. Your judgement of them is yours to examine. And the one thing no one can ever take from you is your choice about who you will be.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **Every worry** sorts itself into two columns: what is mine to act on, and what is not mine to carry.
- **An insult** loses some of its power, because you know the hurt is partly a story you are adding.
- **A traffic jam** stops being an enemy. It becomes a place where you can practise.
- **A family quarrel** shows you two handles, and you start reaching for the one that can actually carry it.
- **Your exam result, your appraisal, your follower count** stop being verdicts on your worth. They become things that happened, while your effort and character stay yours.
- **Freedom** stops meaning only "doing whatever I want". It starts to mean "not being owned by what I want".

A man whose very name meant "acquired", who belonged to another person for years, found the one possession no one could take. He spent the rest of his life showing others where to look for it. It was inside them the whole time.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Epictetus (c. 50 – c. 135 CE), born a slave in Phrygia, owned by Nero's secretary Epaphroditus, freed, taught in Rome, exiled by Domitian, and founded a famous school at Nicopolis.
- **Sources:** he wrote nothing; his student Arrian recorded the *Discourses* and compiled the short *Handbook*.
- **Dichotomy of control:** some things are up to us (our judgements, desires and choices) and some are not (body, property, reputation, outcomes).
- **Judgements:** we are disturbed not by events but by our opinions about them; test every impression before assenting.
- **Freedom:** your faculty of choice (*prohairesis*) is your true self and cannot be conquered; the free person wants nothing others can withhold.
- **Practical teachings:** duties come from relationships; everything has two handles; everything we have is on loan; behave in life as at a banquet; show your philosophy by actions.
- **Legacy:** shaped Marcus Aurelius, Christian monasticism, Descartes, Pascal and modern cognitive therapy; criticised for accepting slavery and for encouraging passivity.

**Key terms**

- **Dichotomy of control:** the division between what is up to us and what is not.
- **Prohairesis:** the faculty of choice or will, the inviolable core of the self.
- **Impression (phantasia):** the first appearance of something in the mind.
- **Assent:** accepting an impression as true and acting on it.
- **Enchiridion:** "handbook", the short summary of Epictetus' teaching.
- **Svadharma (Indian parallel):** one's own duty according to one's situation and relationships.
- **CBT:** cognitive behavioural therapy, a modern therapy built partly on the Stoic idea that thoughts shape emotions.
