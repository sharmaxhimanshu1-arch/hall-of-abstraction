## Life

Friedrich Wilhelm Nietzsche was born in 1844 in Röcken, a village in Prussian Saxony, the son of a Lutheran pastor who died when Friedrich was four. He grew up in a household of women and was a brilliant pupil at the elite boarding school of Schulpforta. At the universities of Bonn and Leipzig he studied classical philology, and in Leipzig he discovered the pessimistic philosophy of **Arthur Schopenhauer**, which overwhelmed him. He also met the composer **Richard Wagner**, who became a father figure and, later, his great antagonist.

In 1869, aged just twenty-four and without a completed doctorate, Nietzsche was appointed professor of classical philology at the University of Basel. He served briefly as a medical orderly in the Franco-Prussian War, where he probably contracted diphtheria and dysentery. His first book, *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872), dazzled Wagner's circle but scandalised fellow scholars. Over the next years he broke with Wagner, repelled by his nationalism, antisemitism and turn to Christian themes in *Parsifal*.

Chronic illness, including migraines, near-blindness and vomiting, forced him to resign his professorship in 1879. For the next decade he lived as a stateless wanderer on a small pension, moving between boarding houses in Switzerland, Italy and France, summering in the Alpine village of Sils Maria. In near-total obscurity he wrote the works that made him famous after his death. In 1882 he proposed to the brilliant Lou Salomé, who refused him.

In January 1889, in Turin, Nietzsche suffered a complete mental collapse. According to a famous but unverified story, it began when he threw his arms around a horse being flogged in the street. He spent his last eleven years in a state of mental incapacity, cared for by his mother and then his sister, **Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche**, and died in Weimar in 1900. Elisabeth, a nationalist and antisemite whose views her brother had despised, took control of his archive, edited and in places falsified his writings, and later courted the Nazis, who claimed him as a prophet.

## Key ideas

Nietzsche wrote in aphorisms, polemics, poetry and prophetic fiction rather than systematic treatises. He philosophised "with a hammer", and his provocative style invites conflicting interpretations.

**The death of God.** In *The Gay Science* a madman announces, "God is dead... And we have killed him." Nietzsche meant that belief in the Christian God had become unbelievable to modern Europe, an event whose consequences most people had not yet grasped. With God goes the foundation for absolute truth and morality.

**Nihilism.** The collapse of these values threatens **nihilism**: the sense that nothing has meaning or worth. Nietzsche saw it as the great crisis of the coming centuries and sought a way through it.

**Master and slave morality.** In *On the Genealogy of Morality* he argued that our moral values have a history. Aristocratic "master morality" called the strong, proud and healthy "good". The weak, out of *ressentiment*, inverted these values in a "slave revolt in morality", praising humility, pity and meekness as "good" and condemning strength as "evil". Christianity, he argued, was its triumph.

**Will to power.** The basic drive in life is not survival or pleasure but **will to power**, the drive to grow, overcome and express strength, which can be sublimated into art, philosophy and self-mastery.

**The Übermensch.** In *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* the prophet Zarathustra proclaims the **Übermensch** ("overman" or "superman"), an ideal of a human being who creates their own values and affirms life without needing the consolations of religion.

**Eternal recurrence and amor fati.** Could you will to live your life again, exactly the same, an infinite number of times? To say yes is the highest affirmation of life: ***amor fati***, love of fate.

**Perspectivism.** There are no facts, only interpretations. Every view is a perspective shaped by needs and drives, though some perspectives are richer and healthier than others.

## Influence and legacy

Nietzsche's influence on twentieth-century thought and culture is vast: on existentialism, on Freud's psychology, on Heidegger, on French thinkers such as Foucault, Deleuze and Derrida, and on writers from Thomas Mann and Rilke to Camus. His association with Nazism, due largely to his sister's manipulations and selective quotation, was dismantled after the war by scholars such as Walter Kaufmann. He is now read as one of the most penetrating critics of morality, religion and modern culture, a thinker who asked how humanity can live, and live well, after the loss of its oldest certainties.
