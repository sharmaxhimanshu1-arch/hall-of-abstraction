## Life

The life of Siddhartha Gautama comes to us through texts written down centuries after his death and rich in legend, so the details are uncertain. The traditional account runs as follows.

He was born a prince of the **Shakya** clan at Lumbini, near the city of Kapilavastu, in the foothills of the Himalayas in what is now Nepal. His father, Suddhodana, determined that his son would become a great king rather than a holy man, raised him in luxury and shielded him from all suffering. Siddhartha married Yasodhara and had a son, Rahula.

At twenty-nine, venturing beyond the palace, he encountered the **Four Sights**: an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a wandering ascetic at peace. Shaken by the realisation that ageing, sickness and death await everyone, he left his family and palace in the night, the "Great Renunciation", to seek liberation. He studied meditation under two renowned teachers, then spent some six years practising extreme austerities, fasting until he was skeletal. Concluding that self-mortification was as fruitless as self-indulgence, he accepted a bowl of rice milk and resolved to follow a **Middle Way**.

Sitting beneath a fig tree at Bodh Gaya, later called the Bodhi tree, he meditated through the night and attained **awakening** (*bodhi*), becoming the Buddha, "the Awakened One". He was about thirty-five. At the Deer Park near Sarnath he gave his first sermon to five former companions, "setting in motion the wheel of the Dharma". For the next forty-five years he wandered the Ganges plain teaching people of every caste, and founded a monastic community (*sangha*) that, after some hesitation, came to include nuns. He died at about eighty at Kushinagar, after a meal that made him ill. His last words urged his followers to "strive on with diligence."

## Key ideas

The Buddha presented his teaching (*Dharma*) as practical rather than speculative, like a doctor's diagnosis and cure. He often declined to answer metaphysical questions, such as whether the world is eternal, that did not lead toward liberation.

**The Four Noble Truths.**
1. Life involves *dukkha*: suffering, stress and unsatisfactoriness.
2. *Dukkha* arises from craving (*tanha*): for pleasure, for existence, for non-existence.
3. *Dukkha* can cease, and that cessation is *nirvana*.
4. The way to cessation is the **Noble Eightfold Path**: right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration.

**The three marks of existence.** All conditioned things are impermanent (*anicca*), unsatisfactory (*dukkha*), and without a permanent self (*anatta*). What we call the self is a changing stream of physical and mental processes, and clinging to it as fixed is a root of suffering.

**Dependent origination.** Everything arises in dependence on conditions, and nothing exists independently. This web of causation explains both how suffering is produced and how it can be undone.

**Karma and rebirth.** Intentional actions have consequences that shape future experience, across lives as well as within one. Liberation means escape from this cycle of rebirth (*samsara*).

**Compassion and non-harm.** Ethical conduct, loving-kindness (*metta*) and compassion for all sentient beings are integral to the path, not optional extras.

## Influence and legacy

The Buddha's teachings were preserved orally by his monks and written down, in the Pali Canon, in Sri Lanka in the first century BCE. In the third century BCE the Indian emperor **Ashoka** converted and sent missionaries across Asia. Buddhism divided into major traditions, Theravada in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, Mahayana in China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam, and Vajrayana in Tibet, and developed sophisticated philosophical schools of its own.

Buddhist ideas reached Western philosophy through Schopenhauer in the nineteenth century, and in the twentieth century Zen and Tibetan teachers attracted wide followings in Europe and America. Today mindfulness meditation, adapted from Buddhist practice by figures such as Jon Kabat-Zinn, is used in medicine and psychology, and Buddhist accounts of the self are discussed in cognitive science.
