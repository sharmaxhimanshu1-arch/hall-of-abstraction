## Life

Kong Qiu, later honoured as Kongzi or Kong Fuzi, "Master Kong", which Jesuit missionaries latinised as **Confucius**, was born in 551 BCE in the small state of Lu in eastern China. It was the late Spring and Autumn period: the Zhou dynasty's kings had lost real power, and rival lords and ministerial families fought for control. Confucius' father, a minor aristocrat and soldier, died when he was a child, and he grew up in modest circumstances. He held humble posts, such as keeper of granaries and overseer of livestock, while devoting himself to study of the rites, music, history and poetry of the early Zhou, which he regarded as a lost golden age.

According to tradition he rose to high office in Lu, perhaps as minister of crime, but grew disillusioned with its rulers. Around 497 BCE he left and spent some fourteen years travelling from state to state with a band of disciples, seeking a ruler who would put his ideas into practice. None did. At times he was in real danger, and once his party nearly starved. Returning to Lu around 484 BCE, he devoted his last years to teaching. Tradition holds that he had some three thousand students, of whom seventy-two mastered his teachings. He died in 479 BCE, believing he had failed.

## Key ideas

Confucius said that he was "a transmitter and not a maker", a restorer of ancient wisdom rather than an innovator. In fact he reshaped that tradition profoundly. His sayings and conversations were collected by his followers in the *Analects*.

**Ren: humaneness.** The highest virtue is *ren*, variously translated as humaneness, benevolence or goodness: a deep concern for others that is expressed in how we treat them. Asked for a single word to guide life, Confucius offered *shu*, reciprocity: "What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others."

**Li: ritual propriety.** *Li* originally meant sacrificial ritual but came to cover the whole range of courtesy, etiquette and social custom. Confucius saw these forms not as empty formalities but as the means through which respect and humaneness are cultivated and expressed.

**The exemplary person.** The *junzi*, once a term for a nobleman by birth, became in Confucius' teaching a person noble by character: someone who pursues righteousness rather than profit, learns constantly, and has "a dignified ease without pride".

**Family and filial piety.** Moral education begins at home. Respect and care for parents (*xiao*) is the root of all virtue, and a well-ordered society grows outward from well-ordered families.

**Government by virtue.** Rulers should lead by moral example, not force and punishment: "If the people be led by virtue, and uniformity sought to be given them by the rules of propriety, they will have the sense of shame, and moreover will become good." He also called for the **rectification of names**: social order requires that a ruler truly act as a ruler, a father as a father.

**The love of learning.** Confucius taught anyone willing to learn, regardless of birth, an idea revolutionary in its time.

## Influence and legacy

Confucius' followers Mencius and Xunzi developed his ideas in opposing directions, Mencius arguing that human nature is good and Xunzi that it must be shaped. Under the Han dynasty, from the second century BCE, Confucianism became the state ideology of China. The Confucian classics formed the basis of the imperial civil service examinations, which in one form or another selected officials until 1905. In the Song dynasty, Zhu Xi's Neo-Confucianism reinterpreted the tradition and spread throughout Korea, Japan and Vietnam.

In the twentieth century Chinese reformers attacked Confucianism as a source of hierarchy and stagnation, and the Cultural Revolution vandalised his temple and tomb. Since the 1980s he has been widely rehabilitated, and debates over "Confucian values", meritocracy, family and social harmony continue across East Asia and beyond.
