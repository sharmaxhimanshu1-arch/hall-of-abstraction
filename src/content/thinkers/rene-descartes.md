## Start here

You are watching a video on your phone. A famous politician is saying something shocking. The face is his. The voice is his. The lips move perfectly with the words.

A few hours later you learn that the video was a **deepfake**, entirely generated by artificial intelligence. He never said any of it.

Now an uncomfortable thought creeps in. If your eyes and ears can be fooled that completely by a video, how many other things have they fooled you about? How do you know the photographs in the news are real? The voice on the phone? The "facts" you have read? And going further: how do you know the world around you is real at all? You have had dreams so vivid that, while dreaming, you were completely sure they were real. How do you know you are not dreaming right now?

These questions feel very twenty-first century. But a Frenchman asked them almost four hundred years ago, with no phones, no AI and no films like *The Matrix*. He imagined that a powerful, evil being might be deceiving him about everything: the sky, the earth, his own body, even the simplest mathematics. And he asked: **if I can doubt everything, is there anything left that I can be absolutely certain of?**

He found one thing. Even if he was dreaming, even if he was being deceived about everything else, there was one fact that could not be doubted: **he was thinking**. And if he was thinking, he must exist. In French: *Je pense, donc je suis*. In Latin: *Cogito, ergo sum*. In English: **"I think, therefore I am."**

His name was **René Descartes**. He was a soldier, a mathematician, a scientist and a philosopher. He invented the coordinate graph that every school student uses. He helped create modern physics and modern mathematics. And he changed philosophy so completely that he is often called **"the father of modern philosophy"**.

By the end of this page, you will understand why he doubted everything, what he found at the bottom of his doubt, how he tried to rebuild all of knowledge from there, and why his idea that the mind and body are two different kinds of thing has troubled philosophers, doctors and neuroscientists ever since. And in a world full of deepfakes, fake news and AI, you will have a powerful mental tool for asking: what do I *really* know?

## The world he was born into

### Old certainties crumbling

Descartes was born in 1596 in France. To understand why he wanted to doubt everything, you need to know how many old certainties were collapsing around him.

**The heavens had moved.** For nearly two thousand years, educated Europeans had believed, following Aristotle and the astronomer Ptolemy, that the Earth was the motionless centre of the universe, with the Sun, Moon, planets and stars circling it. In 1543, **Copernicus** published a book arguing that the Earth goes around the Sun. In 1609–10, **Galileo** turned a telescope to the sky and saw mountains on the Moon, moons circling Jupiter and phases of Venus, all evidence against the old picture. Something everyone had "known" for centuries turned out to be wrong.

**The New World.** European explorers had reached the Americas a century earlier, discovering lands, peoples and civilisations unknown to the ancient authorities. If the ancients had been ignorant of an entire continent, what else were they wrong about?

**Religion was at war.** The Protestant Reformation, begun by Martin Luther in 1517, had split Western Christianity. Catholics and Protestants each claimed to have the truth and accused the other of heresy. Religious wars tore Europe apart. The **Thirty Years' War** (1618–1648), which Descartes saw firsthand, devastated much of central Europe. If two sides could be equally certain and equally willing to kill, how could anyone know who was right?

**Scepticism was fashionable.** Ancient Greek sceptical texts had been rediscovered and printed. The French essayist **Michel de Montaigne**, who died when Descartes was a baby, had popularised a gentle scepticism with his motto *Que sais-je?*, "What do I know?". Many educated people were concluding that certainty was impossible.

### The universities: still teaching Aristotle

Meanwhile, the universities were still teaching **Aristotle**, as interpreted by medieval Christian scholars (a system called **scholasticism**). Students learned that a stone falls because it "seeks" its natural place at the centre of the Earth, that heavenly bodies are made of a perfect fifth element, that every natural thing has purposes built into it. Learning meant memorising authorities and debating fine distinctions.

Descartes went through this education and came out disappointed. He wanted something better: knowledge as certain as mathematics, built on foundations no sceptic could shake, that would actually help humans understand and master nature.

That was his mission: **to find a firm foundation for knowledge in an age when every foundation seemed to be cracking.**

## The life, as a story

### A sickly boy who stayed in bed

René Descartes was born on 31 March 1596 in La Haye, a small town in the Touraine region of France. The town is now called **Descartes** in his honour. His father was a lawyer and member of the regional parliament of Brittany. His mother died about a year after his birth. René was a sickly child and was raised mainly by his grandmother and a nurse.

At around ten, he was sent to the **Jesuit college of La Flèche**, one of the best schools in Europe. He studied Latin, Greek, classical literature, mathematics, physics and scholastic philosophy. Because of his weak health, the rector allowed him to stay in bed in the mornings. Descartes kept this habit all his life: he did much of his best thinking lying in bed until late morning. (Remember this. It becomes important at the end of the story.)

He later wrote in the *Discourse on the Method* that he had been nourished on letters since childhood, and was eager to learn. But by the end of his studies, he found himself so full of doubts and errors that the only benefit of his education seemed to be the discovery of his own ignorance. Only **mathematics** delighted him, because of the certainty and clarity of its reasoning. He was surprised that nothing grander had been built on such solid foundations.

He took a law degree at the University of Poitiers in 1616, to please his family. But he had no intention of practising law.

### "The great book of the world"

Instead, he resolved to seek no knowledge except what he could find in himself or in **"the great book of the world"**. He spent several years travelling, visiting courts and armies, mixing with people of every kind, and reflecting on what he saw.

In 1618 he joined the army of **Maurice of Nassau** in the Netherlands as a gentleman volunteer. (He seems to have seen little actual fighting.) In the Dutch town of Breda, he met **Isaac Beeckman**, a schoolmaster and scientist eight years older, who loved mathematics and physics. Beeckman shared Descartes' dream of explaining nature mathematically. The two worked on problems together, and Descartes later wrote that Beeckman had woken him from idleness.

### Three dreams in a warm room

In 1619, Descartes joined the army of the Duke of Bavaria. That winter, near the town of Ulm in Germany, bad weather kept the army in its quarters. Descartes spent his days alone in a room heated by a stove (in French, a *poêle*), thinking.

On the night of **10 November 1619**, after a day of intense excitement about a new idea, he had **three vivid dreams**. In the first, he was blown by a whirlwind and struggling to walk to a chapel. In the second, he heard a thunderclap and saw sparks filling the room. In the third, he found a dictionary and an anthology of poems on his table, and opened it to a poem beginning with the Latin line *Quod vitae sectabor iter?*, "What path in life shall I follow?"

He interpreted the dreams as a sign: his mission was to create a new, unified science, built on the certainty of mathematics. It is a strange and beautiful irony that the philosopher of pure reason found his vocation in a dream.

### Twenty years in Holland

For the next several years Descartes travelled and lived in Paris, working on mathematics and optics. Then, in 1628, he moved to the **Dutch Republic**, where he lived for most of the next twenty years. The Netherlands was then the most tolerant and intellectually free country in Europe, with a booming economy, a free press and great universities. Even so, Descartes moved house constantly, perhaps a couple of dozen times, to protect his privacy and his time.

He lived quietly. He had a relationship with a servant named **Helena Jans van der Strom**, and in 1635 they had a daughter, **Francine**. Descartes was devoted to her and planned to have her educated in France. In 1640, at the age of five, Francine died, probably of scarlet fever. Descartes said it was the greatest sorrow of his life.

### The book he hid

By 1633, Descartes had nearly finished a major work called *The World*, explaining the whole physical universe mechanically, including the idea that the Earth moves around the Sun. Then he heard news from Rome: **Galileo** had been condemned by the Catholic Inquisition for teaching that the Earth moves.

Descartes was shocked. He was a sincere Catholic and did not want conflict with the Church. He decided not to publish *The World*. It appeared only after his death. Think about what this tells us. The great champion of certain knowledge had to hide his own conclusions for fear of religious authorities. Freedom of thought was not something he could take for granted.

### The books that changed philosophy

Instead, he published more cautiously. In 1637 came the ***Discourse on the Method***, with three essays on optics, meteorology and geometry. Unusually, he wrote it in **French**, not Latin, the language of scholars. He said he hoped that people who used their natural reason, including those who had not studied Latin, and including women, would judge his ideas better than those who only believed old books.

In 1641 came the ***Meditations on First Philosophy***, in Latin, his deepest work. Before publishing it, he sent it to leading thinkers and published their **objections** along with his **replies**: objections from the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes, the French theologian Antoine Arnauld, the philosopher Pierre Gassendi and others. This was a brilliant idea, a kind of early peer review, and the exchanges are still studied today.

In 1644 he published the *Principles of Philosophy*, a textbook version of his whole system, which he hoped would replace Aristotle in the universities. His ideas made him famous, and also controversial. Protestant theologians at the University of Utrecht condemned his philosophy, and there were bitter public disputes.

### The princess who asked the hardest question

From 1643, Descartes carried on a remarkable correspondence with **Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia**, a brilliant young woman living in exile in the Netherlands. In her very first letter she asked him the question that has haunted his philosophy ever since: if the mind is not physical, how can it move the body? How can something with no size, shape or location push or pull a physical arm?

Descartes' answers did not fully satisfy her, and they have not satisfied most philosophers since. But the correspondence led him to think more about emotions, and he wrote *The Passions of the Soul* (1649), partly in response to her.

### The cold queen and the early mornings

In 1649, **Queen Christina of Sweden**, a young and fiercely intellectual monarch, invited Descartes to Stockholm to teach her philosophy. After some hesitation, he accepted.

It was a mistake. Christina wanted her lessons at **five o'clock in the morning**, in the freezing Swedish winter. Descartes, who had spent his whole life lying in bed until late morning, now had to get up in the dark and walk to the palace through the cold. He also felt out of place at court and wrote that men's thoughts seemed to freeze there in winter, like the water.

In February 1650, he caught pneumonia. He died on **11 February 1650**, aged fifty-three. (In recent years, one scholar has suggested he might have been poisoned with arsenic; most historians remain unconvinced.)

His body was later moved to France. His skull ended up separated from the rest of his remains and is held today in a museum in Paris. In 1663, the Catholic Church placed his works on its Index of Prohibited Books.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: A method for thinking

**The idea in one line:** Good thinking needs a clear method, just as good building needs a plan.

In the *Discourse on the Method*, Descartes proposed four simple rules for reasoning:

1. **Never accept anything as true unless you clearly know it to be so.** Avoid hasty judgement and prejudice. Accept only what is so clear and distinct that you have no reason to doubt it.
2. **Divide each difficulty into as many parts as possible**, to solve it better.
3. **Think in order**, starting from the simplest and easiest things, and climbing step by step to the most complex.
4. **Make complete lists and reviews**, so that you are sure nothing has been left out.

**An example.** Imagine you are a doctor facing a patient with confusing symptoms. Rule 1: don't jump to a diagnosis because the patient "looks like" a typical case. Rule 2: break the problem down: fever, cough, fatigue, test results, history. Rule 3: start with the simplest checks and work towards complex ones. Rule 4: before concluding, review everything to make sure nothing is missing.

Or a student solving a hard mathematics problem, or an engineer debugging a broken system, or a detective solving a crime. All use this method, whether or not they've heard of Descartes.

**Why it matters.** This seems obvious to us because we have absorbed it. In Descartes' time, much learning consisted of quoting authorities. His method said: don't trust authority; trust clear reasoning, step by step. It became one of the foundations of the scientific mindset.

He also opened the *Discourse* with a sentence that is both a joke and a deep point: "Good sense is, of all things among men, the most equally distributed; for every one thinks himself so abundantly provided with it" that even those hardest to satisfy in everything else do not usually want more of it than they have. In other words, everybody thinks they have plenty of common sense. The problem is not having reason; it is **using it well**. That needs a method.

### Idea 2: The method of doubt

**The idea in one line:** To find something certain, first doubt everything that can possibly be doubted.

In the *Meditations*, Descartes explains his plan. He realised that since childhood he had accepted many false beliefs, and that everything he had built on them was shaky. So once in his life, he resolved to tear everything down and start again from the foundations.

He compares this to sorting a **basket of apples**. If you fear some of your apples are rotten, and rot spreads, the sensible thing is to tip all of them out, examine each one, and put back only the good ones. Beliefs are the same.

But he doesn't examine every belief one by one; there are too many. Instead, he attacks their **foundations**. Most of our beliefs come from the senses. So: can the senses be trusted?

He builds up doubt in three waves:

**Wave 1: The senses sometimes deceive.** A square tower looks round from a distance. A stick looks bent in water. If the senses sometimes deceive us, it is wise not to trust them completely.

But surely, you might say, I can't doubt that I'm sitting here, wearing my clothes, holding this paper? Only madmen doubt such things.

**Wave 2: The dream argument.** Descartes replies: but how many times have I dreamed that I was sitting by the fire, dressed, when actually I was lying undressed in bed? There are no sure signs to tell waking from dreaming. So perhaps I am dreaming right now.

Still, even in dreams, the basic elements, shapes, sizes, numbers, seem real. Two plus three makes five, whether I'm awake or asleep. A square has four sides in dreams too. Surely mathematics is certain?

**Wave 3: The evil demon.** Descartes now imagines something extreme. Suppose there is an **evil demon**, supremely powerful and cunning, who uses all his energy to deceive me. He could make me see a sky, an earth, colours, shapes, sounds that are all illusions. He could even make me go wrong every time I add two and three. Then I would have no certain knowledge at all.

**An example.** This is exactly the premise of *The Matrix* (1999), in which humans live in a computer simulation so perfect they think it is reality. Philosophers today discuss the **"brain in a vat"**: how do you know you are not a brain floating in a jar, connected to a computer that feeds you a fake world? And some people seriously debate the **simulation hypothesis**, that our universe might be a computer simulation. Every one of these is a modern version of Descartes' evil demon.

**The Indian parallel.** Indian philosophy, too, used the experience of dreaming to question the reality of the external world. The Buddhist philosopher **Vasubandhu**, of the Yogacara school, around the fourth or fifth century CE, argued in his *Twenty Verses* that just as in a dream we experience places, people and even consequences without any external objects, so waking experience might also be mind-only. The Advaita tradition, too, uses the dream as an analogy for how the world of appearances relates to ultimate reality. The arguments and conclusions differ greatly from Descartes'. But the starting move, *how do I know waking life is not like a dream?*, is shared.

**Why it matters.** Descartes was not a sceptic. He did not *believe* the evil demon existed. He used doubt as a **tool**, like a scientist using a stress test: if a belief can survive the most extreme doubt, it is truly solid. This is called **methodical doubt**.

**A common misunderstanding.** People sometimes think Descartes recommends doubting everything in daily life. He explicitly said the opposite: in practical life, we must act on the best available judgement. The method of doubt is an exercise for the study, done once in life to find secure foundations, not a way to live.

### Idea 3: I think, therefore I am

**The idea in one line:** Even if I doubt everything else, I cannot doubt that I am thinking, and so I must exist.

Having doubted everything, Descartes finds himself, as he says, as if he had fallen into deep water and could neither touch bottom nor swim to the surface. Is there anything left?

Then he notices something. Suppose the evil demon is deceiving me. Well, then *I* am being deceived. And if I am being deceived, I must exist. Let him deceive me as much as he can, he can never make it the case that I am nothing, so long as I think I am something. "I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time that I pronounce it, or that I mentally conceive it."

In the earlier *Discourse*, he put it more simply: *Je pense, donc je suis*, I think, therefore I am. This became famous in its Latin form: ***Cogito, ergo sum***. Philosophers call it simply "the cogito".

**Try it yourself.** Try to doubt that you exist. Notice what you are doing: you are doubting. Doubting is a kind of thinking. And to think, you must exist. The very act of doubting your existence proves it. That is what makes the cogito special: it is **self-confirming**.

**What kind of thing is "I"?** Descartes then asks: what exactly is this "I" that I know exists? Not the body, because he can still doubt the body (the demon might be fooling him about having one). He is certain only that he is a **thinking thing**: a thing that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, wills, refuses, imagines and senses.

**The Indian parallel.** In the eighth century, the great Advaita philosopher **Shankaracharya**, in his commentary on the *Brahma Sutras*, made a remarkable observation: everyone is conscious of the existence of their own self, and no one ever thinks "I am not". The self, he argued, is self-evident; even the one who denies it is the self. The great twentieth-century sage **Ramana Maharshi** taught a practice of self-inquiry, asking again and again "Who am I?", to discover the awareness that cannot be doubted. The conclusions are very different: for Shankara, the self that cannot be doubted is ultimately identical with Brahman, pure consciousness; for Descartes, it is an individual thinking substance created by God. But the starting insight, that the knower's own existence is the one thing that cannot be denied, is shared across centuries and continents.

**Why it matters.** The cogito changed the starting point of philosophy. Before Descartes, philosophy usually started from the world, or from God, or from authorities. Descartes started from **the individual thinking mind**. From then on, the question "how do I know?" came first. This inward turn shaped the whole of modern philosophy, and modern ideas of the individual.

**A common misunderstanding.** "I think, therefore I am" is not meant to say that thinking *makes* you exist, or that people who think more exist more. It is a point about **certainty**: of all the things I might believe, my own existence as a thinker is the one I cannot coherently doubt.

### Idea 4: The piece of wax

**The idea in one line:** We know things through the intellect, not just through the senses.

In the second *Meditation*, Descartes takes a piece of **beeswax**, fresh from the honeycomb. It still smells of flowers and tastes of honey. It has a certain colour, shape and size. It is hard, cold, and makes a sound when you tap it.

Now he puts it near the fire. The smell disappears, the taste goes, the colour changes, the shape is lost, it grows larger, becomes liquid and hot, you can hardly touch it, and it makes no sound when tapped. Everything the senses told us about it has changed.

**Is it still the same wax?** Of course, everyone would say. But how do we know? Not by any of the senses, because every sensory quality has changed. We know it is the same wax by our **understanding**: our mind grasps that this is something extended, flexible and changeable, capable of countless shapes, more than the imagination could ever picture.

**An example.** Think of a friend you haven't seen in twenty years. Their face has changed, their hair, their voice, their weight. What makes you sure it's the same person? Not one sensory detail. It's your understanding, putting together their history, memories, behaviour.

**Why it matters.** This supports Descartes' larger view: **reason**, not the senses, is the deepest source of knowledge. That makes him a **rationalist**: someone who believes that reason, not experience alone, gives us our most important knowledge.

### Idea 5: God as the guarantee

**The idea in one line:** A perfect God exists and would not deceive us, so what we perceive clearly and distinctly must be true.

Descartes now has one certainty: "I exist as a thinking thing." But that's not much. How does he get back to the world, to mathematics, to science?

His route goes through **God**. Among the ideas in his mind, he finds the idea of a perfect, infinite being. Where could such an idea come from? Not from himself, because he is finite and imperfect, and the cause of an idea must have at least as much reality as what the idea represents. So, he argues, the idea of a perfect being must have been placed in him by a perfect being: God. (This is sometimes called the **trademark argument**: God stamped the idea in our minds like a craftsman's mark on his work.)

He also offers a version of the **ontological argument**: the idea of a supremely perfect being includes existence, just as the idea of a triangle includes having three angles. So God must exist.

And a perfect God would not be a deceiver, because deception is a defect. So if I use my mind properly and accept only what I perceive **clearly and distinctly**, I will not be deceived. With God as guarantee, Descartes recovers mathematics, the existence of the physical world, and the possibility of science.

**The famous objection: the Cartesian circle.** The theologian **Antoine Arnauld**, in his objections, pointed out a problem. Descartes says we can trust clear and distinct perceptions **because** God exists and is not a deceiver. But how do we know God exists? Because we clearly and distinctly perceive the arguments for God. So we use clear and distinct perception to prove God, and God to prove that clear and distinct perception is reliable. That looks like arguing in a circle. Philosophers still debate whether Descartes has a good answer.

**Why it matters.** Even many who reject his proofs of God find this part of the story revealing. Once you start from the isolated individual mind, getting back to the external world turns out to be very hard. Descartes needed God to build the bridge. Later philosophers would try other bridges, or deny that the gap exists.

### Idea 6: Mind and body, two different things

**The idea in one line:** The mind is a thinking, non-physical substance; the body is an extended, physical substance; a human being is a union of the two.

This is perhaps Descartes' most influential and most controversial idea: **mind–body dualism**.

He argued like this. I can clearly conceive of myself as a thinking thing without a body (I was able to doubt my body while being certain of my thinking). I can clearly conceive of a body as an extended thing (taking up space) that does not think. Whatever I can clearly conceive as separate, God could create separately. So **mind** and **body** are two distinct substances:

- **Mind** (*res cogitans*, "thinking thing"): its essence is thought. It has no size, shape or location. It cannot be divided.
- **Matter** (*res extensa*, "extended thing"): its essence is extension, taking up space. It has size, shape and motion. It does not think.

A human being is a mind joined to a body. Descartes said this union is very close: I am not in my body like a pilot in a ship. If my body is injured, I don't just notice it from outside, as a pilot notices damage to the ship; I feel pain.

**The problem: how do they interact?** If the mind has no physical properties, how can it move the body? When you decide to raise your arm, a non-physical decision somehow produces physical motion. When you stub your toe, physical damage produces a non-physical feeling of pain. How?

Descartes suggested that the interaction happens in a small gland in the centre of the brain, the **pineal gland**, which he chose partly because it is single (not paired, like most brain structures) and centrally placed. Few people found this convincing. Saying *where* the interaction happens doesn't explain *how* a non-physical thing can push a physical thing. This was exactly Princess Elisabeth's challenge.

**An example.** Think about modern medicine. For centuries, Western medicine tended to treat the body as a machine, separate from the mind. Psychological factors were often ignored. Today we know that stress affects the heart, depression affects the immune system, and placebo effects show that belief can change physical outcomes. Some critics blame Descartes' sharp separation of mind and body for the earlier blind spot. The neuroscientist Antonio Damasio wrote a book called *Descartes' Error* (1994), arguing that emotions and the body are essential to reasoning, not separate from it.

**The Indian contrast.** Many Indian philosophical schools also distinguish consciousness from matter. The **Samkhya** school, for example, sharply distinguishes *purusha* (pure consciousness) from *prakriti* (matter, which includes the mind and senses). Interestingly, Samkhya places the thinking mind on the side of *matter*, with only pure witnessing consciousness on the other side, a different line from Descartes'. Meanwhile, the materialist **Charvaka** school held that consciousness arises from the body, as intoxicating power arises from fermented ingredients. The mind–body question is not only Western.

**Why it matters.** Descartes made the **mind–body problem** one of the central puzzles of philosophy. Today, as neuroscientists map the brain and engineers build artificial intelligence, the question is more urgent than ever. Is the mind simply what the brain does? Could a computer think? Is consciousness something beyond physical processes? Every position in these debates is either for or against Descartes.

### Idea 7: The world as a machine

**The idea in one line:** The physical world, including animal and human bodies, works like a vast machine, explained by matter in motion according to mathematical laws.

Descartes wanted to replace Aristotle's physics, full of purposes and qualities, with a **mechanical** physics. Everything in the physical world is just extended matter in motion. A stone falls not because it "seeks" its place, but because of how particles of matter push against it. There are no hidden purposes in nature, only mechanisms.

He applied this to living bodies too. In his *Treatise on Man*, he described the body as a machine, like the elaborate water-powered moving statues in the royal gardens. Nerves were like tubes; muscles were moved by "animal spirits" flowing through them. He gave an early description of what we now call a **reflex**: when you put your foot near a fire, the heat moves tiny particles that tug on a thread in the nerve, opening a valve in the brain that sends spirits back to move the foot away, all without thought.

And he concluded that **animals are machines**, without minds or feelings in the human sense. They do not think; they only react mechanically, like very complex clocks. Only humans, with souls, truly think, as shown by their use of language and their ability to respond flexibly to any situation.

**Why it matters.** Mechanism was enormously productive. It encouraged scientists to look for physical causes rather than mysterious purposes. Modern biology, physiology and medicine owe a great deal to this approach. Many of Descartes' specific mechanical theories were wrong, and Newton's physics replaced his, but the mechanical worldview he championed became the foundation of modern science.

The darker side is his view of animals. If animals feel no real pain, then cutting them open while alive raises no moral problem. Some of his followers performed such experiments. Today, almost no scientist accepts that animals lack feelings, and animal welfare laws, in India and elsewhere, rest on the opposite view.

**A modern twist.** Descartes said a machine could never use language flexibly or respond appropriately to every situation, as humans do. That was his test for a genuine mind. Today's AI chatbots can produce fluent language on almost any topic. Does that mean they think? Or are they very sophisticated versions of Descartes' clockwork statues? We are living inside his question.

### Idea 8: Cartesian coordinates

**The idea in one line:** Geometry and algebra can be united, so that shapes become equations and equations become shapes.

In *La Géométrie* (1637), an appendix to the *Discourse*, Descartes showed how to describe geometrical figures using algebraic equations, by locating points with numbers measured along reference lines. This is the foundation of **analytic geometry**, and the reason the x-y graph is called the **Cartesian coordinate system**. (Descartes' Latin name was Cartesius.) The French mathematician Pierre de Fermat developed similar ideas independently around the same time.

There is a popular story that Descartes got the idea while lying in bed watching a fly on the ceiling, and realised he could describe its position by its distance from two walls. It is probably a legend. But it fits a man who did his best thinking in bed.

**Why it matters.** Every time you plot a graph in school, use GPS coordinates on your phone, look at a stock chart, play a video game or see a computer design a building, you are using Descartes' idea. It made possible the calculus of Newton and Leibniz, and through it, almost all of modern physics and engineering.

### Idea 9: A temporary morality

**The idea in one line:** While you are rebuilding your beliefs, you still have to live, so adopt sensible rules for action in the meantime.

Descartes knew that you cannot stop living while you rethink everything. If you are rebuilding your house, he said, you need somewhere to stay meanwhile. So in the *Discourse*, he set out a **provisional morality** of a few maxims:

1. **Obey the laws and customs of your country**, keep the religion you were raised in, and follow the most moderate opinions of sensible people.
2. **Be firm and decisive in action**, once you have decided, even if your choice was uncertain. He gives an image: travellers lost in a forest should not wander in circles, but walk as straight as they can in one direction. Even if it's not the best direction, they will at least get somewhere better than the middle of the forest.
3. **Try to conquer yourself rather than fortune**, and to change your desires rather than the order of the world, since nothing is entirely in our power except our thoughts.

The third maxim is pure Stoicism, close to Epictetus' dichotomy of control.

**Why it matters.** The forest image is wonderful practical advice. Many people today are paralysed by choice: which course, which job, which city, which partner. Descartes says: when you cannot know for sure, decide on the best available reasons and commit. Walking straight in one direction usually beats endlessly circling.

### Idea 10: Generosity and the passions

In his last book, *The Passions of the Soul*, Descartes analysed emotions as physiological and mental events, and argued that we can learn to manage them through understanding and habit. He named one virtue as the key to all others: ***générosité***, "generosity", which for him meant a well-founded self-esteem: knowing that the only thing truly yours is the free use of your will, and being firmly resolved to use it well. A person with this virtue, he said, does not look down on others, is not envious, and is courteous and helpful, because they recognise that everyone has the same freedom of will.

This shows a warmer side of Descartes than the cold image of the "thinking thing". He was deeply interested in how to live well, not only how to know.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Plato and Aristotle: the ancients he replaced

Descartes shared a lot with **Plato**: distrust of the senses, the belief that the most important truths are grasped by reason, and the idea that some ideas, such as God and mathematical truths, are **innate**, already in the mind. In this sense, he revived a Platonic way of thinking against the Aristotelian one.

Against **Aristotle**, he was a revolutionary. Aristotle's physics of natural places, purposes and qualities had dominated universities for centuries. Descartes wanted to replace it completely with matter in motion and mathematical laws. Ironically, his own physics was in turn replaced by Newton's. But the shift he championed, from purposes to mechanisms, stuck.

### Spinoza and Leibniz: the rationalist successors

**Baruch Spinoza** learned philosophy largely from Descartes; his first published book was an exposition of Descartes' principles. But he rejected dualism. If mind and body are two completely different substances, he asked, how can they interact at all? His answer: they are not two substances, but two aspects, or "attributes", of one single substance, which he called God or Nature. This solves the interaction problem by making it disappear.

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** also accepted rationalism but criticised Descartes' physics and his account of interaction. Leibniz proposed that mind and body don't actually interact at all; God set them up at creation to run in perfect parallel, like two clocks keeping the same time (his "pre-established harmony").

Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz are often grouped together as the great **Continental rationalists**, though they disagreed fiercely with each other.

### Locke and Hume: the empiricist challenge

Across the English Channel, **John Locke** credited Descartes with first making philosophy interesting to him. But Locke rejected Descartes' innate ideas. The mind at birth, he said, is like "white paper", empty; all our ideas come from experience. Locke and his successors are called **empiricists**.

**David Hume** went further. Descartes said: I can be certain I am a thinking thing. Hume looked inside himself for this "thinking thing" and said he could never catch it. Whenever he looked, he found only particular perceptions: heat, cold, love, hatred, pain. The self, he concluded, is just a bundle of perceptions. Hume also doubted that reason could prove God's existence or the existence of the external world in the way Descartes hoped.

### Kant: the great synthesis

**Immanuel Kant**, in the eighteenth century, tried to resolve the battle between rationalists and empiricists. He agreed with Descartes that the mind brings its own structures to knowledge, and with the empiricists that knowledge requires experience. But he criticised Descartes' proofs of God and of the soul as overreaching what reason can know. Kant also pointed out that the "I think" must be able to accompany all my experiences, but that this doesn't tell us what kind of *thing* the "I" is.

### Sartre: beginning from the cogito

Three centuries later, the existentialist **Jean-Paul Sartre** said in his famous lecture *Existentialism Is a Humanism* that there can be no other truth to start from than "I think, therefore I am": the absolute truth of consciousness grasping itself. Existentialism took Descartes' starting point, the individual consciousness, but drew very different conclusions: not a soul created by God, but a free consciousness thrown into a world without given meaning.

### Ryle and the "ghost in the machine"

In 1949, the British philosopher **Gilbert Ryle** published *The Concept of Mind*, attacking Cartesian dualism as "the dogma of the Ghost in the Machine". Ryle argued that Descartes made a category mistake: treating "mind" as if it were a separate thing, like the body but invisible, rather than a way of describing how people behave and are disposed to behave. Ryle's phrase became the most famous criticism of Descartes in the twentieth century.

### Neuroscience and AI

Today, most neuroscientists and many philosophers reject substance dualism: they think the mind is what the brain does, not a separate substance. But the **"hard problem of consciousness"**, a phrase coined by the philosopher David Chalmers in the 1990s, asks why physical brain processes are accompanied by subjective experience at all: why it *feels like* something to see red or taste mango. This problem keeps Descartes' question alive. And every debate about whether an AI is "really" conscious, or only behaving as if it were, is a debate he started.

## See it around you

**Deepfakes and misinformation.** In an age where images, videos and voices can be faked, Descartes' first rule, accept nothing as true unless you clearly know it to be so, is not an abstract principle but a survival skill.

**The Matrix and simulation theories.** Films, video games and debates about whether we live in a simulation are all versions of the evil demon. Descartes asked the question; Hollywood made it visual.

**School mathematics.** Every graph you draw, every equation of a line or curve, is Cartesian geometry.

**GPS and maps.** Your phone locates you with coordinates: Descartes' idea applied to the whole planet.

**Medicine and mental health.** The old habit of treating the body as a machine separate from the mind is fading, as doctors recognise the role of stress, emotion and belief in health. This is partly a correction of Cartesian dualism.

**AI and consciousness.** Every time someone asks whether a chatbot "understands", or whether machines could ever feel, they are standing in Descartes' study, wondering what makes a mind different from a machine.

**Animal rights.** The rejection of Descartes' view that animals are unfeeling machines underlies modern animal welfare laws, debates about factory farming, and campaigns against cruelty.

**Decision paralysis.** The traveller lost in the forest: when you can't decide between options, pick the best one on the evidence you have and walk straight.

**Exams and problem-solving.** Breaking a hard problem into parts, starting with the simplest, and checking everything at the end: Descartes' four rules are taught (without his name) in every good maths class.

## Use it

**1. The apple basket.** Once in a while, take one important belief, about politics, health, religion, or yourself, and examine it as if you were sorting apples. Is it based on evidence, or on habit, authority or emotion? Put it back only if it survives.

**2. Apply rule one to information.** Before you share a video, a message or a "fact", ask: do I clearly know this is true, or do I just want it to be? If you can't verify it, don't pass it on.

**3. Break it down.** When facing a big problem, write down its parts. Solve the simplest first. Climb step by step. Review at the end to check nothing was missed.

**4. Walk straight out of the forest.** When stuck between choices with no clear winner, choose the best on the information you have and commit for a reasonable time. Constant switching keeps you lost.

**5. The cogito moment.** When anxiety makes everything feel uncertain, notice the one thing you can be sure of: you are here, aware, thinking. Start from there. Some people find this simple act of noticing calming.

**6. Change your desires, not the world.** When something is beyond your control, practise Descartes' third maxim: adjust what you want rather than demanding that the world change.

**7. Question the machine.** When you interact with AI, ask yourself Descartes' question: is this understanding, or a very clever mechanism? What would count as evidence either way?

**8. Write for everyone.** Descartes wrote in French so that anyone with good sense could judge his ideas. When you explain something, use language that ordinary people can follow. Clarity is a form of respect.

## Where he went wrong

**1. The mind–body problem remains unsolved.** Descartes never explained how a non-physical mind can affect a physical body. The pineal gland was an unsatisfying answer. Most scientists today think the mind depends entirely on the brain, which suggests his sharp dualism was a mistake.

**2. The Cartesian circle.** His argument for trusting clear and distinct ideas depends on God, and his argument for God depends on clear and distinct ideas. Many philosophers think this is circular.

**3. His proofs of God don't convince most people.** The trademark and ontological arguments have been criticised for centuries, by Kant among many others. Without God, Descartes' bridge from the thinking self back to the world collapses.

**4. Animals do feel.** His view that animals are unfeeling machines was not only wrong but harmful, used to justify cruelty. Modern science overwhelmingly shows that many animals feel pain and have rich inner lives.

**5. Too much isolation of the individual mind.** Descartes started from a lone thinker in a room. But we learn language, concepts and even ways of thinking from others. Later philosophers, from Hegel to Wittgenstein, argued that the mind is social from the start. You could not even formulate "I think, therefore I am" without a language learned from other people.

**6. Reason without emotion.** Descartes treated reason as the core of the self and the body as a machine. Modern psychology and neuroscience suggest that emotion, body and social context are deeply involved in reasoning, not obstacles to it.

**7. His physics was wrong.** Descartes' mechanical physics, including his theory that the planets are carried around by whirlpools (vortices) of invisible matter, was overturned by Newton. His method outlived his conclusions.

**8. Caution before authority.** Descartes hid *The World* after Galileo's trial, and his provisional morality advised obeying the laws and religion of one's country. Some readers see this as wise prudence; others as a reminder that even the champion of independent thought bowed to power when it mattered.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you probably took it for granted that what you see is real, that your beliefs rest on solid ground, and that your mind and body are simply "you", one thing.

**Now,** you can see that almost everything you believe could, in principle, be doubted, and that there is one thing that can't: the fact that you are here, aware, thinking. You can see how much depends on the gap between appearance and reality, and how hard it is to bridge. And you can see that the relationship between your mind and your body, between thought and brain, is one of the deepest mysteries we have.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A viral video** stops being automatically "proof". It becomes something to examine, like an apple from the basket.
- **A dream** becomes a small philosophical puzzle: how did I know, then, that it wasn't real?
- **A graph in a textbook** carries the name of a man who did his best thinking lying in bed.
- **A conversation with an AI** raises a question that a seventeenth-century Frenchman asked first: is this a mind, or a very clever machine?
- **A hard problem** becomes a set of smaller problems, ordered from simplest to hardest.
- **A hard choice** becomes a forest you can walk out of, if you pick a direction and keep going.
- **Your own awareness**, right now, reading this sentence, becomes the one certainty under everything else.

Descartes tore everything down to find one solid stone. He found it in the simplest place of all: the fact of his own thinking. You are standing on that same stone right now.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** René Descartes (1596–1650), French philosopher, mathematician and scientist; educated by Jesuits, lived mostly in the Dutch Republic, died in Stockholm.
- **Context:** old certainties were collapsing under the Scientific Revolution, the Reformation and revived scepticism; universities still taught Aristotle.
- **Method:** four rules: accept only what is clear and certain, divide problems, proceed from simple to complex, review everything.
- **Methodical doubt:** senses deceive; we might be dreaming; an evil demon might deceive us about everything.
- **Cogito:** "I think, therefore I am", the one certainty that survives all doubt; I am a thinking thing.
- **God and knowledge:** a perfect God would not deceive us, so clear and distinct perceptions are true (critics call this circular).
- **Dualism:** mind (thinking, non-physical) and body (extended, physical) are distinct substances; how they interact remains a puzzle.
- **Science and mathematics:** mechanical view of nature; animals as machines; Cartesian coordinates and analytic geometry.
- **Legacy and critics:** influenced Spinoza, Leibniz, Kant and Sartre; challenged by Locke, Hume, Ryle and modern neuroscience.

**Key terms**

- **Rationalism:** the view that reason is the primary source of knowledge.
- **Methodical doubt:** doubting everything possible in order to find what is certain.
- **Cogito, ergo sum:** "I think, therefore I am."
- **Evil demon:** Descartes' imagined deceiver, used to push doubt to its limit.
- **Clear and distinct ideas:** ideas so evident that they cannot be doubted.
- **Dualism:** the view that mind and body are two different kinds of substance.
- **Res cogitans / res extensa:** thinking substance / extended substance.
- **Pineal gland:** the brain structure Descartes proposed as the meeting point of mind and body.
- **Cartesian circle:** the objection that Descartes' reasoning about God and clear ideas is circular.
- **Cartesian coordinates:** the x-y system that turns geometry into algebra.
