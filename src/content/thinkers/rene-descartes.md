## Life

René Descartes was born in 1596 in La Haye, in the Touraine region of France; the town is now named Descartes in his honour. His mother died when he was an infant. He was educated at the Jesuit college of La Flèche, one of the best schools in Europe, and later took a law degree at Poitiers. He finished his schooling, he later wrote, convinced that much of what he had been taught was uncertain. Only mathematics seemed to offer real knowledge.

Resolving to seek knowledge in "the great book of the world", he joined the army of Maurice of Nassau in the Netherlands in 1618. There he met the scientist Isaac Beeckman, who rekindled his interest in mathematics and physics. On the night of 10 November 1619, shut in a stove-heated room in Germany during winter quarters, he had a series of vivid dreams that he took as a calling to found a unified science on mathematical certainty.

In 1628 he settled in the Dutch Republic, where he lived for most of the next two decades, moving house often to protect his privacy. He completed a treatise on physics, *The World*, but suppressed it when he heard of Galileo's condemnation in 1633, since it also placed the sun at the centre. In 1637 he published the *Discourse on the Method* with essays on optics, meteorology and geometry. The *Meditations* followed in 1641, together with objections from leading thinkers, including Hobbes, Gassendi and Arnauld, and his replies. He had a daughter, Francine, whose death at the age of five in 1640 grieved him deeply. His long correspondence with Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia pressed him on the hardest questions about mind and body.

In 1649 Queen Christina of Sweden invited him to Stockholm to instruct her in philosophy. The lessons reportedly began at five in the morning in the northern winter. Within months Descartes caught pneumonia, and he died in February 1650.

## Key ideas

**The method of doubt.** To build knowledge on firm foundations, Descartes resolved to doubt everything that could possibly be doubted. The senses sometimes deceive; we might be dreaming; an all-powerful "evil demon" might be deceiving us even about mathematics.

**The cogito.** One thing survives even the most radical doubt: in the very act of doubting, I must exist. *Je pense, donc je suis*, in Latin *cogito, ergo sum*: "I think, therefore I am." This certainty of one's own thinking existence became the first principle of his philosophy.

**Clear and distinct ideas, and God.** From the cogito Descartes argued to the existence of a perfect God, who would not deceive us. Whatever we perceive "clearly and distinctly" must therefore be true, and this restores our confidence in mathematics and in the existence of the physical world.

**Mind–body dualism.** Reality consists of two fundamentally different substances: **mind**, whose essence is thinking and which has no extension, and **matter**, whose essence is extension in space and which does not think. The human being is a union of the two. But how can an unextended mind move an extended body? Descartes suggested the pineal gland as their point of interaction, an answer few found satisfying.

**A mechanical universe.** The physical world, including animal and human bodies, is a vast machine explained by matter in motion according to mathematical laws. Descartes regarded animals as complex automata without minds.

**Analytic geometry.** In *La Géométrie* he united algebra and geometry by describing curves with equations, the basis of the "Cartesian" coordinate system.

## Influence and legacy

Descartes is often called the father of modern philosophy because he made the question "What can I know, and how?" its starting point, and because he began from the individual thinking subject. Spinoza, Leibniz, Locke and Kant all wrote in response to him. His mechanistic physics prepared the way for Newton, though it was largely superseded.

The "mind–body problem" he bequeathed remains at the centre of philosophy of mind. In 1949 Gilbert Ryle mocked Cartesian dualism as "the dogma of the Ghost in the Machine", and debates in neuroscience and artificial intelligence still return to the questions Descartes posed.
