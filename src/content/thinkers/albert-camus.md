Think about your typical day. Wake up. Brush. Commute: a crowded bus, a local train, a traffic jam. Work or college for eight hours. Commute back. Eat. Scroll. Sleep. Then wake up and do it all again. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday. And again next week. And next year.

Most of the time, we don't notice. But sometimes, on an ordinary day, in a traffic jam or a queue, a question rises up out of nowhere: **why?** What is all this for? Is there any point?

A French writer born in Algeria described this moment perfectly. "It happens that the stage sets collapse," he wrote. "Rising, streetcar, four hours in the office or factory, meal, streetcar, four hours of work, meal, sleep, and Monday Tuesday Wednesday Thursday Friday and Saturday according to the same rhythm... But one day the 'why' arises." (paraphrase of a well-known passage)

He had a name for the feeling that follows: **the absurd**. We are creatures who long for meaning, for clarity, for some reason behind it all. And the universe answers with silence.

What do you do then? Many people find answers in religion. Others find them in politics, career, family. Some give up. **Albert Camus** asked whether there was another way: to face the silence honestly, without false comfort, and still choose to live, fully, passionately, and with care for others. He found his answer in an old Greek myth about a man condemned to push a rock up a hill forever. And he concluded: **"One must imagine Sisyphus happy."**

Camus grew up in poverty, nearly died of tuberculosis at seventeen, edited a secret Resistance newspaper under Nazi occupation, wrote some of the most read novels of the twentieth century, won the Nobel Prize at forty-four, and died in a car crash at forty-six. People often call him an existentialist. He said he wasn't. By the end of this page, you'll know why, and you'll have a way of facing the "why" that doesn't require you to pretend.

## The world he was born into

### French Algeria

Camus was born in **1913** in **French Algeria**, in North Africa. France had conquered Algeria in **1830** and treated it not as a colony but as part of France itself. About a million European settlers lived there, called **pieds-noirs** ("black feet"): French, Spanish, Italian and Maltese, many of them poor workers. They lived alongside about eight million **Arab and Berber Muslims**, who were denied equal rights in their own land.

Camus grew up as one of the poor whites, in a world of sun, sea and injustice. This double inheritance, love of the Algerian land and its light, and the unresolved wrong of colonialism, would shape his whole life.

### A world at war

Camus's lifetime was the age of catastrophe. The **First World War** killed his father before he was one year old. The **Great Depression** of the 1930s brought mass unemployment. **Fascism** rose in Italy, Germany and Spain. The **Second World War** brought the Nazi occupation of France, the Holocaust and the atomic bomb. After the war came the **Cold War**, with intellectuals forced to choose between the American and Soviet camps, and wars of independence across the colonised world, including Algeria's.

### The death of God

Philosophically, Camus inherited a crisis. **Nietzsche** had announced that "God is dead": educated Europeans no longer believed in a God who gave the world meaning. And nothing had replaced him. Science explained how things worked, but not why we should live. The great ideologies, communism and fascism, promised meaning through history, and delivered mass murder.

This was the world in which Camus asked: if there is no God and no meaning built into the universe, how should we live?

## The life, as a story

### The father he never knew

Albert Camus was born on **7 November 1913** in **Mondovi** (now Dréan), in eastern Algeria. His father, **Lucien**, a farm worker, was called up when the First World War began and was killed at the **Battle of the Marne** in **1914**. Albert was less than a year old. All he had of his father was a photograph, a piece of shrapnel and one story: that his father had once gone to watch a public execution, and came home and vomited.

His mother, **Catherine**, was partly deaf, could not read or write, and worked as a cleaning woman. She took her two sons to live with her mother, a harsh, domineering woman, in a small flat with no running water and no electricity in **Belcourt**, a poor working-class district of **Algiers**. Camus later wrote about his mother's silence: she rarely spoke, rarely showed affection, yet he loved her deeply.

### The teacher who changed everything

At primary school, a teacher named **Louis Germain** noticed the boy's brilliance. He gave him extra lessons for free and persuaded his grandmother, who wanted Albert to start earning money, to let him take the exam for a scholarship to the **lycée** (high school). He won it.

Thirty-three years later, the day after he heard he had won the Nobel Prize, Camus wrote to Germain: "Without you, without the affectionate hand you extended to the poor child that I was, without your teaching and example, none of all this would have happened." He dedicated his Nobel speech to him.

### The goalkeeper

At school, Camus loved two things above all: football and the sea. He was a talented **goalkeeper** for the junior team of the Racing Universitaire d'Alger. He chose goalkeeping, he joked, because his grandmother said running around wore out his shoes. Years later, he is reported to have said that what he knew most surely about morality and the duties of human beings, he owed to football.

At **seventeen**, he coughed blood. It was **tuberculosis**, then often fatal. He survived, but his football days were over, and the disease returned throughout his life. Facing death so young gave him an intense sense of the value of life, the body, the sun, here and now.

### Student, actor, communist, journalist

He studied philosophy at the University of Algiers, and wrote his thesis (1936) on how Christianity absorbed Greek philosophy, through **Plotinus** and **Augustine**. His illness kept him from becoming a teacher, since the state exams required a medical certificate.

He founded a theatre company, acting, directing and writing plays. He joined the **Communist Party** in **1935**, because it worked among Arabs and Berbers, but was expelled in **1937** when the party's line changed and it stopped supporting Algerian nationalists.

As a young journalist for the newspaper *Alger Républicain*, he wrote a famous series of reports in **1939** on the **famine in Kabylie**, a mountain region where Berber families were starving while the colonial government did nothing. The colonial authorities came to see him as a troublemaker. When the newspaper was shut down soon afterwards, no one in Algiers would hire him, and he had to leave Algeria to find work.

### Paris, occupation and the absurd

He moved to Paris in **1940**, just before the German army invaded. In **1942**, during the Nazi occupation, he published ***The Stranger*** (*L'Étranger*) and ***The Myth of Sisyphus***. *The Stranger* begins with one of the most famous lines in modern fiction: "Mother died today. Or maybe yesterday, I don't know." Its hero, **Meursault**, kills an Arab man on a beach for no clear reason, and is condemned to death, less for the murder than because he did not cry at his mother's funeral.

### Combat

In **1943**, he joined the **Resistance** and became editor of its underground newspaper, ***Combat***. Writing it was dangerous: discovery meant arrest, torture and death. When Paris was liberated in **August 1944**, *Combat* came out into the open, and Camus's editorials made him the moral voice of a generation. He was thirty.

When the United States dropped the atomic bomb on **Hiroshima** in **August 1945**, almost every French newspaper celebrated. Camus wrote that "mechanical civilisation has just reached its final degree of savagery".

### The Plague and the break with Sartre

In **1947**, he published ***The Plague***, a novel about an epidemic that seals off the Algerian city of Oran. It was read as an allegory of the Nazi occupation, and became a best-seller.

He had become friends with **Jean-Paul Sartre** and **Simone de Beauvoir** during the war. But they drifted apart over politics. Sartre moved closer to the Soviet Union; Camus attacked Soviet labour camps and the idea that history could justify murder. In **1951**, he published ***The Rebel***. Sartre's magazine published a savage review. Camus replied angrily; Sartre replied even more savagely, in **1952**. They never spoke again. The quarrel divided French intellectuals for a generation.

### Nobel

In **1957**, at just **forty-four**, Camus won the **Nobel Prize in Literature**. He was one of the youngest writers ever to receive it. He was not sure he deserved it, and said the prize should have gone to the novelist André Malraux.

### Algeria

By then, Algeria was at war. In **1954**, the Algerian National Liberation Front (FLN) began an armed struggle for independence. The French army responded with torture and mass repression; the FLN with bombings that killed civilians.

Camus was torn. He had fought for Arab rights all his life. But he could not accept Algerian independence that would expel the pieds-noirs, his own people, his mother's people. He called for a civilian truce to protect civilians on both sides and was shouted down. Then he fell publicly silent, while quietly intervening to save condemned Algerian prisoners from execution.

In Stockholm, a student challenged him about Algeria. Camus answered (in words widely misquoted ever since) that bombs were being planted in the trams of Algiers; his mother might be on one of those trams; if that was justice, he preferred his mother. Many on the left never forgave him.

### The unused ticket

On **4 January 1960**, Camus was driving back to Paris from Provence with his publisher's nephew, **Michel Gallimard**. The car hit a tree. Camus was killed instantly. He was **forty-six**.

In his pocket was an unused **train ticket**: he had planned to go by train, but accepted a lift at the last minute. In his bag was the handwritten manuscript of an unfinished novel, ***The First Man***, about a boy growing up poor in Algiers, searching for the father he never knew. It was published in 1994.

He is said to have remarked once that he could imagine nothing more absurd than dying in a car accident.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: The absurd

**The idea in one line:** The absurd is the clash between our deep need for meaning and the silent, indifferent universe that does not answer.

Camus is careful here. The world itself is not absurd; it simply **is**. Human beings are not absurd either; they simply long for meaning. The **absurd** is the **confrontation** between the two: "the confrontation between the human need and the unreasonable silence of the world".

We want the world to make sense, to be fair, to have a purpose. We want to know why we are here, why we suffer, why we die. The universe offers no answer. Good people suffer; bad people prosper; a child dies of illness; an earthquake destroys a village. There is no explanation that satisfies the heart.

The *Mahabharata* has a famous passage in which a spirit asks **Yudhishthira**: "What is the greatest wonder?" He answers: "Every day people die, and yet the living go on as if they will live forever." That is the absurd, seen from another angle: we know we will die, and we live as though we won't.

**Why it matters:** Camus gave a name to a feeling that millions of people experience but often cannot describe: the sudden sense that daily life is mechanical and meaningless. Naming it is the first step to facing it.

**A common misunderstanding:** The absurd is not a reason for despair. For Camus, it is the honest starting point: the truth we must face before we can live freely.

### Idea 2: Three responses, and the one that is right

**The idea in one line:** Faced with the absurd, we can kill ourselves, escape into a comforting belief, or live with it, and only the third is honest.

*The Myth of Sisyphus* begins with a shocking sentence: **"There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide."** Judging whether life is worth living, Camus says, is the fundamental question of philosophy.

He considers three responses to the absurd:

1. **Physical suicide.** If life has no meaning, why live? Camus rejects this. Suicide does not solve the absurd; it abolishes one side of it, the human being. It is a surrender, not an answer.
2. **"Philosophical suicide".** This means escaping the absurd by leaping into a belief that explains everything: God, an afterlife, or a political ideology that promises a perfect future. Camus thought **Kierkegaard** and other religious existentialists did this: they saw the absurd clearly, then jumped over it into faith. It is understandable, he said, but it is an escape.
3. **Revolt.** The third way is to live **with** the absurd: to keep it in view, refuse both despair and false hope, and live as fully as possible. Camus calls this attitude **revolt**, and the freedom and **passion** it brings.

**Why it matters:** Camus argued that a life without ultimate meaning is still worth living, and perhaps worth living more intensely, because it is all we have.

**A common misunderstanding:** Camus is not encouraging anyone to consider suicide. The opposite: his whole book is an argument **against** it. If you are in that place, please reach out for help. Camus's message is that life, even without guarantees, is worth living.

### Idea 3: One must imagine Sisyphus happy

**The idea in one line:** Even an endless, pointless task can be lived with dignity and joy, if we accept it consciously and make it our own.

In Greek myth, **Sisyphus** was a king who cheated death. As punishment, the gods condemned him to push a huge rock up a mountain, only to watch it roll back down, again and again, forever. The gods thought there was no worse punishment than futile and hopeless labour.

Camus turns the story around. The interesting moment, he says, is when Sisyphus walks back down the mountain to start again. In that moment, he is **conscious**. He knows the full extent of his fate. And by knowing it, by refusing to be crushed by it, he rises above it. "There is no fate that cannot be surmounted by scorn."

Then comes his famous conclusion: **"The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy."**

Think of a Mumbai local train commuter, a farmer who plants every season not knowing whether the monsoon will come, a nurse on endless night shifts, a mother cooking the same meals for decades. Their tasks never end. Camus asks: can they find joy and dignity in the struggle itself, without waiting for a final reward?

The ***Bhagavad Gita*** offers a related teaching: "You have a right to your action, but never to its fruits." Do your work fully, without being attached to the result. The Gita grounds this in a cosmic order; Camus grounds it in nothing but human courage. But both find freedom in the act itself.

**Why it matters:** Most of life consists of repetition: work, chores, commutes, routines. Camus offers a way to live it without despair, by finding meaning in the effort, not the outcome.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Imagine Sisyphus happy" is not a call to accept injustice or exploitation, or to be content with any situation. It is about our attitude to the human condition itself. Against injustice, Camus demanded revolt.

### Idea 4: Live fully, here and now

**The idea in one line:** If there is no life after this one, then this life, its sun, sea, body, friendship and love, is everything, and should be lived with passion.

Camus was a writer of **the sun**. He grew up on the beaches of Algiers, swimming in the Mediterranean, and some of his most beautiful essays are hymns to the physical world: the smell of wild herbs among Roman ruins at **Tipasa**, the light on the sea, the taste of a cold drink after a swim.

If there is no heaven, he says, the earth is all we have. So instead of living for a future reward, or a future revolution, live **now**: "The present and the succession of presents before a constantly conscious soul is the ideal of the absurd man." Quantity and intensity of experience matter more than waiting for some later meaning.

And in the darkest times, he found strength there. In the winter of his own life, he wrote: **"In the midst of winter, I found there was, within me, an invincible summer."**

**Why it matters:** Many people postpone life: after the exams, after the promotion, after the children grow up, after retirement. Camus reminds us that life is now.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Living fully" does not mean selfish pleasure-seeking. For Camus, passion for life comes with responsibility to other living people, because they share the same fate.

### Idea 5: I rebel, therefore we exist

**The idea in one line:** Revolt against injustice begins when one person says "no", and in that "no" discovers a value shared with all human beings.

In *The Rebel* (1951), Camus moves from the individual facing the absurd to people facing injustice together.

What is a rebel? "A man who says no." A slave who has obeyed orders all his life suddenly refuses one more command. In that refusal, he says: there is a limit; you cannot go this far. And in saying it, he affirms that something in him is worth defending, something that belongs not just to him but to all human beings. That is why, Camus says, the rebel discovers **solidarity**.

Descartes said: "I think, therefore I am." Camus says: **"I rebel, therefore we exist."**

**Why it matters:** This explains why protests against injustice so often unite strangers. When one person refuses, it reveals a shared dignity. Think of the women of the **Chipko** movement hugging trees in the Himalayas, or **Rosa Parks** refusing to give up her bus seat: one "no" revealing a "we".

**A common misunderstanding:** Rebellion for Camus is not the same as revolution. That difference is his next and most important idea.

### Idea 6: Rebellion with limits

**The idea in one line:** True rebellion respects the human dignity it defends; when it kills for an abstract future, it becomes a new tyranny.

Camus's great argument in *The Rebel* is that **revolutions** tend to betray the rebellion they came from. The rebel says no to injustice in the name of human dignity. But the revolutionary, convinced that history is moving towards a perfect society, decides that any means are justified to get there, including killing innocent people, lying, and building prison camps. The French Revolution led to the Terror; the Russian Revolution led to Stalin's labour camps.

Camus argued that rebellion must have **limits**. Ends do not justify all means. No future utopia can justify murdering living people today.

He dramatised this in his play ***The Just Assassins*** (1949), based on real Russian revolutionaries in **1905**. The young poet **Kaliayev** is supposed to throw a bomb at the Grand Duke's carriage. When the moment comes, he sees two **children** in the carriage, and cannot throw it. His comrades argue: what are two children compared to the millions who will be freed? Kaliayev answers that if the revolution must kill children, it is not worth having.

India's freedom struggle faced the same question. **Gandhi** argued that impure means corrupt the end. **Bhagat Singh** and his comrades threw bombs into the Central Legislative Assembly in **1929** deliberately where no one would be killed, to make the "deaf hear", and then gave themselves up, though their movement had also killed a British police officer, John Saunders, in 1928. The argument about which means are acceptable, and why, is Camus's argument.

**Why it matters:** This is perhaps Camus's most important political idea. It applies wherever people use violence, censorship or cruelty in the name of a good cause.

**A common misunderstanding:** Camus was not a pacifist, and he did not oppose all struggle. He fought in the Resistance against the Nazis. He opposed the idea that history or ideology can justify unlimited violence, especially against innocents.

### Idea 7: The plague and common decency

**The idea in one line:** Against the "plagues" of life, disease, tyranny, cruelty, the answer is not heroism or ideology but ordinary people doing their duty together.

In ***The Plague***, the city of Oran is struck by an epidemic. The gates are closed. People are trapped with their fear, their separation from loved ones, and death.

Different characters respond in different ways. A priest preaches that the plague is God's punishment for sin, until he watches an innocent child die in agony. A smuggler profits from the crisis. A journalist tries to escape to his lover. And a doctor, **Rieux**, simply keeps treating patients, day after day, though he knows he cannot win. When asked why, he explains that it is not about heroism. "It's a matter of common decency," he says. Decency means doing your job.

Another character, **Tarrou**, organises volunteer sanitary squads. His goal in life is to be "a saint without God": to refuse to be on the side of the plague, of anything that kills or justifies killing.

The novel ends with the plague retreating, and a warning: the plague bacillus never dies. It waits, and one day it will return.

**Why it matters:** When **COVID-19** struck in **2020**, sales of *The Plague* soared around the world. People recognised Oran in their own locked-down cities: the doctors and nurses on endless shifts, the sanitation workers and delivery workers who kept cities running, the migrant workers walking hundreds of kilometres home, the profiteers, the preachers of blame, and the ordinary people doing ordinary acts of decency.

**A common misunderstanding:** *The Plague* is not mainly about disease. The plague stands for every evil that falls on people: occupation, war, tyranny, cruelty. And the answer is the same: solidarity, honesty, and doing what must be done.

### Idea 8: Against the death penalty

**The idea in one line:** No state should kill a human being in cold blood, because no one has the absolute innocence to judge absolutely.

Camus fought against capital punishment all his life. In ***Reflections on the Guillotine*** (1957), he argued that the death penalty does not deter crime, is often applied to the poor, is irreversible when mistakes are made, and turns society itself into a calculated killer. A murderer kills in passion; the state kills after months of deliberate planning. "Capital punishment," he wrote, is the most premeditated of murders.

He remembered the story of his father, who went to see an execution and came home and vomited.

**Why it matters:** Debates about the death penalty continue around the world, including in India, where the Supreme Court has said it should be used only in the "rarest of rare" cases. Camus's arguments are still central to those debates.

**A common misunderstanding:** Camus did not argue that murderers should go unpunished. He argued that the state should not kill.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Nietzsche: life without God

**Nietzsche** announced the death of God and called on human beings to say "yes" to life, even with its suffering. Camus learned deeply from him. But he warned that Nietzsche's ideas, misused, could lead to nihilism and the worship of power, as they did among the Nazis. Camus wanted the yes to life without the contempt for the weak.

### Kierkegaard: the leap

**Kierkegaard** saw the absurdity of life as clearly as anyone. But he answered it with a **leap of faith** into belief in God. Camus called this "philosophical suicide": giving up the clear-eyed struggle with the absurd for a comforting answer. Kierkegaard might reply that faith is not comfort but the hardest passion of all.

### Augustine: the North African ancestor

Camus wrote his university thesis on how Christian thought, especially **Augustine**'s, absorbed Greek philosophy. Augustine was also a North African, a lover of life and pleasure, honest about suffering. But where Augustine's restless heart found rest in God, Camus refused to rest: he chose to stay with the restlessness. He once said he felt closer to the Greeks than to Christianity, but he never stopped arguing with it.

### Sartre: friendship and quarrel

**Jean-Paul Sartre** and Camus were friends and fellow celebrities after the war, and both were called existentialists. But Camus rejected the label: "No, I am not an existentialist," he said in a 1945 interview. Their break in 1952 was about politics. Imagine them in conversation:

*Sartre:* You condemn the Soviet camps. But you give comfort to the capitalists and colonialists. History must be made, and you cannot keep your hands clean.

*Camus:* I will not justify murder in the name of a future no one has seen.

*Sartre:* Then you are a moralist sitting outside history.

*Camus:* And you are a philosopher who has made history into a god.

Many later readers, especially after the Soviet camps were fully revealed, came to feel that Camus had been right.

### Marx: the revolution and its limits

Camus respected **Marx**'s anger at injustice and poverty, which he had known firsthand. But he believed that Marxism's faith in history, the certainty that the future classless society justified any present sacrifice, led directly to the camps. He wanted justice for the poor, without the murder of the present for the sake of the future.

### Later voices

- **Hannah Arendt**, in a 1952 letter, called him "the best man now in France".
- **Edward Said**, the Palestinian-American critic, argued that Camus's novels erase Arabs: in *The Stranger*, the murdered man has no name.
- In **2013**, the Algerian writer **Kamel Daoud** wrote *The Meursault Investigation*, retelling *The Stranger* from the point of view of the murdered Arab's brother.

**Repetitive work.** The commuter, the factory worker, the homemaker, the gig worker doing the same tasks again and again. Can they be Sisyphus happy, and should society also make their burdens lighter?

**Violence in the name of a cause.** Whenever a movement justifies attacks on civilians for a future goal, Kaliayev's question returns: if it must kill children, is it worth having?

**Death penalty debates.** Every time a court sentences someone to death, and public outrage demands it, Camus's argument is heard.

**Colonial blind spots.** The nameless Arab in *The Stranger* reminds us how even sympathetic writers can fail to see the colonised as full human beings.

**Small refusals.** The employee who refuses to sign a false report; the citizen who refuses to join a lynch mob. "I rebel, therefore we exist."

## Use it

**1. Name the absurd.** When the "why" arises, don't run from it. Sit with it. Recognise that the longing for meaning is human and the silence of the universe is real.

**2. Don't make your life's value depend on one result.** An exam, a job, a relationship, a match. Life is bigger than any outcome.

**3. Find joy in the effort.** Choose one repetitive task in your life and try to do it consciously and well, for its own sake.

**4. Live now.** Don't postpone joy to "after". Notice the sun, the sea, the food, the friend, today.

**5. Practise common decency.** In a crisis, at work, in your neighbourhood, don't wait for heroes. Do your part.

**6. Set limits on your means.** For any cause you believe in, ask: what will I not do, even for this? Where is my line?

**7. Say no when it matters.** When asked to join an injustice, even a small one, remember that one "no" can reveal a "we".

**8. Seek your invincible summer.** In hard times, look for the inner warmth, a memory, a love, a passion, that winter can't reach.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Algeria's blind spot.** Camus fought for Arab rights but could not accept full Algerian independence. Critics argue that, as a pied-noir, he could not see past his own community's interests, and that his novels largely leave Arabs nameless and silent.

**2. Philosophy as mood.** Professional philosophers have criticised Camus for lacking rigour: his arguments are often images and metaphors, not careful reasoning. Sartre accused him of being a moralist rather than a thinker.

**3. Was the leap really suicide?** Religious thinkers argue that Camus misread Kierkegaard: faith is not an escape from the absurd but a passionate way of living with it.

**4. Neutrality can serve injustice.** In the Algerian war, Camus's call for a truce and his later silence were seen by many as a refusal to take sides in a struggle against colonial oppression.

**5. Private life.** He was repeatedly unfaithful to his wife, Francine, who suffered serious depression. The man who wrote so movingly about solidarity could be careless of those closest to him.

**6. Can Sisyphus be happy if he is exploited?** Critics on the left argue that "imagine Sisyphus happy" can be used to make workers accept miserable conditions. Camus would answer that revolt against injustice is also part of his philosophy, but the danger is real.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought that life must have a ready-made meaning to be worth living, that feeling "what is the point?" is a sign that something is wrong with you, and that fighting for a good cause justifies any means. You may have known Camus as a name associated with existentialism and gloomy French novels.

**Now,** you can see the absurd as an honest meeting between your longing for meaning and a silent world. You can see a third way between despair and false comfort: to live fully, consciously, with passion and solidarity. You can see Sisyphus walking down the mountain, aware and unbroken. You can see rebellion as a "no" that reveals a "we", and the limits that keep rebellion from becoming tyranny. And you can see his blind spots: the nameless Arab, the unresolved colonial wrong.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **The Monday commute** becomes a mountain you can climb with open eyes.
- **A failure** no longer measures the worth of your life.
- **A cause you love** comes with a question: what won't I do for it?
- **A crisis** calls for common decency, not heroes.
- **A moment of sunlight** is everything, because it is now.
- **A single "no"** can be the start of solidarity.

A poor boy from Algiers, raised by a silent mother, who almost died at seventeen, looked into the silence of the universe and refused both despair and illusion. He found, in the midst of winter, an invincible summer, and he asked us to imagine Sisyphus happy.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Albert Camus (1913–1960), French-Algerian novelist, essayist, playwright and journalist; Nobel Prize in Literature (1957).
- **Context:** French Algeria and colonialism; the world wars; Nazi occupation; the Cold War; the Algerian war of independence.
- **Life:** father killed in 1914; raised in poverty in Algiers; teacher Louis Germain; goalkeeper; tuberculosis at seventeen; thesis on Augustine and Plotinus; Communist Party (1935–37); Kabylie famine reports (1939); *The Stranger* and *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942); editor of *Combat* in the Resistance; *The Plague* (1947); *The Rebel* (1951) and break with Sartre (1952); Nobel (1957); killed in a car crash (1960).
- **The absurd:** the clash between our need for meaning and the silent world.
- **Three responses:** suicide, philosophical suicide, revolt; only revolt is honest.
- **Sisyphus:** "one must imagine Sisyphus happy".
- **Live fully now:** the invincible summer.
- **Rebellion:** "I rebel, therefore we exist."
- **Limits:** ends don't justify all means; *The Just Assassins*.
- **The Plague:** common decency and solidarity.
- **Death penalty:** the state must not kill.
- **Criticisms:** Algerian blind spot, lack of rigour, misreading Kierkegaard, neutrality, private life, misuse of Sisyphus.

**Key terms**

- **The absurd:** the confrontation between the human need for meaning and the silence of the world.
- **Philosophical suicide:** escaping the absurd by leaping into a comforting belief.
- **Revolt:** living with the absurd consciously, refusing both despair and false hope.
- **Sisyphus:** the Greek figure condemned to roll a rock uphill forever, Camus's model of the absurd hero.
- **The rebel:** one who says "no" to injustice and discovers solidarity.
- **Pieds-noirs:** European settlers in French Algeria.
- **Limits:** the moral boundaries that rebellion must not cross, even for a good end.
- **Common decency:** Dr Rieux's word for simply doing one's duty against the plague.
