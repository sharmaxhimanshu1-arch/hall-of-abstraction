## Start here

Imagine a chicken on a farm.

Every morning, the farmer comes out, opens the gate and throws grain. Day after day. A hundred days. Two hundred. The chicken learns: when the farmer comes, food arrives. If chickens could reason, this one would be very confident: "The farmer is my friend. Tomorrow he will come and feed me. He always does."

Then one morning the farmer comes out, picks the chicken up, and wrings its neck.

(This story was told by the philosopher Bertrand Russell in 1912, to illustrate an idea that was first set out, sharply and unforgettably, by a Scotsman nearly two hundred years earlier.)

Now let's turn it on ourselves. You are completely sure the sun will rise tomorrow. You are sure that if you drop your phone, it will fall, not float. You are sure that bread will nourish you and not poison you, that water boils when heated, that the medicine your doctor gives you will work as it did for thousands of others.

Why are you sure?

"Because it has always happened before," you say.

Fine. But how do you know that what happened in the past will happen in the future? "Because in the past, the future has always resembled the past." But that is exactly what we're asking about! You are using the past to prove that the past is a reliable guide to the future. That is arguing in a circle.

So, strictly speaking, you cannot *prove* that the sun will rise tomorrow. You believe it, firmly, and you are right to. But your belief does not rest on logic. It rests on **habit**.

The man who saw this most clearly was **David Hume**. He was a big, cheerful, sociable Scotsman who loved good food, good conversation and a game of cards. He was also the most unsettling philosopher of his age. He argued that we can never observe a cause actually *making* something happen; that our belief in cause and effect comes from custom, not reason; that when we look inside ourselves, we find no fixed "self", only a bundle of perceptions; that reason cannot tell us what we *ought* to do, only how to get what we already want; that no report of a miracle can ever be rationally believed; and that the famous arguments for God's existence are much weaker than people think.

He was refused two university posts because of his reputation for atheism. And yet he died so peacefully and cheerfully that a famous visitor, who had come hoping to see the unbeliever panic at the end, went away shaken.

By the end of this page, you will understand why Immanuel Kant said Hume woke him from his "dogmatic slumber", why scientists still struggle with "Hume's problem", and how Hume's ideas about habit, feeling and evidence can make you wiser about everything from cricket superstitions to WhatsApp miracle cures. And you may never feel quite so certain about tomorrow's sunrise, or about yourself, again.

## The world he was born into

### Scotland changing fast

David Hume was born in **Edinburgh** in 1711. Four years earlier, in **1707**, the **Acts of Union** had joined the kingdoms of Scotland and England into one country, Great Britain, with one Parliament in London.

Scotland was poor compared to England, and many Scots felt the union had been forced on them. There were armed uprisings, called the **Jacobite risings**, in 1715 and 1745, aiming to restore the exiled Catholic Stuart kings. The 1745 rising, led by "Bonnie Prince Charlie", reached deep into England before being crushed in 1746. Hume lived through all of this.

### The Kirk

Religious life in Scotland was dominated by the **Church of Scotland** (the "Kirk"), a strict Presbyterian Calvinist church. Calvinism taught that God has predestined some people for salvation and others for damnation, that human nature is deeply sinful, and that strict moral discipline is essential. Sundays were for long sermons and prayer. Blasphemy could be punished: in 1697, just fourteen years before Hume was born, a young Edinburgh student named Thomas Aikenhead was **hanged for blasphemy**, the last person executed for that crime in Britain.

Hume grew up in a pious family. As a boy he took religion seriously, even, he later said, using a devotional book to check himself against a list of vices. He lost his faith as a young man, probably by reading philosophy. But he lived his whole life in a society where open unbelief could cost you your career, or worse.

### The Scottish Enlightenment

At the same time, Scotland was entering one of the most extraordinary intellectual flowerings in history: the **Scottish Enlightenment**. Within a few decades, this small, poor country produced Hume, the economist **Adam Smith**, the historian William Robertson, the sociologist Adam Ferguson, the geologist James Hutton, the chemist Joseph Black, the engineer James Watt and many others. Edinburgh became known as the "**Athens of the North**". Clubs and societies met in taverns to debate philosophy, economics, science and politics.

### Newton's shadow

The great intellectual model of the age was **Isaac Newton**, who had explained the motions of the planets and the fall of an apple with a few simple laws, based on observation and mathematics. Philosophers dreamed of doing for the human mind what Newton had done for nature.

Hume's ambition was exactly this. The full title of his first book was *A Treatise of Human Nature: Being an Attempt to introduce the experimental Method of Reasoning into Moral Subjects*. He wanted to build a **science of man**: to study how the human mind actually works, by observation and experience, as Newton had studied nature. And he believed that all other sciences, even mathematics and physics, depend on this science, because all of them are products of the human mind.

The empiricist road had already been opened by **John Locke**, who argued that all our ideas come from experience, and by the Irish bishop **George Berkeley**, who pushed Locke's ideas further. Hume would push them all the way.

## The life, as a story

### A boy who wanted only to read

David Hume (originally spelled Home, pronounced "Hume") was born on 7 May 1711 (by the modern calendar) in Edinburgh. His family were minor gentry with a modest estate called **Ninewells** in the Scottish Borders. His father died when David was two, and he and his brother and sister were raised by their mother, Katherine, who devoted herself to her children. She reportedly said of young David that he was "uncommonly wake-minded", which some read as "unusually weak-minded" and others, more plausibly, as "unusually wide-awake".

He entered the **University of Edinburgh** at around ten or twelve, which was not unusual then. His family expected him to become a lawyer. He tried. But, as he later wrote in his short autobiography, *My Own Life*, he found "an insurmountable aversion to every thing but the pursuits of Philosophy and general Learning; and while they fancied I was poring upon Voet and Vinnius" (famous law books), "Cicero and Virgil were the authors which I was secretly devouring".

### A new scene of thought, and a breakdown

At around eighteen, Hume felt he had glimpsed something new. He later wrote to a physician that "there seem'd to be open'd up to me a new Scene of Thought, which transported me beyond Measure". He threw himself into study with tremendous intensity.

And then he collapsed. For several years he suffered from what seems to have been depression and physical symptoms: he couldn't concentrate, felt listless, had digestive problems and, oddly, developed a ravenous appetite and grew from a thin youth into a heavy man. He wrote a long, detailed letter describing his condition to a doctor, in which he called it "the disease of the learned". It is one of the earliest first-hand descriptions of what we might call burnout.

To break out of it, he tried the opposite of a scholarly life. In 1734 he went to **Bristol** to work as a clerk for a sugar merchant. He lasted a few months. Commerce was not for him.

### Writing a masterpiece in a French village

Instead, Hume went to **France**, where living was cheap, and settled at **La Flèche**, a small town in Anjou, home of the Jesuit college where Descartes had been educated. He talked with the Jesuits, sometimes arguing about miracles, and wrote. In about three years, before he was twenty-six, he completed one of the greatest works in the history of philosophy: ***A Treatise of Human Nature***.

### Dead-born from the press

He returned to London to publish it. Books I and II appeared in 1739, Book III in 1740, anonymously. Hume expected a sensation. Instead, as he wrote later in *My Own Life*:

"Never literary attempt was more unfortunate than my Treatise of Human Nature. It fell dead-born from the press, without reaching such distinction as even to excite a murmur among the zealots."

That isn't quite true; there were a few reviews, mostly baffled or hostile. But it sold poorly and was largely ignored. Hume, being cheerful by nature, recovered quickly. He decided the problem was not the ideas but the presentation: the *Treatise* was too long, too technical, too youthful. He would rewrite his philosophy in shorter, more elegant, more accessible forms.

### Essays, rejection and odd jobs

His *Essays, Moral and Political* (1741–42), short pieces on politics, society, taste and manners, were a success. He seemed set for a respectable career.

In 1745, he applied for the **chair of moral philosophy at the University of Edinburgh**. The clergy and their allies campaigned against him, citing the *Treatise* as proof that he was a sceptic and an atheist. He was rejected. In 1751–52 he applied for a chair at the **University of Glasgow**; again he was rejected for the same reasons.

So Hume took other jobs. For a disastrous year he was tutor to a young nobleman, the Marquess of Annandale, who turned out to be insane. He became secretary to General James St Clair, accompanying a failed military raid on the coast of France in 1746 (Hume wrote up the account), and later a diplomatic mission to Vienna and Turin. A friend described the sight of the heavy philosopher in a military uniform.

Meanwhile he kept rewriting his philosophy. In 1748 came the *Philosophical Essays Concerning Human Understanding*, later called ***An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding***, a shorter, clearer version of Book I of the *Treatise*, with new sections on miracles and on providence. In 1751 came ***An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals***, a reworking of Book III. Hume said this one was, "of all my writings, historical, philosophical, or literary, incomparably the best".

### The librarian who wrote a bestseller

In 1752, Hume was appointed **Keeper of the Advocates' Library** in Edinburgh, the library of the Scottish legal profession, which gave him access to thousands of books. He used them to write a huge ***History of England***, in six volumes, from the Roman invasion to the Glorious Revolution of 1688.

The first volume, on the Stuart kings, was attacked from all sides: Whigs thought it too sympathetic to King Charles I, and others thought it too sceptical about religion. Hume shed, he joked, a "generous tear" for Charles. But the later volumes sold enormously. For about a century, Hume was known to the British public not as a philosopher but as **the historian**. Library catalogues listed him as "David Hume, historian".

### The darling of Paris

In 1763, Hume went to Paris as secretary to the British ambassador. To his astonishment, he became a celebrity. The French intellectual elite, the *philosophes*, admired his writings. Fashionable ladies in the salons competed for his company. They called him "**le bon David**", "good David", for his kindness and good humour. One story tells of him being cast, at a party, as a sultan in a little play, required to woo two beautiful women; he could only stammer, pat his knees and repeat, "Well, ladies, well, here you are". Hume, large, awkward and middle-aged, enjoyed it all enormously.

### The quarrel with Rousseau

In 1766, Hume tried to do a great kindness. The famous philosopher **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** was being persecuted in France and Switzerland for his writings. Hume brought him to England, found him a place to live and arranged a royal pension for him.

Rousseau, who was by then suffering from serious paranoia, became convinced that Hume was part of a conspiracy against him. He wrote Hume a long, wild letter of accusation. Hume, hurt and anxious to protect his reputation, published his side of the story. The quarrel became one of the great public scandals of the literary world. Hume, who prided himself on his calm temper, was rattled by it.

### Home to Edinburgh

After a period as an Under-Secretary of State in London, Hume returned to Edinburgh in 1769, now wealthy and famous. He built a house in the elegant new part of the city, the **New Town**. According to a well-known story, a witty young woman chalked the words "St David's Street" on the wall of the street where he lived, mocking the famous unbeliever as a saint. Hume's servant was upset; Hume told her not to mind: many better men had been made saints before. The street is still called St David Street.

He spent his last years hosting dinners, writing letters, playing whist and enjoying his friends, especially **Adam Smith**, whose *Wealth of Nations* came out in 1776. Hume wrote him a delighted letter of congratulation.

### A death that shook a believer

In 1775, Hume became seriously ill, probably with cancer of the bowel or liver. He knew he was dying. He wrote a short, calm autobiography, *My Own Life*, which ends: "I am, or rather was (for that is the style I must now use in speaking of myself)... a man of mild dispositions, of command of temper, of an open, social, and cheerful humour, capable of attachment, but little susceptible of enmity, and of great moderation in all my passions."

In July 1776, the young **James Boswell**, famous later as the biographer of Samuel Johnson, visited him. Boswell was a believing Christian who was fascinated, and frightened, by Hume's unbelief. He asked whether Hume did not think there might be a future state after death. Hume said he thought it a most unreasonable fancy. Boswell asked whether the thought of annihilation didn't trouble him. Hume said it troubled him no more than the thought that he had not existed before he was born. Boswell left deeply disturbed. Seeing a man face death so cheerfully, with no hope of heaven, shook his own faith for some time.

Hume joked with friends about what excuses he might offer **Charon**, the ferryman of the dead in Greek myth, to delay his crossing: he was revising his works for a new edition; he hoped to see the Church collapse. Charon, he imagined, would reply: "You loitering rogue, that will not happen these many hundred years. Do you fancy I will grant you a lease for so long a term? Get into the boat this instant."

He died on **25 August 1776**, aged sixty-five. Adam Smith wrote that he had always considered Hume, both in his lifetime and since his death, "as approaching as nearly to the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man, as perhaps the nature of human frailty will permit". For saying this, Smith was attacked by religious critics for years.

Hume had arranged for his most dangerous work, the ***Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion***, to be published after his death. Smith, anxious about the reaction, declined to do it. Hume's nephew published it in 1779.

## The big ideas, one by one

Hume's philosophy can feel like a series of demolitions. But keep in mind his purpose. He was not trying to destroy knowledge for fun. He wanted to understand how the human mind actually works, honestly, without flattering ourselves, so that we would be more modest, more tolerant and less fanatical.

### Idea 1: Impressions and ideas

**The idea in one line:** Everything in the mind is either a vivid **impression** (from sensation or feeling) or a fainter **idea** (a copy of an impression).

Hume divided all the contents of the mind into two kinds:

- **Impressions:** our lively, forceful perceptions: when we see, hear, feel, love, hate, desire or will. The experience of actually biting into a mango, or of feeling anger.
- **Ideas:** fainter copies of impressions, as when we remember or imagine. Remembering the taste of a mango, or thinking about your anger yesterday.

Then he stated a principle, often called the **copy principle**: all our simple ideas are copies of earlier simple impressions. You cannot have an idea of a colour you have never seen, or a taste you've never tasted. A person born blind has no idea of colour; a person who has never tasted pineapple has no real idea of its taste.

We can, of course, imagine things we've never experienced: a golden mountain, a flying elephant. But these are **complex ideas**, built by combining simple ideas we already have (gold, mountain, elephant, flying).

**The missing shade of blue.** Hume was honest enough to point out an exception to his own principle. Imagine a person who has seen every shade of blue except one. Lay all the other shades before him in order from darkest to lightest, with a gap where the missing shade belongs. Could he imagine it? Hume thought he probably could. He called this exception so singular that it was not worth altering the general principle. Philosophers still discuss the "missing shade of blue".

**Why it matters: a meaning test.** Hume used the copy principle as a powerful tool. When a philosopher or theologian uses an impressive-sounding word, like "substance", "soul", "necessary connection" or "essence", ask: **from what impression is this idea derived?** If you cannot find one, the word may be empty, a sound with no clear meaning behind it.

**An example.** Someone tells you that a product contains "quantum energy" that "balances your aura". Ask Hume's question: what experience does "quantum energy" or "aura balance" correspond to? If no one can point to any impression, you may be looking at words that sound meaningful but mean nothing at all.

### Idea 2: How the mind connects ideas

**The idea in one line:** The mind moves from one idea to another according to three principles: resemblance, contiguity and cause and effect.

Hume noticed that thoughts don't jump around randomly. One idea leads to another by **association**. He found three principles of association:

1. **Resemblance:** a photo makes you think of the person in it.
2. **Contiguity in time or place:** thinking of a room makes you think of the room next to it; thinking of your school makes you think of the shop at the gate.
3. **Cause and effect:** seeing a wound makes you think of the pain it causes.

He called this a kind of **attraction** in the mental world, comparable to Newton's gravity in the physical world. It was his attempt to find the basic laws of the mind.

**Why it matters.** Advertisers, film-makers and propagandists use the association of ideas every day: they put their product next to happy faces, beautiful places and admired celebrities, hoping the good feelings will attach to the product. Psychologists later developed theories of association and conditioning, from Pavlov's dogs onwards, that build on similar ideas.

### Idea 3: Two kinds of knowledge (Hume's fork)

**The idea in one line:** All reasoning concerns either **relations of ideas** (certain but about nothing in the world) or **matters of fact** (about the world but never certain).

Hume divided everything we can know into two kinds:

- **Relations of ideas:** truths of mathematics and logic, such as "three times five equals half of thirty" or "all bachelors are unmarried". These are certain: denying them involves a contradiction. And you can know them just by thinking, without looking at the world. But they tell you nothing about what actually exists.
- **Matters of fact:** truths about the world, such as "the sun will rise tomorrow" or "Delhi is north of Mumbai". Their opposite is always possible without contradiction: you can imagine the sun not rising. So they can never be proved by pure reasoning. We know them only through **experience**.

This sharp division is often called **Hume's fork**: two prongs, and every claim must sit on one or the other.

Then Hume ended the *Enquiry* with one of the most explosive paragraphs in philosophy:

"When we run over libraries, persuaded of these principles, what havoc must we make? If we take in our hand any volume; of divinity or school metaphysics, for instance; let us ask, Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No. Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No. Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."

**Why it matters.** This was a direct challenge to traditional metaphysics and theology, which claimed to prove things about God, the soul and ultimate reality by pure reasoning. Hume says: either your book is mathematics, or it is based on evidence. If it's neither, it's empty. Twentieth-century philosophers called **logical positivists** took this idea and made it the centre of their philosophy.

### Idea 4: We never see causation

**The idea in one line:** We never observe a cause actually producing its effect; we only see one event regularly followed by another, and the idea of a necessary connection comes from our own habit of expectation.

This is Hume's most famous and most influential argument.

**An example: billiard balls.** Hume's favourite example was billiards. (Think of carrom, if you prefer.) You hit one ball; it rolls and strikes a second ball; the second ball moves. You say: the first ball *caused* the second to move. Now look carefully. What do you actually **see**?

1. The first ball moving.
2. The first ball touching the second.
3. The second ball moving.

That's all. You see one event, then another, close together, the first before the second. What you **never see** is the *cause itself*, some power or force passing from the first ball to the second and making it move. Watch as closely as you like, slow it down, put it under a microscope. You'll see more events, but never the "making happen" itself.

**So where does the idea of causation come from?** Hume's answer: from **repetition**. After we've seen events of type A followed by events of type B many, many times, our mind develops a **habit**. When we see A, we automatically expect B. That feeling of expectation, of "B must follow", is the source of our idea of **necessary connection**. The necessity isn't something we see in the world; it's something we **feel** in our own minds, and then project onto the world.

**The first man.** Hume imagined Adam, the first man, created with perfect reasoning powers but no experience. Could Adam, seeing water for the first time, have known that it would drown him? Seeing fire, that it would burn him? No. Nothing in the appearance of water or fire tells you what it will do. Only experience teaches cause and effect.

**Why it matters.** This shook philosophy to its foundations. If causation is not something we can observe or prove, then all of science, which is a search for causes, rests on something less than certain. Hume didn't think we should stop believing in causes; that would be impossible. But he showed that our belief in causes is a natural habit of the mind, not a conclusion of pure reason.

**A modern example.** Every scientist knows the saying: **correlation is not causation**. Two things happening together does not prove one causes the other. Ice cream sales and drowning deaths both rise in summer; ice cream doesn't cause drowning (hot weather causes both). Hume's analysis is the deep root of this caution.

### Idea 5: The problem of induction

**The idea in one line:** We cannot prove, by reason or experience, that the future will resemble the past, yet all our reasoning about the world assumes it will.

This follows directly from the last idea, and it's what our chicken story was about.

All reasoning about matters of fact, Hume said, is based on cause and effect, and all our knowledge of cause and effect comes from experience. We observe that bread has nourished us in the past, so we expect it to nourish us in the future. But what justifies the step from "in the past" to "in the future"?

- It can't be **logic**, because there's no contradiction in imagining bread suddenly becoming poisonous.
- It can't be **experience**, because any argument from experience already assumes that the future will resemble the past, which is exactly what we're trying to prove. That's circular.

So we have no rational justification for **induction**, the move from observed cases to unobserved cases.

**Then why do we do it?** Because of **custom** or **habit**. Nature has built us to expect the future to resemble the past. And thank goodness: we couldn't survive otherwise. "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life. It is that principle alone which renders our experience useful to us, and makes us expect, for the future, a similar train of events with those which have appeared in the past."

**The Indian parallel: the Charvakas.** Long before Hume, the Indian materialist school known as **Charvaka** or **Lokayata** argued something remarkably similar. Indian logicians of the Nyaya school relied on ***vyapti***, invariable concomitance, the universal rule that, for example, "wherever there is smoke, there is fire". The Charvakas asked: how do you know this rule holds everywhere and always? You have only seen some cases of smoke and fire. You cannot observe all past, present and future cases. So the universal rule is never established, and inference based on it is never certain. Only direct perception, they said, is a reliable source of knowledge. Their arguments survive mostly in the writings of their opponents, but they show that the problem of induction was discovered more than once.

**Why it matters.** The problem of induction has never been fully solved. Philosophers like Karl Popper argued that science doesn't really prove theories through induction; it proposes bold hypotheses and tries to **falsify** them. A thousand white swans can't prove that all swans are white, but one black swan can prove that they aren't. (Europeans believed all swans were white until they found black swans in Australia.) Financial experts use the term "**black swan**" for rare, unpredictable events that nobody expected because they had never seen them before: market crashes, pandemics. Every one of these is Hume's chicken.

**A common misunderstanding.** Hume did **not** say that we should stop relying on induction, or that all predictions are equally good. He said we can't *prove* it is reliable, but we can't help relying on it, and a wise person relies on it carefully, proportioning belief to the evidence.

### Idea 6: The self is a bundle

**The idea in one line:** When I look inside myself, I never find a "self", only particular perceptions; the self is nothing but a bundle of perceptions.

Descartes had said: I can be certain of one thing, that I am a thinking thing, a self. Many philosophers believed in a simple, unchanging soul at the core of each person.

Hume tried to find it. In one of the most famous passages in philosophy, he wrote:

"For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception."

And so he concluded that a person is "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement".

He compared the mind to a **theatre**, where perceptions appear, pass, re-pass, glide away and mingle in endless variety. But, he added, we shouldn't be misled by the comparison: there is no stage, no building; there are only the perceptions themselves.

**Try it yourself.** Close your eyes for a moment and try to observe your "self" directly. Not a thought, not a feeling, not a sensation, but the self that has them. What do you find? Most people report what Hume reported: a thought, a sound, a feeling of their body, a memory... but no separate "self" sitting behind them.

**The Buddhist parallel.** This is strikingly close to the Buddha's teaching of ***anatta***, non-self: that a person is a changing collection of physical and mental processes (the five aggregates), with no permanent self at the centre. Scholars have even wondered whether Hume could have learned of Buddhist ideas through Jesuit contacts at La Flèche, who had missionary connections in Asia. There's no firm evidence. It may simply be that two honest observers, looking carefully at their own experience, found the same thing.

**Why it matters.** If there is no fixed self, then much of our anxious defence of "me", my image, my reputation, my identity, rests on a kind of illusion. Modern neuroscience has found no single place in the brain where "the self" sits; many researchers describe the self as a construction of many interacting processes. Hume got there by introspection.

**An honest admission.** Interestingly, Hume himself was not fully satisfied with his account. In an appendix to the *Treatise*, he admitted that he could not explain what binds the bundle together into one mind, and confessed that the problem was "too hard for my understanding". This kind of honesty about the limits of one's own theory is rare in great philosophers.

### Idea 7: Scepticism, and backgammon

**The idea in one line:** Philosophical reasoning can lead us to doubt almost everything, but nature is too strong; we can't live as sceptics, and we shouldn't try to.

At the end of Book I of the *Treatise*, Hume describes his state of mind after following his arguments to their conclusions. He feels plunged into despair, surrounded by the deepest darkness, unsure of anything: who he is, what causes things, what his relation is to anything else. He feels like a man shipwrecked on a barren rock.

And then nature rescues him. "I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends; and when after three or four hours' amusement, I would return to these speculations, they appear so cold, and strained, and ridiculous, that I cannot find in my heart to enter into them any farther."

This is one of the most human moments in philosophy. Hume admits that reason, pushed to its limits, leads to doubt about everything. But we are not purely rational creatures. We are animals with natural instincts to believe in the external world, in causes, in other people. Nature will not let us stay sceptics for long.

So Hume recommends a **mitigated scepticism**: be modest, cautious and undogmatic in your beliefs, aware of the limits of human understanding, but don't pretend you can doubt everything in practice. Be a philosopher, he says, "but, amidst all your philosophy, be still a man".

**Why it matters.** This balance is wise. Total scepticism is impossible to live by and can become an excuse for believing nothing, or anything. Total certainty is arrogant and dangerous. Hume's middle way, a cheerful, modest, evidence-based humility, is a good model for anyone in a world of loud opinions.

### Idea 8: Reason is the slave of the passions

**The idea in one line:** Reason alone cannot motivate us to act; it can only tell us how to get what our feelings and desires already want.

Philosophers since Plato had pictured the soul as a battle between **reason** and **passion**, with the good person being the one whose reason controls their passions. Hume turned this upside down:

"Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them."

What does he mean? Reason, for Hume, deals with two things: relations of ideas (like mathematics) and matters of fact (like cause and effect). Neither of these can, by itself, make you *want* anything. Reason can tell you that eating this food will nourish you, but only your desire to live and be healthy makes you eat it. Reason can tell you the fastest route to the station, but only your wish to catch the train makes you take it.

So **desires**, feelings, passions, set our goals. **Reason** works out how to reach them. Reason is like a GPS: it can tell you how to get anywhere, but it can't tell you where you want to go.

He even made a deliberately shocking statement: "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger." Of course such a preference would be monstrous. But, he says, it isn't a mistake in *reasoning*. It's a defect of feeling, of humanity.

**An example.** You know that exercise is good for you. You have all the information. And still you don't exercise. Why? Because knowing isn't the same as wanting. What gets you to the gym is not more information, but a feeling: worry about your health, desire to look good, enjoyment of the activity, or a friend who makes it fun. Every health campaign that only gives information, without engaging feelings, learns Hume's lesson the hard way.

**A common misunderstanding.** "Slave of the passions" doesn't mean we should give in to every impulse, or that reason is useless. Reason can correct our passions when they are based on false beliefs (you're afraid of the dark because you think there's a snake, but there isn't), and it helps us choose the best means. And Hume included among the "passions" calm, steady sentiments such as benevolence and the love of justice, not just wild impulses.

### Idea 9: Is and ought

**The idea in one line:** You cannot logically get a statement about what **ought** to be from statements about what **is**.

In a short paragraph in the *Treatise*, Hume made an observation that became one of the most famous in moral philosophy. He noticed that in every system of morality he had read, the author would proceed for a while in the ordinary way, reasoning about God or human affairs with statements using "is" and "is not". Then suddenly, without explanation, he would start using "ought" and "ought not". This change, Hume said, is of the "last consequence": since "ought" expresses a new relation, a reason should be given for how it can be deduced from the "is" statements, which seems completely inconceivable.

**An example.** "Human beings naturally desire wealth. Therefore, people ought to pursue wealth." Or: "This tradition has existed for a thousand years. Therefore, we ought to keep it." Or: "Animals in nature kill each other. Therefore, it's okay for humans to be violent." In each case, a fact about what *is* gets turned into a claim about what *ought* to be, without justification. Something is missing: a value, a principle, a feeling.

This gap is often called **Hume's guillotine** or the **is–ought problem**. A related error, coined by a later philosopher, G. E. Moore, is the "naturalistic fallacy".

**Why it matters.** This is one of the most useful tools for critical thinking about morality and politics. Whenever someone says "it's natural, so it's right", or "it's always been this way, so it should stay this way", or "this is what most people do, so it's what we should do", you can ask: how did you get from *is* to *ought*?

### Idea 10: Morality comes from feeling and sympathy

**The idea in one line:** Moral distinctions come not from reason but from **sentiment**: feelings of approval and disapproval, made possible by our capacity for **sympathy** with others.

If reason can't tell us what we ought to do, where do our moral judgements come from? Hume's answer: from **feeling**.

When we call an action virtuous or vicious, we are expressing a feeling of approval or disapproval that arises when we contemplate it. Take a deliberate murder. Examine it from every side, Hume said. You'll find passions, motives, actions and thoughts. But where is the "vice" in it? You'll never find it, until you turn your attention to your own heart and find a feeling of disapproval arising in you. The vice lies in you, as the colour lies in your perception.

But this doesn't mean morality is random or merely personal. Our moral feelings are grounded in **sympathy**: our natural capacity to share the feelings of others. "The minds of men are mirrors to one another." When we see someone in pain, we feel pain too; when we see someone happy, we feel some happiness. So we naturally approve of qualities that tend to make people happy and disapprove of those that cause suffering.

And to make moral judgements fair, we learn to take up a **general point of view**: to consider how an action affects everyone concerned, not just how it affects us personally.

Hume grouped the virtues into four kinds: qualities **useful** to others (like benevolence and justice), **useful** to ourselves (like prudence and industry), **agreeable** to others (like good manners and wit) and **agreeable** to ourselves (like cheerfulness and self-respect).

**An example.** Why do we admire someone who rescues a child from a burning building? Not because a rational calculation shows it's virtuous, but because we instantly feel the child's danger and relief, the parents' fear and gratitude, and something in us responds with warm approval. Why do we despise someone who cheats a poor widow? Because we feel her loss and helplessness. Morality, for Hume, is woven out of these shared feelings.

**Why it matters.** Hume's view became one of the foundations of modern moral philosophy. His friend **Adam Smith** built an entire moral theory on sympathy. **Jeremy Bentham** took Hume's emphasis on utility, on what produces happiness, and built utilitarianism. Modern psychology and neuroscience, studying empathy and "mirror neurons", have found that our moral sense is indeed deeply rooted in emotion and social feeling, much as Hume suggested.

### Idea 11: Miracles

**The idea in one line:** A wise person should never believe a report of a miracle unless it would be even more miraculous for the report to be false.

In Section 10 of the *Enquiry*, Hume made an argument that infuriated religious readers.

A **miracle**, he said, is a violation of the laws of nature. And the laws of nature are established by the **most uniform experience** humans have: that the dead stay dead, that water doesn't turn into wine on its own, that people don't walk on water. Against this enormous weight of experience stands a **report**, a testimony, that a miracle happened.

Now, how reliable is testimony? People lie. People exaggerate. People are deceived. People love to tell and believe astonishing stories. People with a religious cause may think a little exaggeration is justified. Reports of miracles tend to come from ignorant and distant times and places, and multiply where people are already eager to believe.

So a wise person, who "proportions his belief to the evidence", must weigh the two. His rule: "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish".

In other words: what is more likely, that the laws of nature were broken, or that the person telling you about it is mistaken, deceived or lying?

**An example.** Every few months a story goes viral: a statue drinks milk, a "godman" produces objects from thin air, a faith healer makes the lame walk. Hume would say: consider which is more likely: a genuine suspension of the laws of nature, or a trick, a misunderstanding, a natural explanation not yet known, or a rumour that grew in the telling? In India, **rationalist** organisations have for decades investigated such claims and often demonstrated the tricks involved. The rationalist activist **Narendra Dabholkar** campaigned against superstition and exploitation in Maharashtra for years; he was murdered in 2013, and the state passed an anti-superstition law shortly afterwards. Investigating miracle claims can be dangerous work.

**Why it matters.** Hume's argument is not just about religion. It's a general principle of evidence: **extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence**, as the astronomer Carl Sagan later put it. It applies to conspiracy theories, health scams, investment frauds and fake news as much as to miracles.

**A common misunderstanding.** Hume did not prove that miracles are impossible. He argued that it's never *rational to believe a report* of one, given the nature of human testimony. And he was careful: he admitted that if the testimony were extraordinarily strong, the case might be different.

### Idea 12: The argument from design

**The idea in one line:** The order of the world does not prove the existence of a wise, good, all-powerful God, as many believed.

The most popular argument for God's existence in Hume's time was the **argument from design**. Look at the world: it's like a great machine, full of order and purpose. The eye is perfectly made for seeing, the wings of birds for flying. Just as a watch implies a watchmaker, the world implies a divine designer.

In the ***Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion***, published after his death, Hume has three characters debate this argument: Cleanthes, who defends it; Demea, a traditional believer who prefers faith to arguments; and Philo, a sceptic who takes it apart. Philo is usually thought to speak for Hume, though Hume was careful to leave it ambiguous.

Philo's objections are devastating:

- **Is the analogy good?** The world is not much like a watch or a house. It's just as much like an animal or a plant, which grow from seeds without any designer. Why not say the world grew from a cosmic seed?
- **Even if there's a designer, which one?** The argument gives no reason to think there is only one designer, or that the designer is perfect. Perhaps the world was made by a committee of gods, or by an inexperienced young god who later abandoned it, or by an old, careless god whose powers were failing. Philo says that the world, for all we know, may be "the first rude essay of some infant deity" who afterwards abandoned it, ashamed of his poor performance.
- **The problem of evil.** Philo quotes old questions he attributes to Epicurus: Is God willing to prevent evil, but not able? Then he is not all-powerful. Is he able, but not willing? Then he is malevolent. Is he both able and willing? Then where does evil come from?

**Why it matters.** Hume's *Dialogues* are among the most important works on religion ever written. Seventy years after them, Charles Darwin showed how the appearance of design in living things could arise naturally, through evolution by natural selection, without a designer. Hume had already shown that the design argument was logically weak; Darwin provided an alternative explanation.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Locke and Berkeley: the empiricist road

Hume started where **John Locke** started: all ideas come from experience. Locke still believed we could know a great deal: that God exists, that the external world exists, that there are real substances. Berkeley argued that matter doesn't exist apart from minds. Hume went further than both, showing that empiricism, followed consistently, undermines our claims to know about causes, the external world and the self. He took the empiricist road to its end, and found a cliff.

### Descartes: the self that wasn't there

**Descartes** began from the certainty of the thinking self. Hume looked for that self and couldn't find it. Descartes believed reason could prove God's existence and the reliability of our ideas. Hume believed reason was far weaker, and that our deepest beliefs come from habit and nature, not proof.

### The Buddha: two observers of the mind

We've already seen the remarkable parallel between Hume's "bundle" and the **Buddha's** *anatta*. But there's a difference in what they did with the insight. For the Buddha, seeing through the illusion of self was the path to liberation from suffering. For Hume, it was an interesting philosophical discovery, which he then set aside to enjoy dinner and backgammon. The Buddha built a way of life on it; Hume built a science of human nature.

### Adam Smith: the closest friend

**Adam Smith** was Hume's closest friend for twenty-five years. Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759) built on Hume's idea of sympathy, adding the concept of the "impartial spectator". Smith's economics, too, owed much to Hume's essays on trade, money and the balance of payments, which argued against the mercantilist idea that a nation grows rich by hoarding gold. Their friendship, and Smith's loving tribute after Hume's death, is one of the most moving in intellectual history.

### Bentham and utilitarianism

**Jeremy Bentham** said that when he read Book III of Hume's *Treatise*, he felt "as if scales had fallen from my eyes", seeing that virtue is founded on **utility**, on what tends to produce happiness. Bentham built utilitarianism partly on this insight, though he turned Hume's careful analysis of moral sentiment into a system for calculating the greatest happiness.

### Kant: the great awakening

The most famous response to Hume came from **Immanuel Kant**, who wrote that "the remembrance of David Hume was the very thing that many years ago first interrupted my dogmatic slumber". Kant took Hume's challenge about causation very seriously. His answer was revolutionary: causation is not something we learn from experience, nor is it a mere habit; it is a **structure our minds bring to experience**. We can be certain that every event has a cause, not because we observe it in the world, but because our minds organise all experience that way. This was the starting point of Kant's whole philosophy, and of much of modern philosophy since.

### The logical positivists and Popper

In the twentieth century, a group of philosophers and scientists in Vienna, the **logical positivists**, adopted Hume's fork as their banner: meaningful statements are either logical truths or empirically verifiable claims; everything else, including most metaphysics and theology, is meaningless. The philosopher **Karl Popper** took Hume's problem of induction and argued that science works not by proving theories but by trying to falsify them.

### Modern psychology

Psychologists studying how people make judgements, such as Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, found that much human reasoning is driven by fast, automatic, emotional processes rather than slow, careful logic, and that people are deeply influenced by habit, association and feeling. Moral psychologists like Jonathan Haidt have argued that moral judgements are mostly driven by intuitions and emotions, with reasoning often coming afterwards to justify them, a view Haidt explicitly connects to Hume. Hume's "science of man" turned out to be remarkably prophetic.

## See it around you

**Cricket superstitions.** A batsman wears the same "lucky" socks because he scored a century the last time he wore them. Fans sit in the same spot on the sofa during a run chase. This is Hume's constant conjunction gone wild: seeing two things together a few times, the mind invents a causal link.

**Correlation and causation in the news.** "People who drink coffee live longer!" "Children who play video games score lower!" Hume teaches you to ask: did one actually cause the other, or did both come from something else?

**Black swans in markets and pandemics.** Every financial crash and every unexpected crisis is Hume's chicken: people assumed the future would resemble the past, and it didn't.

**Miracle cures and godmen.** Every viral miracle, every "doctors hate this one trick", every faith healer: Hume's question is, which is more likely, a law of nature broken, or a story mistaken?

**Moral debates.** Every time someone argues "it's natural, so it's right" or "it's tradition, so it must continue", Hume's is–ought gap is there to examine.

**Advertising.** Ads associate products with happy families, beautiful landscapes and celebrities, hoping the feeling will transfer. That's Hume's association of ideas, monetised.

**Self-help and identity.** Discussions about how we "construct" our identities, how the self changes over time, and how clinging to a fixed self-image causes suffering, echo Hume's bundle theory.

**Empathy and morality.** When a photograph of a suffering child moves millions to donate, you are seeing Hume's sympathy at work: moral action driven by shared feeling, not abstract reasoning.

## Use it

**1. Proportion your belief to the evidence.** Before you accept a claim, especially a surprising one, ask: what's the evidence? How strong is it? Believe accordingly, no more and no less.

**2. Apply the miracle test.** For any extraordinary claim, ask: which would be more surprising, that this claim is true, or that the person telling it is mistaken or lying?

**3. Look for the impression.** When someone uses an impressive-sounding word, ask: what experience does this actually correspond to? If no one can say, be suspicious.

**4. Separate correlation from causation.** When two things go together, ask: does one really cause the other, or could something else cause both?

**5. Watch for the is–ought jump.** When an argument moves from "this is how things are" to "this is how things should be", stop and ask what value or principle has been slipped in.

**6. Use feelings wisely.** Hume says reason serves the passions. So if you want to change a habit, don't rely only on information; find a feeling that motivates you, and use reason to plan how to get there.

**7. Practise sympathy, then step back.** Let yourself feel others' joys and sorrows, which is the root of morality. Then take the "general point of view": how does this action affect everyone involved, not just me and my friends?

**8. Be a philosopher, but be still a person.** Think deeply, question boldly. Then go and have dinner with your friends. Don't let doubt paralyse you or cut you off from ordinary life.

**9. Look for your "self".** Sit quietly and try to observe your self directly. Notice what you find. It may change how tightly you hold onto your image of who you are.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Racism.** In a footnote to his essay "Of National Characters", added in 1753, Hume wrote that he was "apt to suspect the negroes, and in general all the other species of men... to be naturally inferior to the whites", dismissing reports of accomplished Black people. This passage is ugly, was influential, and was used by defenders of slavery. It was challenged even in his own time, notably by the Scottish philosopher James Beattie. It is a serious stain on the reputation of a man celebrated for his humane temper, and a reminder that a sceptic about so much failed to be sceptical about his own prejudices.

**2. The self remains a puzzle.** Hume himself admitted he couldn't explain what ties the "bundle" of perceptions together into one mind. Critics argue that perceptions don't just float around; they are always *someone's* perceptions. The question "who is doing the perceiving?" doesn't go away.

**3. Is causation really just habit?** Many philosophers, from Kant onwards, have argued that Hume's account of causation leaves out something real. Modern physics describes causal laws mathematically, and many scientists believe they are discovering real features of the world, not just habits of expectation.

**4. Too much reliance on introspection.** Hume's "science of man" was based largely on looking inside his own mind. But modern psychology shows that introspection is often unreliable: we frequently don't know why we do what we do.

**5. Morality as feeling can seem too weak.** If morality rests on sentiment, what do we say to someone whose sentiments are cruel? Hume relied on the natural sympathy of most people and the general point of view. Critics, like Kant, argued that morality needs a firmer foundation in reason, binding on all rational beings regardless of their feelings.

**6. The miracle argument may be too strong.** Some critics argue that Hume's reasoning about miracles, applied strictly, would also make us reject any genuinely new and surprising scientific discovery, since it too contradicts "uniform experience". (When reports of meteorites falling from the sky first came in, many educated people dismissed them as impossible.) Hume's defenders reply that science doesn't rely on single testimonies but on repeatable evidence.

**7. Conservative in politics.** Some readers note that Hume's scepticism about grand theories and his emphasis on custom and habit made him cautious, even conservative, in politics: favouring established institutions and gradual change. Whether this is wisdom or a lack of moral urgency depends on your point of view.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you probably felt quite sure of many things: that you see causes making things happen, that the future will be like the past, that you have a solid, unchanging self, that reason tells you what's right, and that a confident report of something astonishing deserves to be believed.

**Now,** you can see that much of what you call knowledge rests on **habit**, a deep and useful habit, but a habit nonetheless. You can see that causes are inferred, not seen; that the self may be more like a river than a rock; that feelings, not reasons alone, move us and ground our morality; and that the wise person proportions belief to the evidence.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **Tomorrow's sunrise** becomes a confident expectation, not a proven certainty, and you feel a little more like a well-fed chicken.
- **A batsman's lucky socks** look like the mind inventing causes where there is only coincidence.
- **A viral miracle** triggers Hume's question: which is more likely, a broken law of nature or a broken story?
- **"It's natural, so it's right"** sounds like a leap across a gap.
- **Your "self"** starts to look like a stream of thoughts, feelings and sensations, held together by memory and habit.
- **A moral debate** reveals the feelings underneath the arguments.
- **Deep doubt** no longer seems frightening, because you know that after a good dinner and an evening with friends, nature will bring you back.

Hume took apart some of humanity's most cherished certainties, and then went to dinner, played backgammon and died laughing with his friends. Perhaps that is the deepest lesson: you can live without certainty, and still live well.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** David Hume (1711–1776), Scottish philosopher, historian and essayist, a leading figure of the Scottish Enlightenment; refused two professorships for alleged atheism; famous in his lifetime as a historian.
- **Aim:** a "science of man", studying the mind by observation and experience, as Newton studied nature.
- **Mind:** all perceptions are impressions or ideas; simple ideas copy impressions; ideas are connected by resemblance, contiguity and cause and effect.
- **Hume's fork:** relations of ideas (certain, but not about the world) vs matters of fact (about the world, but never certain).
- **Causation:** we never observe necessary connection, only constant conjunction; the idea of necessity comes from habit.
- **Induction:** we cannot prove the future will resemble the past; custom is the great guide of life (the Charvakas raised a similar problem in India).
- **Self:** nothing but a bundle of perceptions (close to the Buddha's *anatta*).
- **Scepticism:** mitigated; nature prevents total doubt ("I dine, I play a game of backgammon").
- **Ethics:** reason is the slave of the passions; you cannot derive "ought" from "is"; morality rests on sentiment and sympathy.
- **Religion:** no testimony can establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be more miraculous; the argument from design is weak.
- **Legacy:** woke Kant from his dogmatic slumber; influenced Smith, Bentham, the logical positivists, Popper and modern psychology.
- **Criticisms:** a racist footnote; an unsolved puzzle about the self; possibly too narrow a view of causation and morality.

**Key terms**

- **Empiricism:** knowledge comes from experience.
- **Impressions and ideas:** vivid perceptions and their fainter copies.
- **Copy principle:** all simple ideas copy prior impressions.
- **Hume's fork:** the division between relations of ideas and matters of fact.
- **Constant conjunction:** the regular succession of one type of event by another.
- **Problem of induction:** the difficulty of justifying inference from observed to unobserved cases.
- **Bundle theory:** the self is a collection of perceptions.
- **Is–ought problem:** the gap between factual and moral claims.
- **Sympathy:** our capacity to share the feelings of others.
- **Mitigated scepticism:** modest, cautious belief that accepts the limits of human understanding.
