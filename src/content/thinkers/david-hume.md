## Life

David Hume was born in Edinburgh in 1711, the younger son of a minor landowning family. He entered the University of Edinburgh at about ten or twelve, and was expected to become a lawyer, but found himself with "an insurmountable aversion to everything but the pursuits of philosophy and general learning." At eighteen he believed he had glimpsed "a new scene of thought". The intense work that followed brought on a breakdown, from which he recovered by taking up a career in commerce at Bristol, which he abandoned within months.

He moved to France, living cheaply at La Flèche, where Descartes had been educated, and there wrote *A Treatise of Human Nature*, published when he was not yet thirty. It "fell dead-born from the press," he later recalled. He recast its ideas in more accessible form in the *Enquiries*, but his reputation for religious scepticism cost him professorships at Edinburgh and Glasgow.

Fame came instead from history. His six-volume *History of England* was a bestseller for a century. As secretary to the British embassy in Paris in the 1760s he was lionised in the salons as "le bon David". He brought Jean-Jacques Rousseau to England in 1766, a kindness that ended in a famously public quarrel when the paranoid Rousseau turned on him. He was a close friend of **Adam Smith**. Dying of an abdominal illness in 1776, Hume remained so cheerful and unafraid that the biographer James Boswell, who visited hoping to see an unbeliever's deathbed terror, left disturbed. He arranged for his *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* to be published after his death.

## Key ideas

Hume aimed to build a "science of man" on the experimental method of Newton, investigating how the human mind actually works.

**Impressions and ideas.** All the contents of the mind are either **impressions**, vivid sensations and feelings, or **ideas**, fainter copies of them. To test whether a philosophical term means anything, ask: from what impression is it derived?

**Causation and habit.** We never observe a necessary connection between cause and effect, only that one kind of event is constantly followed by another. Our expectation that the future will resemble the past comes not from reason but from **custom or habit**. This is the famous **problem of induction**.

**The self as a bundle.** When Hume looked inward for his "self", he found only a succession of perceptions: heat, cold, love, hatred. The self is "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions."

**Reason and the passions.** Reason alone cannot move us to act; it only tells us how to get what we already want. "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions."

**Morality from sentiment.** Moral distinctions come from feelings of approval and disapproval, shaped by sympathy with others, not from reason. Hume also observed that writers slide from statements about what *is* to claims about what *ought* to be without justification, the "is–ought" problem.

**Religion.** Hume argued that testimony can never rationally establish a miracle, and in the *Dialogues* he dismantled the argument from design, showing how weak the analogy between the universe and a human artefact really is.

## Influence and legacy

Hume is often regarded as the greatest philosopher to have written in English. Immanuel Kant credited him with interrupting his "dogmatic slumber" and set out to answer his challenge, transforming philosophy in the process. Hume's ethics influenced Adam Smith and the utilitarians, his empiricism inspired the logical positivists of the twentieth century, and his naturalistic picture of the mind anticipates modern cognitive science. The problem of induction remains unsolved.
