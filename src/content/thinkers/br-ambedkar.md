## Start here

Imagine you are a small, thirsty boy at school. It's a hot day. Other children walk to the water tap and drink. You can't. You must wait until the school peon comes and pours water into your cupped hands from a height, so that your hands never touch the tap. If the peon isn't there, you don't drink. You sit on a gunny sack you bring from home, separate from the other children, because the classroom floor would be "polluted" by your touch. Teachers will not touch your notebooks.

You are not being punished for anything you did. You are being punished for what you were born.

The boy's name was **Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar**. He later wrote, in a short memoir, a line that sums it up: "No peon, no water."

That boy grew up to earn a doctorate from **Columbia University** in New York and another from the **London School of Economics**, at a time when few Indians of any caste had done either. He became a barrister, an economist, a journalist, a labour leader, India's first Law Minister, and the chairman of the committee that drafted the **Constitution of India**. In his last year, he led hundreds of thousands of his people out of Hinduism and into **Buddhism** in a single day.

He asked a question that India still has not fully answered:

**Can a society be free if it is built on inequality by birth?**

He argued that political freedom, the vote, independence from Britain, would mean little if social life remained a ladder of caste, with some born at the top to be revered and others born at the bottom to be despised. He told the Constituent Assembly that on 26 January 1950, India was entering "a life of contradictions".

By the end of this page, you will understand how he thought, why he clashed with Gandhi, what he built into the Constitution you live under, and why his ideas are not just for Dalits, but for anyone who believes in liberty, equality and fraternity.

## The world he was born into

### Caste: a ladder without a staircase

To understand Ambedkar, you need to understand the system he spent his life fighting: **caste**.

Hindu society was traditionally divided into four **varnas** (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra), and within them thousands of **jatis** (castes), each tied to a traditional occupation, each marrying only within itself. Below and outside the varnas were the people considered "**untouchable**", today called **Dalits** (meaning "broken" or "oppressed", a name they chose for themselves).

Untouchables were assigned the work considered most polluting: removing dead animals, tanning leather, cleaning latrines, sweeping. In many places they could not:

- draw water from public wells and tanks;
- enter temples;
- walk on certain streets, or let their shadows fall on upper-caste people;
- own land, or wear certain clothes and ornaments;
- go to school.

These rules were justified by religious texts, especially the ***Manusmriti*** (the "Laws of Manu"), which prescribed harsh punishments for Shudras and outcastes who dared to learn sacred texts or claim equality.

### The Mahars of Maharashtra

Ambedkar was born into the **Mahar** caste, one of the largest untouchable communities in Maharashtra. Mahars traditionally served as village servants: messengers, watchmen, removers of dead cattle. Many had served in the **British Indian Army**, which had recruited them in large numbers; in the army, they could gain education, pay and some dignity. Ambedkar's father and grandfather were soldiers.

### Reformers before him

Ambedkar did not start from nothing. In Maharashtra, **Jyotirao Phule** (1827–1890) and **Savitribai Phule** had opened schools for girls and Dalits and attacked Brahmin domination. In the south, **Narayana Guru** and later **Periyar** fought caste. But the mainstream Indian national movement was dominated by upper-caste leaders, many of whom saw caste as a social question to be dealt with after political freedom.

### The empire and the Constitution

By Ambedkar's youth, the British were slowly allowing Indians a share of power through reforms in 1909, 1919 and 1935. A key question was **representation**: who would speak for which group in these new legislatures? This is where Ambedkar would fight one of his greatest battles.

## The life, as a story

### A soldier's son

Bhimrao was born on **14 April 1891** at **Mhow**, a British army cantonment in central India (now in Madhya Pradesh), the fourteenth child of **Ramji Maloji Sakpal**, a subedar (non-commissioned officer) in the army, and **Bhimabai**. His father was a devout follower of the Bhakti saint **Kabir**, and a strict believer in education.

The family later moved to **Satara** and then **Bombay**. In school, Bhimrao suffered every humiliation untouchability could inflict: the gunny sack, the water from a height, teachers refusing to touch him. Once, travelling with his brothers to visit their father, they were left stranded when a cart driver discovered their caste; on the way, no one would give them water.

A kind **Brahmin teacher**, according to the usual account, gave the boy his own surname, **Ambedkar**, in place of the family's village name (Ambavadekar), which would have revealed his caste.

He married **Ramabai** in **1906**, when he was about fifteen and she about nine, as was common at the time.

### The first Mahar graduate

He was a brilliant student. In **1912**, he graduated from **Elphinstone College**, Bombay, in economics and political science, one of the very first untouchables in India to do so. The reformist ruler of **Baroda**, **Sayajirao Gaekwad III**, gave him a scholarship to study abroad.

### New York and London

In **1913**, he went to **Columbia University** in New York. For the first time in his life, he could eat, drink, sit and study anywhere, like anyone else. He studied economics, sociology, history, philosophy and anthropology, and was deeply influenced by the philosopher **John Dewey**, who taught that democracy is not just a form of government but a way of life, a way of living together as equals.

In **1916**, he presented a paper at Columbia, "**Castes in India**", arguing that the essence of caste was **endogamy**: marrying only within one's group. He earned an MA (1915) and, later, a PhD from Columbia.

In **1916**, he moved to **London** to study at the **London School of Economics** and at **Gray's Inn** for the bar. His scholarship ran out, and he had to return to India in **1917**, but he later returned to London and completed a DSc in economics (his thesis was published as ***The Problem of the Rupee***, 1923) and was called to the bar.

### "No room in the inn"

Back in India in 1917, he joined the service of the Baroda state, as part of his scholarship agreement. No one would rent him a room. He stayed at a Parsi inn under a false name; when his identity was discovered, a mob of men with sticks threw him out. He sat under a tree, weeping. Clerks in his office threw files at his desk so as not to touch him. He left Baroda within weeks.

He later wrote that these experiences taught him that for a Hindu, an untouchable is an untouchable, whatever his degrees.

### The organiser

In the **1920s**, Ambedkar became a teacher, lawyer, journalist and organiser. He started newspapers in Marathi: ***Mooknayak*** ("Leader of the Voiceless", 1920), ***Bahishkrit Bharat*** (1927) and others. He founded the **Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha** (1924), an organisation for the welfare of the "excluded", whose call to Dalits is often summed up as "**Educate, Agitate, Organise**".

### Mahad: water and fire

In **1927**, the town of **Mahad** in Maharashtra had a public tank, the **Chavdar Tale**, which, by law, was open to all, but in practice Dalits were not allowed to touch its water. On **20 March 1927**, Ambedkar led thousands of Dalits to the tank, and they drank from it. It was a simple act, like Gandhi's salt, with enormous meaning: claiming a basic human right.

Upper-caste Hindus retaliated: they attacked Dalits and "purified" the tank with cow dung and cow urine.

On **25 December 1927**, at Mahad, Ambedkar and his followers publicly **burned the *Manusmriti***, the law book that sanctified their degradation. It was a declaration of war on the religious foundations of caste.

In **1930**, he led a satyagraha for entry into the **Kalaram temple** in **Nashik**, which lasted years without success. He began to conclude that untouchables would never be accepted as equals within Hinduism.

### The Round Table and the Poona Pact

In **1930–32**, Ambedkar attended the **Round Table Conferences** in London as a representative of the Depressed Classes. He argued that untouchables were a distinct group, with distinct interests, who needed their own political representation; otherwise caste Hindus, who controlled every party, would always choose "safe" Dalit representatives who would not challenge them.

In **August 1932**, the British Prime Minister announced the **Communal Award**, granting the Depressed Classes **separate electorates** (Dalits would elect their own representatives) for a period. Gandhi, in prison, began a **fast unto death** against it, saying it would divide Hindus forever.

Ambedkar faced an impossible choice. If Gandhi died, there might be violence against Dalits across India, and he would be blamed. On **24 September 1932**, he signed the **Poona Pact**, giving up separate electorates in exchange for a larger number of **reserved seats** in general constituencies, where Dalit candidates would be elected by all voters. Gandhi ended his fast.

Ambedkar later said bitterly that the pact had been forced on him by coercion, and that Dalit representatives elected by caste-Hindu majorities would be the "tools" of others. This debate about representation continues today.

### "I will not die a Hindu"

In **1935**, at **Yeola**, Ambedkar made a shocking announcement:

"Though I was born a Hindu, I solemnly assure you that I will not die as a Hindu."

He spent the next twenty-one years considering which religion to embrace.

### Annihilation of Caste

In **1936**, a Hindu reform group in Lahore, the **Jat-Pat-Todak Mandal** (Society for the Abolition of Caste), invited Ambedkar to give its presidential address. When they read his draft, they were horrified by its attack on the Hindu scriptures and asked him to remove parts. He refused. The event was cancelled. He published the speech himself as ***Annihilation of Caste***, one of the most powerful texts in Indian history. Gandhi replied to it in his journal *Harijan*; Ambedkar replied to Gandhi.

### Labour, law and the war

In **1936**, he founded the **Independent Labour Party**, campaigning for workers and peasants of all castes. From **1942 to 1946**, he served as the **Labour Member** of the Viceroy's Executive Council, and pushed through important labour reforms, including the reduction of the working day in factories to eight hours, and provisions for women workers.

### Architect of the Constitution

When India became independent in **1947**, Nehru invited Ambedkar, a fierce critic of the Congress, to join the first cabinet as **Law Minister**. When the Constituent Assembly set up its **Drafting Committee** on **29 August 1947**, he became its **Chairman**.

Over the next two years, he steered the draft through the Assembly, defending it clause by clause in long debates. The Constitution, adopted on **26 November 1949** and in force from **26 January 1950**:

- abolished untouchability and made its practice an offence (**Article 17**);
- guaranteed equality before law and prohibited discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth (**Articles 14 and 15**);
- allowed special provisions and **reservations** in education, jobs and legislatures for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes;
- gave every adult the vote, regardless of caste, sex, property or education.

In his final speech to the Assembly, on **25 November 1949**, he warned of the dangers ahead (we'll look at it below).

### The Hindu Code Bill

As Law Minister, Ambedkar drafted the **Hindu Code Bill**, which would have reformed Hindu personal law: giving women rights of inheritance and divorce, allowing inter-caste marriage, and banning polygamy. It faced fierce opposition from conservative Hindus, including within the Congress. Nehru delayed it. In **1951**, Ambedkar **resigned** from the cabinet, citing the bill's stalling among his reasons. The reforms were passed later, in separate acts (1955–56), in a weaker form.

### Into Buddhism

In his last years, Ambedkar's health was failing (he had diabetes and other illnesses). His first wife, Ramabai, had died in 1935; in **1948** he married **Dr Sharada Kabir** (later **Savita Ambedkar**), a doctor.

On **14 October 1956**, at **Nagpur**, at a site now called **Deekshabhoomi**, he formally converted to **Buddhism**, and then administered the conversion to his followers in a single ceremony. Estimates of the crowd that converted with him range from about 200,000 to half a million. He gave them **twenty-two vows**, including the rejection of Hindu gods and rituals, belief in the equality of all human beings, and the practice of the Buddha's ethics.

He died seven weeks later, on **6 December 1956**, in Delhi, aged sixty-five. His final book, ***The Buddha and His Dhamma***, was published the next year. His death anniversary is observed as **Mahaparinirvan Diwas**, and millions visit **Chaityabhoomi** in Mumbai, where he was cremated. In **1990**, he was posthumously awarded the **Bharat Ratna**.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Caste is graded inequality

**The idea in one line:** Caste is not just a division of society into groups; it is a ladder of graded inequality, where every caste looks down on those below and is looked down on by those above.

Ambedkar's most important insight about caste is that it is **graded**. It is not a simple division between oppressors and oppressed, like masters and slaves. It is a ladder with many rungs. Each caste (except the very top) is humiliated by those above it, but also gets to look down on those below it.

He described this as an ascending scale of reverence and a descending scale of contempt.

Why does this matter? Because it makes caste remarkably stable. Every group, except the very bottom, has someone to look down on, so it has a stake in keeping the system going. The lower castes are divided among themselves, and so cannot unite against those at the top.

Think about how this works in daily life: a caste that is itself discriminated against may still refuse to marry into, or eat with, a caste it considers lower. The system recruits almost everyone into maintaining it.

**Why it matters:** This explains why caste has survived thousands of years, conquests, reform movements and the Constitution. It is not maintained by one group alone; it is maintained by the whole graded structure. Understanding this changes how you think about fighting it.

**A common misunderstanding:** Caste is not simply "the same as class". Class is about wealth and can change in one generation; caste is about birth and is fixed. A rich Dalit is still a Dalit in the eyes of many; a poor Brahmin is still a Brahmin.

### Idea 2: A division of labourers

**The idea in one line:** Caste is not a natural or efficient division of labour; it is a division of labourers, assigning people to work by birth, not by ability or choice.

Defenders of caste often said it was just a **division of labour**, like in any society: some people farm, some trade, some teach. Ambedkar demolished this argument in *Annihilation of Caste*:

"Caste System is not merely division of labour. It is also a division of labourers."

He made three points:

1. In modern societies, the division of labour is based on **choice and ability**. In caste, it is based on **birth**. A Brahmin child's occupation is fixed before he can show any talent.
2. Caste divides labourers into **watertight compartments** and ranks them one above the other, which no civilised division of labour does.
3. Caste is economically **inefficient**: it prevents people from moving to occupations that suit them and that the economy needs, and it makes people do work they hate, without interest.

Think about a child born in a family of manual scavengers. Under caste, she is expected to do that work, whatever her talents, perhaps she could be a doctor, an engineer, a scientist. Caste wastes human talent on a massive scale.

**Why it matters:** This argument is economic as well as moral. A society that assigns jobs by birth wastes human potential and stays poor. Ambedkar, a trained economist, saw caste as a barrier to India's development.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ambedkar was not against people doing traditional work if they chose it. He was against the system that forced them into it by birth and attached shame to it.

### Idea 3: Annihilation, not reform

**The idea in one line:** Caste cannot be reformed away while the scriptures that sanctify it are held sacred; it must be annihilated at its root.

Many Hindu reformers, Gandhi among them, wanted to remove **untouchability** while keeping the Hindu social order, perhaps in a purified form of **varna**. Ambedkar argued this was impossible.

Why? Because Hindus followed caste not because they were wicked but because they were **religious**: they believed caste was commanded by the Shastras (scriptures). As long as people believed the scriptures were divine and infallible, they would keep caste. So, he wrote, the real enemy was the authority of the Shastras themselves. People must be taught that the Shastras were not divine and could be wrong. "Caste is a notion, it is a state of the mind," he wrote; the destruction of caste requires a "notional change".

He also argued that the best remedy for caste was **inter-caste marriage**, because caste is held together by endogamy. Only mixing of blood, he wrote, could create the feeling of being kith and kin.

And he rejected **varna**, even an idealised version, because, he said, once you sort people into four fixed categories, it will inevitably become birth-based and hierarchical. You cannot have a "good" version of a ladder.

**Why it matters:** This is a radical argument: social reform requires confronting religious authority. It remains controversial, but it explains why, decades after the Constitution banned untouchability, caste remains, sustained by belief, custom and marriage.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ambedkar was not against religion as such. He believed people need religion, a moral foundation. He was against a religion that sanctified inequality. That is why he eventually chose Buddhism, rather than atheism.

### Idea 4: Social democracy, not just political democracy

**The idea in one line:** Political democracy (one person, one vote) cannot survive unless it rests on social democracy, a way of life based on liberty, equality and fraternity.

This is the heart of his famous final speech to the Constituent Assembly on **25 November 1949**:

"On the 26th of January 1950, we are going to enter into a life of contradictions. In politics we will have equality and in social and economic life we will have inequality. In politics we will be recognizing the principle of one man one vote and one vote one value. In our social and economic life, we shall, by reason of our social and economic structure, continue to deny the principle of one man one value. How long shall we continue to live this life of contradictions?"

He warned that if this contradiction were not resolved, those who suffered from inequality would blow up the structure of political democracy that the Assembly had so laboriously built.

He defined **social democracy** as a way of life that recognises **liberty, equality and fraternity** as principles of life, and said these three could not be separated: liberty without equality would produce the supremacy of the few over the many; equality without liberty would kill individual initiative; and without **fraternity**, a sense of common brotherhood among all Indians, neither liberty nor equality could become a natural course of things.

Like his teacher Dewey, he believed that democracy is not merely a form of government but a form of **associated living**, a way of treating fellow human beings.

**Why it matters:** This is perhaps the single most important warning about Indian democracy. India has had elections for over seventy years; but has it achieved social democracy? Caste violence, honour killings, discrimination in housing and employment, manual scavenging: these are signs of the contradiction Ambedkar described.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ambedkar was not saying that political democracy is worthless. He fought hard for universal adult franchise and constitutional rights. He was saying that it is not **enough**, and that it will not survive on its own.

### Idea 5: Constitutional morality and the grammar of anarchy

**The idea in one line:** Democracy depends on respect for constitutional methods and institutions; once those exist, unconstitutional methods like civil disobedience become "the grammar of anarchy".

In the same speech, Ambedkar warned against three dangers.

**First**, he said that Indians must hold fast to **constitutional methods** of achieving social and economic objectives, and abandon the bloody methods of revolution, and also the methods of **civil disobedience, non-cooperation and satyagraha**. When there were no constitutional methods, these might have been justified; but now, he said, they were "nothing but the Grammar of Anarchy".

This is a direct challenge to Gandhi's legacy. Ambedkar believed that in a democracy, change must come through law, argument and elections, not through mass pressure that bypasses institutions.

**Second**, he warned against **hero-worship**:

"In India, Bhakti or what may be called the path of devotion or hero-worship, plays a part in its politics unequalled in magnitude by the part it plays in the politics of any other country in the world. Bhakti in religion may be a road to the salvation of the soul. But in politics, Bhakti or hero-worship is a sure road to degradation and to eventual dictatorship."

**Third**, he warned against being content with mere political democracy (see Idea 4).

He also spoke, earlier in the Assembly, of **constitutional morality**: the habit of respecting the constitution's forms and spirit. He quoted the historian Grote, and warned that it is "not a natural sentiment. It has to be cultivated." He added that democracy in India was only "a top-dressing on an Indian soil, which is essentially undemocratic".

**Why it matters:** Indian courts have repeatedly drawn on "constitutional morality" in landmark judgements, for instance in the Sabarimala temple-entry case and the decriminalisation of homosexuality in **Navtej Singh Johar** (2018). And the warning against hero-worship applies to every political leader and every fan culture, of any party.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ambedkar's rejection of satyagraha is sometimes read as hostility to all protest. He meant that in a functioning constitutional democracy, the primary means of change should be constitutional. He himself had led satyagrahas at Mahad and Nashik when no constitutional route existed.

### Idea 6: State socialism and economic justice

**The idea in one line:** Political rights mean little without economic security; the state must actively ensure economic justice.

Ambedkar was a trained economist. In ***States and Minorities*** (1947), a memorandum on the Constitution, he proposed a form of **state socialism**: key industries and agricultural land to be owned by the state, with land leased to village cooperatives, to prevent exploitation by landlords and capitalists. He wanted this written into the Constitution so that no future legislature could undo it.

The Constituent Assembly did not adopt this, but some of his concerns went into the **Directive Principles of State Policy**, which direct the state to secure adequate livelihood for all, prevent concentration of wealth and protect workers.

As Labour Member (1942–46), he introduced the eight-hour working day, maternity benefits for women workers, and employment exchanges.

**Why it matters:** Ambedkar saw that caste and class are intertwined: most Dalits were landless labourers. Social equality required economic equality too. Debates about land reform, minimum wages, labour codes and welfare schemes continue his concerns.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ambedkar's socialism was not Marxist. He wanted economic equality achieved by constitutional and democratic means, not by violent revolution or dictatorship.

### Idea 7: Women's equality and the Hindu Code Bill

**The idea in one line:** A society's progress is measured by the progress of its women, and women must have equal rights in marriage, property and inheritance.

Ambedkar is often quoted as saying that he measured the progress of a community by the degree of progress women had achieved. He linked caste and gender: caste was maintained through control of women's marriages, and the subordination of women was built into the same scriptures that sanctified caste.

His **Hindu Code Bill** would have given Hindu women the right to inherit property equally, the right to divorce, and ended polygamy for Hindu men. It also recognised inter-caste marriage and adoption across castes. When it was stalled by opposition, he resigned from the cabinet in 1951.

**Why it matters:** Many of these reforms became law in **1955–56**; the right of daughters to equal shares in ancestral property came only with the **2005 amendment** to the Hindu Succession Act. Ambedkar was decades ahead. Feminist and Dalit feminist movements today draw on his work.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ambedkar is sometimes seen only as a Dalit leader. He was also one of the most important champions of women's rights in modern India, for women of all castes.

### Idea 8: Buddhism as a religion of reason and equality

**The idea in one line:** People need a moral religion; the Buddha's Dhamma, based on reason, compassion and equality, is the best foundation for a just society.

After twenty-one years of searching, Ambedkar chose **Buddhism**. Why?

- The Buddha **rejected caste**: in his Sangha, people of all castes were equal.
- Buddhism is based on **reason** and experience, not on revelation or the authority of scriptures; the Buddha told his followers to test his teachings.
- It teaches **compassion** (*karuna*) and **prajna** (wisdom), and its ethics are focused on human relations, not rituals.
- It is an **Indian** religion, so conversion would not cut Dalits off from Indian culture.

He reinterpreted Buddhism in ***The Buddha and His Dhamma***, emphasising its social and ethical message: the Dhamma is about the relationship between human beings, about justice and equality, rather than about the afterlife or rebirth. His followers' form of Buddhism is often called **Navayana** ("new vehicle").

In his essay ***Buddha or Karl Marx***, he compared the two. Both, he said, wanted to end exploitation and suffering. But Marx's way of violence and dictatorship would destroy liberty and fraternity, while the Buddha's way of persuasion, moral change and democracy would preserve them.

**Why it matters:** The 1956 conversion, by hundreds of thousands of people in one day, was one of the largest religious conversions in history. Today there are several million Ambedkarite Buddhists in India, especially in Maharashtra. It showed that a people could change their religious identity as an act of self-respect and liberation.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ambedkar's Buddhism is sometimes seen as purely political. It was also deeply ethical and personal. He believed society needed a moral foundation, and that only a religion of reason and equality could provide one.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### The Buddha: the teacher at last

The **Buddha** became Ambedkar's final teacher. Ambedkar saw him as a social revolutionary who rejected caste, ritual and blind belief, and preached reason, compassion and equality. His *The Buddha and His Dhamma* reinterprets the Buddha's life and teaching for the modern world. Some Buddhist scholars have criticised his reading as too political, especially his doubts about rebirth and karma in their traditional sense.

### Gandhi: the great clash

The conflict between Ambedkar and **Gandhi** is one of the most important debates in Indian history. They disagreed on almost everything about caste:

- **Separate electorates:** Ambedkar wanted them, so that Dalits could elect real representatives; Gandhi opposed them, fearing they would divide Hindus. The Poona Pact settled it, with Ambedkar feeling coerced.
- **Varna:** Gandhi defended varna (for much of his life) as a division of duties; Ambedkar said varna and caste were inseparable.
- **Method:** Gandhi wanted a "change of heart" among caste Hindus; Ambedkar wanted rights guaranteed by law and power in Dalits' own hands.
- **The word "Harijan":** Gandhi's name for untouchables; Ambedkar and many Dalits rejected it as patronising.

In ***What Congress and Gandhi Have Done to the Untouchables*** (1945), Ambedkar delivered a scathing critique of Gandhi and the Congress.

Yet in independent India, they ended up on the same side of the Constitution, with Gandhi's heirs inviting Ambedkar to draft it.

### Marx: shared goal, different path

Ambedkar respected **Karl Marx**'s analysis of exploitation, but in *Buddha or Karl Marx* he rejected Marx's methods of violence and the dictatorship of the proletariat. He also criticised Indian communists for treating caste as secondary to class, and for being led, as he saw it, largely by upper-caste men.

Imagine them talking:

*Marx:* Abolish private property, and caste will disappear with the class system.

*Ambedkar:* Will it? The workers in Bombay mills would not drink water from a Dalit's hand, though they all owned nothing. Caste is not only property; it is in the mind, in marriage, in religion.

*Marx:* Then the revolution will sweep it away.

*Ambedkar:* A revolution that kills liberty and fraternity will build another ladder. I prefer the Buddha's way: change the mind, and secure equality by law.

### Vivekananda and the reformers

Ambedkar respected sincere reformers but argued that those who condemned untouchability while praising varna, including, implicitly, **Vivekananda**, were trying to remove the fruit while keeping the tree.

### Nietzsche: the superman and Manu

Ambedkar noticed that **Friedrich Nietzsche** had praised the *Manusmriti* for its caste order. He argued that Manu's philosophy, like Nietzsche's, was a philosophy of the superman: the elevation of a few at the expense of the many. For Ambedkar, this was exactly what was wrong with both.

### Dewey: democracy as a way of life

His teacher at Columbia, **John Dewey**, taught that democracy is a mode of associated living, not just a system of government. Ambedkar said he owed his whole intellectual life to Dewey, and Dewey's ideas run through his definition of social democracy.

### Later voices

- **Dalit Panthers** (1972) and the Dalit literature movement drew on his radical spirit.
- **Kanshi Ram** and the **Bahujan Samaj Party** built political power for Dalits and backward castes in his name.
- Scholars across the world, including at Columbia, have recognised him as a major political thinker. Statues of Ambedkar, holding the Constitution, stand in villages and cities across India.

## See it around you

**The Constitution.** Every time you read Article 14 (equality before law), Article 15 (no discrimination), Article 17 (abolition of untouchability) or the provisions for reservations, you are reading Ambedkar's work.

**Reservations.** Debates over reservations in education, jobs and legislatures, over the "creamy layer", over extending reservations to new groups, all turn on questions Ambedkar raised: what is equality, and how is it achieved when people start from unequal positions?

**Caste violence.** News of honour killings, Dalit grooms attacked for riding horses, violence over temple entry, and manual scavengers dying in sewers shows that the "life of contradictions" continues.

**Matrimonial ads.** Columns of caste-specific matrimonial ads, and "caste no bar" as an exception, show that endogamy, the essence of caste in Ambedkar's analysis, is very much alive.

**Hero-worship in politics.** Political rallies where leaders are treated like gods, devotees who will hear no criticism: Ambedkar's warning that bhakti in politics leads to dictatorship applies to every party.

**Ambedkar Jayanti.** On 14 April, millions celebrate his birthday with processions, music and readings; on 14 October (Dhammachakra Pravartan Din) Buddhists gather at Deekshabhoomi.

**Courts and constitutional morality.** When the Supreme Court cites "constitutional morality" to strike down discriminatory practices, it uses Ambedkar's phrase.

## Use it

**1. Look at your own ladder.** Honestly ask: whom do I look down on, and why? Caste, class, region, language, skin colour? Graded inequality survives because almost everyone has someone below them. Refuse your rung.

**2. Educate, agitate, organise.** Whatever cause you care about, learn about it deeply, speak up, and work with others. Individual goodwill is not enough; organisation creates change.

**3. Test traditions by liberty, equality and fraternity.** When a custom or rule is defended as "our tradition", ask: does it respect each person's liberty? Does it treat people as equals? Does it build brotherhood? If not, it's not worth keeping.

**4. Don't worship leaders.** Admire politicians, celebrities and gurus if you like, but never surrender your judgement to them. Ambedkar himself warned against hero-worship, including of himself.

**5. Marry, befriend and eat across lines.** Ambedkar believed inter-caste marriage was the real solvent of caste. Even if marriage isn't your decision now, friendships and shared meals across caste lines break the walls.

**6. Use the Constitution.** Read the Preamble and Fundamental Rights. Know your rights and the rights of others. Ambedkar believed that law, used actively, is a tool of liberation.

**7. Value economic justice.** Notice who does the dirtiest, most dangerous work in your city, and who is paid least for it. Ask why.

**8. Read him directly.** Read *Annihilation of Caste*. It is short, sharp and still burning.

## Where he went wrong

**1. The limits of law.** Ambedkar placed great faith in the Constitution and the state to end caste. Seventy-five years later, untouchability is illegal but caste discrimination persists. Critics argue that he underestimated how deeply caste is embedded in society and how laws can be ignored or misused.

**2. Rejection of satyagraha.** His description of civil disobedience as "the grammar of anarchy" in a democracy has been criticised by those who argue that peaceful mass protest remains an essential democratic tool when institutions fail or are captured.

**3. The Poona Pact and representation.** Some critics argue that the system of reserved seats in joint electorates, which he accepted under pressure, has indeed produced Dalit representatives dependent on non-Dalit voters and parties, as he feared, and that the question of representation remains unresolved.

**4. Reading of Buddhism.** His reinterpretation of Buddhism, downplaying rebirth and karma and emphasising social ethics, has been criticised by traditional Buddhists as a modern, political reading that departs from the canon.

**5. Tribal and other groups.** His focus was primarily on untouchable castes, especially in western India. Critics note that his writings gave less attention to Adivasis (tribal communities) and to the particular oppression of Dalit women by Dalit men.

**6. Harsh judgements.** In polemics he could be sweeping, as in some of his statements about Muslims during the Partition debates (in *Thoughts on Pakistan*, 1940) and about villages, which he called "a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism", though this comment came from bitter experience of how villages treated Dalits.

**7. Hero-worship of Ambedkar.** Ironically, the man who warned against bhakti in politics has himself become an object of devotion, with statues, temples and political parties claiming him. He would likely have been the first to object.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have known Ambedkar as the face on posters holding the Constitution, as "the Dalit leader", or as one of the founding fathers of India, honoured once a year. You may have thought of caste as a thing of the past, abolished by law.

**Now,** you can see a thinker of world stature, an economist, lawyer, sociologist and philosopher, who showed that caste is a ladder of graded inequality held together by belief and marriage, that political democracy cannot survive without social democracy, that liberty, equality and fraternity stand or fall together, and that hero-worship in politics leads to dictatorship. You can see why he clashed with Gandhi, why he burned the Manusmriti, and why he chose the Buddha. And you can see the contradictions of Indian life that he named in 1949 and that still remain.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A matrimonial ad specifying caste** looks like the engine of the system Ambedkar analysed.
- **A news story of caste violence** looks like the life of contradictions, still unresolved.
- **A leader worshipped like a god** sets off his warning about bhakti in politics.
- **The Constitution** looks like a living tool, not a museum piece.
- **Your own sense of superiority** over anyone, by birth, starts to look like the bottom rung of a very old ladder.
- **"Educate, agitate, organise"** becomes a plan for any struggle against injustice.

A boy who was not allowed to touch the school tap grew up to write the law of the land. He told India that it must choose between a life of contradictions and a life of liberty, equality and fraternity. India is still choosing.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956), economist, jurist, social reformer, Dalit leader, India's first Law Minister and chairman of the Constitution's Drafting Committee; Bharat Ratna (1990).
- **Context:** the caste system and untouchability, Mahar soldiers, Phule's reform movement, British constitutional reforms, Gandhi's national movement.
- **Life:** humiliations at school; Columbia (PhD) and LSE (DSc); Baroda humiliation; *Mooknayak*; Mahad Satyagraha and the burning of the *Manusmriti* (1927); Round Table Conferences; Poona Pact (1932); "I will not die a Hindu" (1935); *Annihilation of Caste* (1936); Labour Member (1942–46); Constitution (1947–49); resigned over the Hindu Code Bill (1951); converted to Buddhism (1956).
- **Graded inequality:** caste as a ladder of reverence above, contempt below.
- **Division of labourers:** caste assigns work by birth, not ability.
- **Annihilation:** caste cannot end while the scriptures that sanctify it are held sacred; inter-caste marriage as the solvent.
- **Social democracy:** liberty, equality, fraternity as one; the "life of contradictions".
- **Constitutional morality:** constitutional methods over the "grammar of anarchy"; against hero-worship.
- **Economic justice:** state socialism, labour reforms.
- **Women:** Hindu Code Bill.
- **Buddhism:** a religion of reason and equality; *Buddha or Karl Marx*.
- **Criticisms:** limits of law, rejection of satyagraha, representation, reading of Buddhism, gaps on Adivasis, harsh polemics, his own deification.

**Key terms**

- **Dalit:** "broken" or "oppressed", the name formerly untouchable castes chose for themselves.
- **Graded inequality:** Ambedkar's description of caste as a hierarchy in which each level looks down on those below.
- **Endogamy:** marriage only within one's own group, the essence of caste in Ambedkar's analysis.
- **Manusmriti:** the ancient law book that sanctified caste hierarchy; burned at Mahad in 1927.
- **Separate electorates:** a system in which a group elects its own representatives.
- **Poona Pact:** the 1932 agreement replacing separate electorates for Dalits with reserved seats.
- **Social democracy:** a way of life based on liberty, equality and fraternity.
- **Constitutional morality:** respect for the forms and spirit of the constitution.
- **Grammar of anarchy:** Ambedkar's term for unconstitutional methods in a democracy.
- **Navayana:** Ambedkar's reinterpretation of Buddhism, emphasising social ethics.
- **Deekshabhoomi:** the site in Nagpur of the 1956 mass conversion.
