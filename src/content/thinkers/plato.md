## Life

Plato was born around 428 BCE into one of Athens' most distinguished aristocratic families. His relatives included Critias and Charmides, leaders of the oligarchic Thirty Tyrants. As a young man he expected a career in politics. Instead he fell under the spell of Socrates, and the older man's execution in 399 BCE turned him away from public life. In his *Seventh Letter* (whose authenticity is debated) he recalls concluding that no existing city was well governed, and that justice would come only when philosophy and political power coincided.

After Socrates' death Plato travelled, probably to Megara, Egypt and southern Italy, where he met Pythagorean philosophers. Around 387 BCE he founded the **Academy** in a grove outside Athens, often called the first institution of higher learning in the West. Its students studied mathematics, astronomy, dialectic and politics, and it endured in some form for centuries. Plato made three journeys to Syracuse in Sicily, hoping to educate its rulers in philosophy. They ended badly, at one point with his own brief captivity. He died in Athens around 348 BCE, reportedly still writing.

## Key ideas

Plato wrote dialogues, not treatises. Socrates is usually the main speaker, and conclusions are often provisional or left open. Scholars traditionally divide the works into early "Socratic" dialogues, middle dialogues developing Plato's own doctrines, and late dialogues that criticise and refine them.

**The Theory of Forms.** The objects we perceive are changing, imperfect and many: many beautiful things, many just acts. Plato argued that there must also be the Beautiful itself, the Just itself, eternal and unchanging, which particular things "participate in" or imitate. Only the Forms can be truly *known*; the sensible world gives us mere opinion. The highest Form, the **Form of the Good**, makes everything else intelligible, as the sun makes things visible.

**The Allegory of the Cave.** In the *Republic*, prisoners chained in a cave take the shadows on the wall for reality. A freed prisoner climbs painfully into daylight, sees the sun, and returns to free the others, who resist and threaten him. It is an image of education as the "turning around" of the soul, and of the philosopher's dangerous duty.

**The tripartite soul and justice.** The soul has three parts: reason, spirit (*thumos*) and appetite. A person is just when each part does its own work and reason rules. The ideal city mirrors the soul, with guardians ruling, auxiliaries defending and producers providing, and its rulers are philosophers trained for decades.

**Recollection and immortality.** In the *Meno* and *Phaedo*, Plato suggests that learning is really remembering truths the immortal soul knew before birth.

**Love.** In the *Symposium*, love (*erōs*) is a ladder: from attraction to one beautiful body, to all beauty, to beautiful souls, laws and knowledge, and finally to Beauty itself.

## Influence and legacy

Alfred North Whitehead famously remarked that the European philosophical tradition "consists of a series of footnotes to Plato". Aristotle studied at the Academy for twenty years before building his own system, largely in critique of the Forms. Neoplatonism, developed by Plotinus in the third century CE, shaped Christian theology through Augustine, and Islamic philosophy through al-Farabi and others. Renaissance Florence revived the Academy in name.

Plato's political ideas have been both admired and attacked. Karl Popper's *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) accused the *Republic* of laying the groundwork for totalitarianism. His questions about knowledge, mathematics, justice and education remain, as Whitehead suggested, the questions that philosophy keeps returning to.
