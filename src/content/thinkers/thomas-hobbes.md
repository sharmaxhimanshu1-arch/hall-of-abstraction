## Start here

Imagine that tomorrow morning, every government in your country simply disappears. No police. No courts. No army. No traffic signals that anyone has to obey. No bank that can be forced to pay you back. No one to call when someone breaks into your house.

What do you do?

Most people's first answer is cheerful: "We'd manage. People are basically good. We'd help each other."

Now think a little longer. You have a family to feed. The shops are running out of rice and medicine. Your neighbour is probably a good person, but you don't know what he'll do when his children are hungry. He doesn't know what you'll do either. You both start thinking about locking your doors, keeping a stick by the bed, maybe getting together with a few relatives to guard the lane. Other groups do the same. Someone with a gang decides to "protect" the market, for a price. Soon nobody plants crops, because the harvest might be stolen. Nobody builds anything that takes years, because nobody knows who'll be alive next year.

You have probably seen glimpses of this. In riots, when the police vanish for a few days. In news from countries where the state has collapsed. In films about the end of the world, where the first thing people do is fight over petrol.

An English philosopher in the seventeenth century looked hard at this picture and drew a frightening conclusion. Without a power strong enough to keep everyone in awe, he said, life would be "**solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short**". And therefore, he argued, almost any government, even a harsh one, is better than no government at all.

His name was **Thomas Hobbes**. He wrote one of the greatest and most disturbing books of political philosophy ever written, ***Leviathan***. He was the first modern thinker to ask, from scratch, without relying on God's command or tradition: *why should anyone obey a government at all?*

His answer still lies underneath every argument about law and order, police powers, emergency laws and "strong leaders". By the end of this page you will understand it, see where it is right, and see why almost every later thinker, from Locke to Rousseau to the makers of the Indian Constitution, spent their energy trying to keep his insight while escaping his conclusion.

## The world he was born into

### The Armada year

Hobbes was born in **1588**, the year Spain sent the great **Armada**, a fleet of some 130 ships, to invade England. Rumours of invasion terrified the country. Hobbes later joked, in a verse autobiography written in old age, that his mother was so frightened by the news that she gave birth to twins: himself and **fear**.

That joke is the key to his philosophy. Hobbes thought fear, especially fear of violent death, was the most powerful force in human life, and the foundation on which peace could be built.

### Religion and blood

Europe in Hobbes's lifetime was torn apart by **wars of religion**. Catholics and Protestants slaughtered each other, each convinced that God was on their side. The **Thirty Years' War** (1618–1648) devastated Germany; by some estimates, a large share of its population died from fighting, famine and disease.

In England, the quarrel was between the **King** and **Parliament**, tangled up with religion: the king's high-church Anglicans on one side, Puritans and other Protestant groups on the other. Each side claimed the authority of God and of the "ancient constitution".

### Civil war

In **1642**, the quarrel exploded into the **English Civil War**. For nearly a decade, Englishmen killed Englishmen. In **1649**, King **Charles I** was tried and beheaded in public, something unimaginable a generation earlier. For the next eleven years, England was ruled without a king, first as a republic and then under **Oliver Cromwell** as Lord Protector. In **1660**, the monarchy was restored under **Charles II**.

Hobbes lived through all of this, and wrote *Leviathan* while it was happening. Its central question was the one on everyone's mind: when men cannot agree about God, justice or the constitution, what can stop them killing each other?

### The new science

At the same time, a scientific revolution was under way. **Galileo** showed that motion could be described mathematically. **William Harvey** discovered the circulation of the blood. **Descartes** was trying to rebuild knowledge from first principles. Hobbes was fascinated. He wanted to do for **politics** what Galileo had done for physics: to start from simple principles about human beings and reason step by step to firm conclusions.

## The life, as a story

### A vicar's son from Malmesbury

Thomas Hobbes was born on **5 April 1588** in **Westport**, near **Malmesbury**, in Wiltshire. His father, also called Thomas, was a poorly educated country vicar who, according to the gossip-writer **John Aubrey** (Hobbes's friend and first biographer), got into a brawl with another clergyman at the church door and had to flee, abandoning his family. Thomas was raised and educated with help from a prosperous uncle, a glove-maker.

He was a gifted student of Latin and Greek, and at about fourteen translated a Greek tragedy into Latin verse. He went to **Magdalen Hall, Oxford**, where he found the old Aristotelian philosophy taught there dull and useless, and preferred reading maps and travel books.

### Tutor to the Cavendishes

In **1608**, he became tutor to the son of **William Cavendish**, later the **Earl of Devonshire**. It was the start of a lifelong connection: for most of his long life, Hobbes was a servant, tutor, secretary and friend of the Cavendish family. This gave him security, a great library, travel, and contact with the leading minds of Europe.

On tours of the continent with his young pupils, he met scientists and philosophers. He worked for a time as a secretary to **Francis Bacon**, taking down his thoughts in Latin.

### Thucydides, and a warning about democracy

His first major work was a translation of the Greek historian **Thucydides**' *History of the Peloponnesian War* (1629). Thucydides describes how democratic Athens, swayed by ambitious orators and popular passions, destroyed itself in war and civil strife. Hobbes said he translated it to show his countrymen the dangers of democracy and faction. It was a warning they did not heed.

### Falling in love with geometry

According to Aubrey's famous story, around the age of forty Hobbes was in a gentleman's library and saw a copy of **Euclid's *Elements*** open at a famous theorem. He read it and exclaimed, "By God, this is impossible!" Then he read the proof, which referred back to an earlier proof, and that to an earlier one, until he was convinced. "This made him in love with geometry."

What excited him was the **method**: starting from clear definitions and simple axioms, and reasoning step by step to conclusions that no one could deny. He decided to apply this method to everything, including human nature and politics.

In **1636**, in Italy, he visited **Galileo**, the great scientist, by then old and under house arrest by the Inquisition. Galileo's idea that everything in nature could be explained by **matter in motion** became the basis of Hobbes's whole philosophy.

### The first to flee

By **1640**, the conflict between King and Parliament was boiling over. Hobbes circulated a manuscript, ***The Elements of Law***, arguing that sovereign power must be absolute and undivided. It was read as a defence of the king. Fearing for his safety, Hobbes fled to **Paris** in late 1640. He later boasted that he was "the first of all that fled".

He stayed in Paris for eleven years, in the company of French scientists and philosophers, including **Marin Mersenne**, who connected scholars across Europe. He wrote a set of objections to **Descartes'** *Meditations* (1641), which Descartes published with his replies. The two men did not like each other.

In **1642** he published ***De Cive*** (*On the Citizen*), and in the late 1640s he became tutor in mathematics to the exiled **Prince of Wales**, the future **Charles II**, who was also living in Paris.

### Leviathan

In **1651**, two years after the execution of Charles I, Hobbes published his masterpiece in London: ***Leviathan, or the Matter, Forme and Power of a Common-Wealth Ecclesiasticall and Civill***.

The famous frontispiece shows a giant crowned figure rising over a peaceful landscape, holding a sword in one hand and a bishop's staff in the other. Look closely and you see that his body is made up of hundreds of tiny people. That giant is the **state**, the "Leviathan", named after a sea monster in the Bible's Book of Job, of which it is said: there is no power on earth to be compared with him.

The book delighted almost no one. Royalists were offended that Hobbes based the king's authority on a human contract rather than divine right, and seemed to say that subjects could switch allegiance to whoever actually protected them, including Cromwell. Clergymen of every kind were horrified by his materialism and his attacks on the Church's claims to power; they called him an atheist. Parliamentarians disliked his defence of absolute power.

Cut off from the exiled royal court, which now distrusted him, Hobbes returned to England in **1651–52** and submitted to Cromwell's government.

### Old age and quarrels

After the Restoration of **1660**, Charles II welcomed his old tutor back, gave him a pension, and enjoyed his wit, but Hobbes was never safe. In **1666**, after the Great Plague and the Great Fire of London, some in Parliament blamed God's anger at atheism, and a committee was asked to look into *Leviathan*. Hobbes, now in his late seventies, reportedly burned some of his papers and studied the law of heresy to see if he could be executed for it.

He spent years in bitter quarrels with mathematicians, especially **John Wallis** of Oxford, who exposed Hobbes's mistaken claims to have "squared the circle". He was forbidden to publish on politics and religion in England. His history of the civil war, ***Behemoth***, was published only after his death.

He remained astonishingly active. He played tennis into his seventies. At eighty-six, he translated Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* into English verse. He sang at night, alone in bed, to strengthen his lungs, according to Aubrey.

He died on **4 December 1679**, at the Cavendish house of **Hardwick Hall**, aged ninety-one, a remarkable age for his time, for a man who said he was born twin to fear.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Everything is matter in motion

**The idea in one line:** The universe, including human beings and their thoughts, consists of bodies moving according to physical laws.

Hobbes was a thorough **materialist**. He believed that everything that exists is a body, and that everything that happens is motion. Sensation, he said, is caused by the motion of external objects pressing on our sense organs. Imagination is "decaying sense", the fading echo of that motion. Thoughts are motions in the head. Desire is a motion toward something; aversion, a motion away.

Even the soul, for Hobbes, must be some kind of body, and talk of "incorporeal substance" (as in Descartes' mind) was, he said, as absurd as talking of a "round quadrangle".

Think of a modern neuroscientist who says that your feelings are chemical and electrical signals in your brain. Hobbes was saying something similar, three centuries earlier, with much less knowledge.

**Why it matters:** This view led Hobbes to treat human beings as natural machines, whose behaviour could be explained by causes, like any other machine. That made a **science of politics** possible: if you know how the parts move, you can design a structure that keeps them in balance.

**A common misunderstanding:** Materialism didn't make Hobbes an open atheist; he always said he believed in God (though a God who must also, somehow, be a body). His critics thought this was a disguise. Scholars still argue about what he really believed.

### Idea 2: Humans are roughly equal, and that is the problem

**The idea in one line:** Because people are roughly equal in strength and cunning, anyone can kill anyone, so everyone has reason to fear everyone.

You might think inequality causes conflict. Hobbes argued the opposite: **equality** does. He wrote that nature has made people so equal in body and mind that even the weakest has enough strength to kill the strongest, "either by secret machination, or by confederacy with others".

The strongest wrestler in the village can be poisoned by the smallest cook, or knifed in his sleep, or attacked by three friends together. No one is so strong that he can be safe without protection.

And from equality of ability, Hobbes said, comes **equality of hope**: since no one is naturally superior, everyone thinks they have as much right to things as anyone else. When two people want the same thing which they can't both have, they become enemies.

**Why it matters:** This is a striking democratic premise inside an authoritarian argument. Hobbes rejected the old idea (from Aristotle) that some people are natural rulers and others natural slaves. In the state of nature, all are equal, and that is precisely why everyone is in danger.

**A common misunderstanding:** Hobbes didn't say all people are equally talented. He said the differences are not great enough to let anyone feel secure. That is a claim about **vulnerability**, not about ability.

### Idea 3: Three causes of quarrel

**The idea in one line:** People fight for three main reasons: gain, safety and reputation.

In chapter 13 of *Leviathan*, Hobbes identified three principal causes of quarrel in human nature:

1. **Competition**: people attack others for **gain**, to take their land, goods, wives or cattle.
2. **Diffidence** (meaning distrust, fear): people attack others for **safety**. If I fear that you might attack me, the safest thing may be to strike first. Even peaceful people may reason this way.
3. **Glory**: people fight for **reputation**, over "a word, a smile, a different opinion, or any other sign of undervalue".

Think of each one in daily life:

- **Competition**: two brothers fighting in court for years over a strip of ancestral land.
- **Diffidence**: two neighbouring countries building up armies because each fears the other; the arms race between India and Pakistan, or the US and the Soviet Union during the Cold War. Neither may want war, but each fears what the other might do.
- **Glory**: a road-rage fight that starts because someone honked rudely; gang wars over "respect"; online pile-ons over an insult.

The second cause is the most chilling. It means that even if **everyone** were peaceful, war could still break out, because no one can be sure the others are peaceful. Political scientists today call this the "**security dilemma**".

**Why it matters:** This analysis is the foundation of **realism** in international relations: the view that states, living without a world government, behave toward each other like individuals in the state of nature, arming out of fear.

**A common misunderstanding:** Hobbes didn't say people are evil or love violence. He said that rational people, in conditions of insecurity, have good reasons to fight. Change the conditions, and you change the behaviour.

### Idea 4: The state of nature, a war of all against all

**The idea in one line:** Without a common power, human life would be a permanent condition of war, with no industry, no culture, and constant fear.

This is the most famous passage in *Leviathan*. Hobbes imagines people living with no government at all, which he calls the **state of nature**. He writes:

"During the time men live without a common power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called war; and such a war as is of every man against every man."

Note that "war", for Hobbes, does not mean constant fighting. It means a **condition** in which there is no assurance of peace, as "foul weather" doesn't mean constant rain but a tendency to rain for days together.

And in such a condition, he says:

"there is no place for industry, because the fruit thereof is uncertain: and consequently no culture of the earth; no navigation, nor use of the commodities that may be imported by sea; no commodious building ... no knowledge of the face of the earth; no account of time; no arts; no letters; no society; and which is worst of all, continual fear, and danger of violent death; and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short."

Notice his argument: it is not just that you might be killed. It is that **nothing worth having can be built** without security. Why plant a mango orchard that takes seven years to fruit, if you don't know who will own the land next year? Why start a business, if you can't enforce a contract? Why write books, run schools, explore the world?

Hobbes anticipated an objection: was there ever such a time? He pointed to three examples: "savage people in many places of America" (a claim based on prejudiced European reports), **civil wars**, which show what happens when government collapses, and **kings and nations**, which live in a permanent posture of war with each other because there's no power above them. And he added a challenge to the reader: if you doubt that people distrust each other, notice that you lock your doors when you sleep and your chests even in your own house.

Indian tradition has a strikingly similar image. In the **Arthashastra** of **Kautilya** and in the **Mahabharata**, the condition without a king is called **matsya nyaya**, "the law of the fish": the big fish eat the small fish. The Mahabharata's Shanti Parva tells how, in such a time, people went to the god Brahma and asked for a king, and agreed to pay him a share of their produce in return for protection. Hobbes would have recognised the story at once.

**Why it matters:** Whenever a state collapses, in civil wars, in failed states, in riots when the police withdraw, Hobbes's description comes true in front of our eyes. Economists have shown that secure property rights and the rule of law are among the most important conditions for growth. Hobbes was saying this in 1651.

**A common misunderstanding:** The state of nature is mainly a **thought experiment**, a way of asking "what would life be like without government?" to explain why we need it. Hobbes was not mainly writing history.

### Idea 5: The laws of nature, and the social contract

**The idea in one line:** Reason tells us to seek peace, and to give up our unlimited freedom on condition that others do the same.

In the state of nature, Hobbes says, everyone has a **right of nature**: the liberty to use any means to preserve their own life, including taking anything and killing anyone they judge a threat. This unlimited right is exactly what makes life so dangerous.

But reason also discovers **laws of nature**: general rules, found by reason, which forbid a person to do what is destructive of his own life. The first and fundamental law is: **seek peace**, as far as you have hope of obtaining it; and when you cannot, use all the advantages of war. The second law is: be willing, **when others are too**, to lay down your right to all things, and be contented with so much liberty against others as you would allow others against yourself.

Hobbes noticed that this is close to the Golden Rule: do not do to others what you would not have done to yourself.

So reasonable people in the state of nature would agree to a **covenant**: a mutual agreement in which each gives up his right to govern himself and authorises one person or assembly to govern them all. The words Hobbes imagines each person saying are roughly: I authorise and give up my right of governing myself to this man, or this assembly, on this condition, that you give up your right to him, and authorise all his actions in like manner.

This creates the **commonwealth**, the state. Hobbes calls it "that great **Leviathan**, or rather (to speak more reverently) of that **mortal god**, to which we owe, under the immortal God, our peace and defence."

**Why it matters:** This is the founding statement of **social contract theory**: the idea that political authority comes not from God, tradition or conquest, but from the agreement of the people governed. Locke, Rousseau, Kant and, in the twentieth century, John Rawls all built on it. When the Indian Constitution begins "**We, the people of India**, ... do hereby adopt, enact and give to ourselves this Constitution", it speaks the language of the social contract.

**A common misunderstanding:** Hobbes didn't think people actually met in a field and signed a contract. The contract is a way of showing what makes government **legitimate**: it is what rational people would agree to, given the alternative.

### Idea 6: Covenants without the sword are but words

**The idea in one line:** Promises are only reliable when there is a power to enforce them.

Here is the catch in the contract. In the state of nature, if I keep my promise to lay down my weapons and you don't keep yours, I'm dead. So why would anyone keep a promise without assurance that others will keep theirs?

Hobbes's answer:

"Covenants, without the sword, are but words, and of no strength to secure a man at all."

Agreements need **enforcement**. That's why the contract must create a sovereign with real power, a "common power to keep them all in awe", strong enough that breaking the agreement is more dangerous than keeping it.

Think of everyday life. Why do people pay back bank loans, follow traffic signals, or honour business contracts? Partly from conscience, yes. But also because there are courts, police, fines and penalties. Imagine a city where traffic signals are just suggestions and no one ever gets a challan. You can predict the result.

Game theorists today describe this with the "**prisoner's dilemma**": two people who would both benefit from cooperating may each be tempted to cheat, and without enforcement, both end up worse off. Hobbes's sovereign is a solution to the prisoner's dilemma.

**Why it matters:** This insight runs through law, economics and international relations. Why are international agreements on climate change so hard to keep? Partly because there is no world sovereign to enforce them: they are, in Hobbes's words, but words.

**A common misunderstanding:** Hobbes didn't think people keep promises **only** out of fear. He thought justice is a real virtue and that keeping covenants is a law of nature. But he thought that, in a world full of distrust, virtue alone is too weak to hold society together.

### Idea 7: The sovereign must be absolute

**The idea in one line:** To keep peace, sovereign power must be undivided and unlimited; a divided power will fall into civil war.

This is Hobbes's most controversial conclusion. Having created the sovereign, the people cannot take their authority back, and the sovereign is not bound by the contract (since he is not a party to it; the contract is among the subjects). The sovereign has the right to make laws, judge disputes, choose officials, make war and peace, and even decide which doctrines may be taught, because, Hobbes said, disputes over religion and doctrine had caused the civil war.

Above all, sovereignty must be **undivided**. Hobbes believed England's civil war happened precisely because power was divided between King, Lords and Commons, each claiming a share. "A kingdom divided in itself cannot stand," he quoted.

Hobbes preferred **monarchy**, because a single person is less likely to be torn by faction, but his argument applies to any form of sovereign: one person or an assembly. What matters is that the sovereign's power is absolute.

**Why it matters:** This argument is still made, in softer forms, whenever people say: "This country needs a strong leader", "Democracy is too slow", "Courts and opposition are obstructing development", or "In an emergency, rights must be suspended." When India declared the **Emergency** in **1975** and suspended civil liberties in the name of order and stability, the logic was Hobbesian. So were the protests against it, which drew on Locke and others to argue that power without limits is itself a danger.

**A common misunderstanding:** Hobbes's sovereign is not a tyrant in the sense of a ruler who may do whatever he likes **morally**. Hobbes said the sovereign is bound by the laws of nature and accountable to God, and that a sensible sovereign will rule well, because his strength depends on the prosperity of his people. But subjects have no right to punish or depose him for failing.

### Idea 8: Liberty, and the right to save your own life

**The idea in one line:** Subjects are free wherever the law is silent, and no one can be obliged to give up their own life.

Hobbes is often thought to leave no room for freedom. In fact he had a precise theory of **liberty**. Liberty, he said, is the absence of external obstacles to motion. A subject's liberty lies in all the things the sovereign has not forbidden: buying and selling, choosing a home, a diet, a trade, how to bring up children. In most of daily life, the law is silent, and there people are free.

And there are some rights no contract can take away. Since people enter the commonwealth to preserve their lives, no one can be obliged to kill himself, or not to resist those who come to kill him, imprison him or wound him. A condemned criminal may rightfully try to escape, even though the sovereign may rightfully punish him. A person need not accuse himself. And if the sovereign can no longer protect his subjects, their obligation to him ends: "The obligation of subjects to the sovereign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth by which he is able to protect them."

**Why it matters:** These ideas were radical. The right against self-incrimination, which is in **Article 20(3)** of the Indian Constitution ("No person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself"), has roots in this kind of thinking. And the idea that obedience depends on protection means a state that cannot or will not protect its citizens loses its claim on them.

**A common misunderstanding:** Hobbes's liberty is narrow by modern standards: freedom of speech, religion and assembly are, for him, matters the sovereign may regulate. But he was not indifferent to freedom; he thought a peaceful life with modest liberty is far better than unlimited liberty in a war of all against all.

### Idea 9: Words, definitions and the danger of empty talk

**The idea in one line:** Clear definitions are the beginning of knowledge; confused words lead to absurdity and conflict.

Hobbes, the lover of geometry, was obsessed with language. Reasoning, he said, is "reckoning", adding and subtracting names, and it is only as good as the definitions you begin with. He wrote:

"Words are wise men's counters, they do but reckon by them; but they are the money of fools."

Wise people use words as counters, tokens for calculating; fools treat words as if they had value in themselves, and are dazzled by the authority of famous writers.

He mocked the scholastic philosophy taught in universities, with its "insignificant speech" about "incorporeal substances" and "essences", and he blamed preachers and scholars who stirred up the people with empty, emotional words for helping to cause the civil war.

**Why it matters:** Think of political slogans, advertising jargon and social media outrage: words that sound powerful but mean nothing clear. Hobbes would ask: define your terms. What exactly do you mean by "freedom", "nation", "justice", "development"? Many quarrels dissolve once words are defined; others become clearer.

**A common misunderstanding:** Hobbes wasn't against eloquence as such; he wrote some of the most vivid prose in English. He was against using language to inflame and confuse instead of to clarify.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Aristotle: the political animal rejected

**Aristotle** taught that human beings are by nature political animals, who naturally form communities and fulfil themselves in them. Hobbes flatly rejected this. Bees and ants, he said, live sociably by nature, but humans compete for honour, find fault with how they are governed, and use words to deceive. Human society is not natural but **artificial**, created by a covenant out of fear. "The agreement of these creatures is natural; that of men is by covenant only, which is artificial."

### Descartes: two kinds of new philosophy

Hobbes and **Descartes** were both builders of a new, mechanical philosophy, but they quarrelled. In his objections to the *Meditations*, Hobbes argued that "I think, therefore I am" proves only that there is something that thinks, not that it is a non-material mind; it might be a body. Descartes replied with irritation. The two represent the split between **materialism** and **dualism** that runs through modern philosophy of mind.

### Spinoza: same premises, different conclusion

**Baruch Spinoza** read Hobbes closely. He accepted much of Hobbes's account of the passions and the state of nature. But Spinoza drew a different conclusion: the best state is a **democracy** that allows freedom of thought, because suppressing people's opinions only creates resentment and instability. He also argued that the natural right of the people never fully disappears into the sovereign.

### Locke: the escape route

**John Locke**, a generation later, used the same tools: a state of nature, natural rights, a social contract. But he reached the opposite conclusion. For Locke, people have natural rights to life, liberty and property even in the state of nature, government is a **trust**, and if the ruler betrays it, the people may **resist** and replace him. Locke's famous jibe captures the difference: to think that people would escape the dangers of polecats and foxes by agreeing to be devoured by lions, he said, is absurd.

Imagine them arguing:

*Hobbes:* Without an absolute sovereign, you will have civil war. I have seen it.

*Locke:* With an absolute sovereign, you have a lion who may eat you whenever he likes. What protects you from him?

*Hobbes:* Nothing on earth. But a lion that guards you from all the foxes is better than the foxes.

*Locke:* Better still is a guard who answers to the people he guards.

### Rousseau: the state of nature reimagined

**Jean-Jacques Rousseau** argued that Hobbes had made a basic mistake: he had projected the passions of civilised, competitive people, pride, greed and vanity, back onto "natural" humans. Natural man, Rousseau said, would be solitary but peaceful, moved by self-preservation and pity. It is **society**, with property and comparison, that makes us violent.

### Later voices

- **Carl Schmitt** (1888–1985), the German jurist who later served the Nazis, admired Hobbes's idea of sovereignty and the decision in emergencies.
- **John Rawls** (1921–2002) revived the social contract, but made it a contract about justice, not just about survival.
- **Realists** in international relations, from Hans Morgenthau to Kenneth Waltz, see the world of states as a Hobbesian state of nature.
- **Game theorists** and economists, studying cooperation and the prisoner's dilemma, constantly return to Hobbes's insight about enforcement.

## See it around you

**Riots and the absent state.** When communal riots break out and the police are absent or slow, ordinary people arm themselves, form local "defence" groups, and trust collapses. In a few days, neighbours who lived together for decades can become enemies. That is Hobbes's state of nature in miniature.

**Failed states.** Countries where government has collapsed, where warlords control territory and people flee as refugees, show what life without a common power looks like.

**The security dilemma.** Every arms race, between India and Pakistan, India and China, or the great powers, shows Hobbes's "diffidence": each side builds weapons out of fear of the other, making both less safe.

**"Strong leader" politics.** In many countries, voters tired of chaos, corruption or crime choose leaders who promise order above all, and accept limits on liberty in return. That is the Hobbesian bargain.

**Emergency powers.** During the COVID-19 lockdowns, governments everywhere used sweeping powers to restrict movement and gatherings, justified by the need to protect life. Most citizens accepted them, at least for a while. Hobbes would understand.

**Contracts and courts.** Every rental agreement, bank loan and online purchase relies on the fact that, if someone cheats, a court can enforce the deal. Where courts are slow, as with India's huge backlog of cases, trust and business suffer.

**Locks and CCTV.** Hobbes's challenge still stands: you lock your door at night, you install a camera, you don't leave your phone unattended in a train. What does that say about your trust in your fellow citizens, even under a government?

## Use it

**1. Ask "what keeps this agreement working?"** In any group, a family business, a housing society, a project team, notice what makes people keep their promises. If the answer is "nothing", expect trouble. Build in clear rules and fair enforcement.

**2. Spot the security dilemma.** When you and someone else are both escalating, in an office rivalry, a family dispute, a neighbour quarrel, ask whether each of you is acting out of fear of the other. Often, a small step of reassurance can break the spiral.

**3. Check the three causes.** When a conflict starts, ask: is this about gain, about safety, or about glory? Each needs a different remedy: a fair division, reassurance, or a way to save face.

**4. Define your words.** In an argument, ask both sides to define the key terms. "What exactly do you mean by fairness here?" Many quarrels are about words used in different senses.

**5. Value order, and question it.** Appreciate how much of your daily life depends on a functioning state: roads, courts, police, water. And then ask Locke's question: what limits this power, and what protects me from it?

**6. Don't trust mere promises in high-stakes deals.** In business, property or money matters, put it in writing and make it enforceable. Covenants without the sword are but words.

**7. Notice your appetite for power.** Hobbes said humans have a restless desire of power after power, that ceases only in death. Notice it in yourself, in the constant need for more money, status, followers, and ask when enough is enough.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Too dark a picture of human nature.** Critics from Rousseau to modern anthropologists argue that Hobbes projected the competitive, fearful psychology of his war-torn century onto all of humanity. Early human societies were often cooperative; many small societies without states have lived without constant war. Humans are social and empathetic as well as selfish.

**2. The lion problem.** If the people give up all their power to an absolute sovereign, what protects them from the sovereign? History is full of states that killed far more of their own people than any state of nature, from Stalin's Soviet Union to the Khmer Rouge. Hobbes's cure can be worse than the disease. Locke saw this clearly.

**3. Divided power can work.** Hobbes was sure that divided sovereignty leads to civil war. But constitutional democracies with separation of powers, federalism and judicial review, including India and the United States, have often been stable. Montesquieu's idea that power should check power proved more durable than Hobbes's single sovereign.

**4. Prejudiced examples.** His description of "savage people in America" as living without government was based on ignorant European prejudice. Indigenous American nations had complex political institutions, such as the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy.

**5. Freedom of thought.** Hobbes gave the sovereign power to decide what doctrines may be taught and published. He saw this as necessary to prevent religious war; most later thinkers, from Spinoza to Mill, argued that free thought is itself a source of stability and progress.

**6. Women and the family.** Hobbes was unusual in arguing that, in the state of nature, mothers rather than fathers have dominion over children, and that men and women are not naturally unequal. But he still accepted that, in actual commonwealths, fathers usually rule families, and did little to challenge it.

**7. Mathematics and pride.** His long war with mathematicians over his false proofs of squaring the circle shows a stubborn streak: the man who praised geometry's certainty could not admit when he was wrong in it.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have taken law and order for granted, as part of the background of life, like weather. You may have thought that people are basically good, and that government mostly gets in the way.

**Now,** you can see the state as a fragile, artificial creation, built out of fear and agreement, that makes everything else possible: farming, business, schools, science, culture, and the simple ability to sleep at night. You can see that equal people without a common power have every reason to fear each other, that promises without enforcement are only words, and that even peaceful people may strike first out of distrust. And you can see the danger in Hobbes's answer too: a power strong enough to protect you is also strong enough to crush you.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A traffic signal** looks like a tiny covenant backed by a sword.
- **A riot on the news** shows the state of nature breaking through.
- **An arms race** looks like two frightened neighbours, each locking the door tighter.
- **A demand for a "strong leader"** sounds like Hobbes's bargain, and makes you ask what limits the lion.
- **A signed contract** looks like a small piece of civilisation.
- **Your locked door at night** becomes a quiet answer to Hobbes's challenge.

A man born twin to fear spent ninety-one years thinking about how to escape it. His answer, a mortal god to keep us all in awe, still frightens us. But his question, what stands between us and the war of all against all, is one no society can afford to forget.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679), English philosopher; tutor to the Cavendish family; author of *Leviathan* (1651).
- **Context:** the Spanish Armada, wars of religion, the English Civil War and the execution of Charles I, the scientific revolution.
- **Life:** born in Malmesbury; translated Thucydides; fell in love with geometry at forty; met Galileo; fled to Paris in 1640; tutored the future Charles II; returned to England in 1651; accused of atheism; translated Homer at eighty-six; died at ninety-one.
- **Materialism:** everything is matter in motion, including thought.
- **Equality and fear:** rough equality means anyone can kill anyone.
- **Three causes of quarrel:** competition, diffidence (distrust), glory.
- **State of nature:** a war of all against all; life "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short".
- **Laws of nature and the contract:** seek peace; lay down your right if others do; create the sovereign.
- **Enforcement:** "Covenants, without the sword, are but words."
- **Sovereignty:** absolute and undivided; the Leviathan as a "mortal god".
- **Liberty:** the silence of the law; the inalienable right to preserve one's life.
- **Criticisms:** too dark a view of human nature, no protection against the sovereign, divided power can work, prejudiced examples, no freedom of thought.

**Key terms**

- **State of nature:** the condition of human beings without government.
- **War of all against all:** Hobbes's description of the state of nature.
- **Right of nature:** the liberty to use any means to preserve oneself.
- **Law of nature:** a rule found by reason that forbids what is self-destructive, starting with "seek peace".
- **Social contract (covenant):** the mutual agreement by which people create a sovereign.
- **Sovereign:** the person or assembly authorised to rule, holding absolute power.
- **Leviathan:** the commonwealth or state, pictured as a giant made of its citizens.
- **Security dilemma:** when one side's efforts to be safe make others feel less safe.
- **Matsya nyaya:** "the law of the fish", the Indian image of anarchy in which the big devour the small.
- **Materialism:** the view that everything that exists is physical.
