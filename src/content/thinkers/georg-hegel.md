## Life

Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel was born in Stuttgart in 1770, the son of a civil servant in the Duchy of Württemberg. He studied theology at the Protestant seminary in Tübingen, where he shared rooms with two other future giants of German culture, the poet Friedrich Hölderlin and the philosopher F. W. J. Schelling. The three young men greeted the French Revolution with enthusiasm. Legend has it that they planted a "liberty tree".

After working as a private tutor in Bern and Frankfurt, Hegel joined Schelling at the University of Jena in 1801. He finished his first great work, the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, in 1806, just as Napoleon defeated Prussia at the Battle of Jena. Seeing the emperor ride through the town, Hegel wrote to a friend that he had seen "the world-soul" on horseback.

The war closed the university, and Hegel spent the next years as a newspaper editor in Bamberg and as headmaster of a secondary school in Nuremberg, where he married Marie von Tucher and wrote the *Science of Logic*. After a professorship at Heidelberg he was called in 1818 to the University of Berlin, the most prestigious chair in Germany. There his lectures on history, art, religion and the history of philosophy made him the dominant intellectual figure in the country, with followers throughout Prussia's universities and state. He died in Berlin in 1831, probably of cholera or a stomach illness, at the height of his fame.

## Key ideas

Hegel's writing is notoriously difficult, dense with abstract terminology, and he has been read in radically different ways: as a metaphysician of cosmic Spirit, a philosopher of social life, a conservative apologist for the Prussian state, and a forerunner of revolution.

**Dialectic.** Hegel saw thought and reality as dynamic. Every limited concept or form of life contains internal tensions that drive it beyond itself into a richer form that both cancels and preserves what came before. The German word is *Aufhebung*. The popular formula "thesis–antithesis–synthesis" is not Hegel's own; he rarely used it.

**Spirit (Geist).** Reality is ultimately the self-development of **Spirit**, mind or reason, which comes to know itself through nature and above all through human history, culture and thought. The process culminates in "absolute knowing", in which Spirit grasps reality as its own expression.

**Master and slave.** In a famous passage of the *Phenomenology*, two self-consciousnesses struggle for recognition. One becomes master and the other slave. Paradoxically, it is the slave, through labour and fear, who achieves true self-consciousness. Self-awareness, Hegel suggests, is fundamentally social: we become who we are through others' recognition.

**History as the progress of freedom.** World history moves from the ancient East, where "one is free", through the Greek and Roman world, where "some are free", to the modern Christian-Germanic world, where "all are free". Great individuals such as Napoleon are unwitting instruments of this "cunning of reason".

**Ethical life and the state.** Freedom is realised not in isolated individuals but in institutions, the family, civil society and the state, which Hegel called *Sittlichkeit*, ethical life. Philosophy understands its age only as that age is passing: "the owl of Minerva spreads its wings only with the falling of the dusk."

## Influence and legacy

After Hegel's death his followers split into "Right Hegelians", who emphasised religion and the state, and radical "Young Hegelians", among them **Karl Marx**, who turned the dialectic into a materialist theory of history. **Kierkegaard** founded existentialism largely in protest against his system. In twentieth-century France, Alexandre Kojève's lectures on the *Phenomenology* shaped Sartre, Beauvoir and Lacan, and Francis Fukuyama's "end of history" thesis drew on Kojève's Hegel.

Critics such as Karl Popper attacked Hegel as an enemy of the open society. Analytic philosophers long dismissed him as obscure, but recent decades have brought a major revival, especially of his ideas about recognition, social freedom and the historical character of reason.
