## Start here

Imagine a scientist at ISRO. In the morning she checks the calculations for a rocket launch: orbits, fuel, the exact moment of ignition. Everything is mathematics, testing and proof. Nothing is taken on faith. Then, a few days before the launch, she joins her colleagues on a visit to a temple to pray for its success.

Is she being inconsistent? Some people say yes. They say science and religion are enemies: one is about evidence, the other about belief, and a thinking person must choose. Others say the opposite: that faith comes first, and when science disagrees with scripture, science must be wrong.

Eight and a half centuries ago, a judge in Muslim Spain thought both sides were making a mistake. He was a devout Muslim, an expert in Islamic law, the chief judge of one of the greatest cities in Europe. He was also a doctor, an astronomer, and the most careful reader of Aristotle the world had ever seen. And he argued something that sounded shocking to many of the religious scholars around him: that **the Qur'an itself commands believers to do philosophy**. God, he said, asks us to reflect on the world. Reflection done properly is reasoning, and reasoning done properly is philosophy. So philosophy is not the enemy of religion. For those able to do it, it is a religious duty.

And if reason and scripture seem to clash? Then, he said, we must look again, because **truth does not contradict truth**.

His name was **Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Rushd**. Europe called him **Averroes**. His own world eventually burned some of his books and sent him into exile. But in Latin translation, his commentaries became the textbooks of Paris and Oxford. Dante placed him among the great minds of antiquity, and the painter Raphael is often said to have included him in his *School of Athens*.

By the end of this page, you will know who he was, why he matters, and how his way of holding faith and reason together can help you think about some of the most heated arguments of our own time.

## The world he was born into

### Al-Andalus: Muslim Spain

Ibn Rushd was born in **1126** in **Córdoba**, in what Arabs called **al-Andalus**, the parts of Spain and Portugal under Muslim rule. Muslims had crossed from North Africa into Spain in 711. For centuries afterwards, al-Andalus was one of the richest and most cultured places in Europe.

At its height in the tenth century, Córdoba was perhaps the largest city in Western Europe. It had paved streets, street lighting, public baths, a vast mosque with a forest of striped arches (which still stands), and libraries. The caliph al-Hakam II was said to own a library of hundreds of thousands of books, at a time when the greatest monasteries of France owned a few hundred.

In al-Andalus, Muslims, Jews and Christians lived side by side. The relationship was never one of equals, and there were periods of persecution, but there was also real exchange. Arabic was the language of learning for all three communities. The great Jewish philosopher **Moses Maimonides** was born in Córdoba in 1138, twelve years after Ibn Rushd.

### The great translation movement

Behind this culture stood one of the most important events in the history of ideas. In the eighth and ninth centuries, the Abbasid caliphs in **Baghdad** had sponsored the translation of Greek science and philosophy into Arabic. In institutions such as the famous "House of Wisdom", scholars translated Aristotle, Plato's ideas, Euclid, Ptolemy and Galen, along with works from Persian and Sanskrit. Indian numerals, including the zero, travelled to the Arab world in this period and from there to Europe, which is why Europeans still call them "Arabic numerals".

Muslim philosophers like **al-Kindi**, **al-Farabi** (called "the Second Teacher", after Aristotle) and **Ibn Sina** (Avicenna) built on this inheritance. Greek philosophy became an Arabic tradition, called **falsafa**.

### The backlash: al-Ghazali

But not everyone welcomed it. Some religious scholars feared that philosophy led people away from faith. Around **1095**, the brilliant theologian **Abu Hamid al-Ghazali** published *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*. He attacked the philosophers on twenty points and declared that on three of them, they were not just wrong but guilty of **unbelief**:

1. They said the **world is eternal**, rather than created in time.
2. They said **God knows only universals**, not particular things and events.
3. They denied the **bodily resurrection** of the dead.

Al-Ghazali's book was enormously influential. Ibn Rushd would spend part of his life answering it.

### The Almohads

In Ibn Rushd's time, al-Andalus and Morocco were ruled by the **Almohads**, a dynasty that rose in the Atlas Mountains of Morocco in the early twelfth century. They were religious reformers with a strict, puritanical side. But some of their caliphs were also deeply curious about philosophy and science, and they became Ibn Rushd's patrons. His life would rise and fall with their favour.

### The Christian north

Meanwhile, the Christian kingdoms of northern Spain were pushing south, in the long wars Spaniards call the **Reconquista**. Toledo had fallen to the Christians in 1085. In Toledo, scholars were already translating Arabic books into Latin. Within decades of Ibn Rushd's death, Córdoba itself would fall (1236).

## The life, as a story

### A family of judges

Ibn Rushd was born on **14 April 1126** into one of Córdoba's most respected families. His grandfather, also named Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Rushd, had been the **chief judge (qadi) of Córdoba** and a famous expert in **Maliki law**, the school of Islamic law dominant in North Africa and Spain. His father was also a judge. To distinguish him from his grandfather, Arabic writers sometimes call him **Ibn Rushd al-Hafid**, "the grandson".

He received the full education of a scholar: the Qur'an, Hadith (the sayings of the Prophet), law, theology, Arabic grammar and poetry. He then studied medicine and philosophy. We know little about his childhood. He left no autobiography, and almost no personal letters survive.

### The interview with the caliph

The most famous story about his life was told by one of his students. In about **1168 or 1169**, the older philosopher and court physician **Ibn Tufayl** (author of a philosophical novel about a boy who grows up alone on an island and discovers the truth by reason alone) introduced Ibn Rushd to the Almohad caliph, **Abu Yaqub Yusuf**.

The caliph asked him a dangerous question: what did the philosophers believe about the heavens? Were they eternal, or created in time?

This was exactly the question on which al-Ghazali had accused the philosophers of unbelief. Ibn Rushd was frightened. He pretended he knew nothing about philosophy. Then the caliph turned to Ibn Tufayl and began discussing the question himself, showing that he knew what Plato, Aristotle and the theologians had said. Ibn Rushd relaxed, and spoke. When he left, the caliph sent him money, a robe of honour and a horse.

Later, Ibn Tufayl told him that the caliph had complained about the difficulty of Aristotle's books and their translations. Someone needed to explain them clearly. Ibn Tufayl said he was too old for the job. Ibn Rushd should do it.

He did. It became his life's work.

### Judge, doctor, commentator

For the next quarter of a century, Ibn Rushd lived three lives at once.

**The judge.** He was appointed qadi of **Seville** in **1169** and later became **chief qadi of Córdoba**, the post his grandfather had held. He wrote *The Distinguished Jurist's Primer* (*Bidayat al-Mujtahid*), a book on Islamic law that is still studied. Unusually, it does not simply tell you the right answer. It sets out where the different schools of law agree and disagree, and why, so that a judge can reason for himself.

**The doctor.** In **1182** he succeeded Ibn Tufayl as personal physician to the caliph. His medical encyclopedia, the *Kulliyat* ("General Principles"), was translated into Latin as the *Colliget* and studied in European medical schools. He is often credited with seeing that the retina, not the lens, is the part of the eye that senses light, and with noticing that a person who survives smallpox does not catch it again.

**The commentator.** Above all, he explained Aristotle. He wrote three kinds of commentary:

- **Short commentaries (epitomes):** summaries of the main ideas.
- **Middle commentaries:** paraphrases that follow the text section by section.
- **Long commentaries:** line-by-line explanations, quoting each passage of Aristotle and then explaining it in detail.

He covered almost every work of Aristotle that existed in Arabic. The one important exception was the *Politics*, which he could not find. So instead, he wrote a commentary on **Plato's *Republic***.

### The original works

Around **1179 and 1180**, he also wrote his own short, powerful books defending philosophy:

- ***The Decisive Treatise*** (*Fasl al-Maqal*), on whether Islamic law permits, forbids or commands the study of philosophy.
- ***The Examination of the Methods of Proof*** (*al-Kashf*), on how different groups argue about religious belief.
- ***The Incoherence of the Incoherence*** (*Tahafut al-Tahafut*), his point-by-point reply to al-Ghazali. Even the title is a joke: al-Ghazali had written *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, so Ibn Rushd replied that al-Ghazali's book was itself incoherent.

### The fall

In **1195**, everything changed. The caliph now was **Abu Yusuf Yaqub al-Mansur**, son of Ibn Rushd's first patron. He was fighting a war against the Christian kings in Spain and needed the support of the conservative religious scholars and the common people. Philosophy had become politically dangerous.

Ibn Rushd was put on trial before an assembly in Córdoba. His views were condemned. He was **banished to Lucena**, a small town near Córdoba with a largely Jewish population. Many of his books were **burned**, and the study of philosophy was banned, except for medicine, arithmetic and basic astronomy. One story says that he and his son were driven out of a mosque in Córdoba by a mob.

The disgrace did not last long. Within a couple of years the caliph recalled him to Marrakesh and restored him to favour. But he was old and tired. He died in **Marrakesh** on **10 December 1198**.

### A body balanced by books

His body was later carried back to Córdoba for burial. The great Sufi mystic **Ibn Arabi**, who had met Ibn Rushd as a young man, watched the funeral procession. He saw the coffin loaded on one side of a mule, and on the other side, to balance it, Ibn Rushd's books. Ibn Arabi wrote a short verse about it: on one side the master, on the other his works. Had his hopes, he wondered, been fulfilled?

### Two afterlives

In the Islamic world, Ibn Rushd's philosophy was largely forgotten for centuries. Many of his works survive today only in Hebrew or Latin translation, because Jewish scholars and Christian translators preserved what Muslim libraries lost.

In Europe, it was the opposite. From about **1220**, his commentaries were translated into Latin, and he became simply **"the Commentator"**, just as Aristotle was "the Philosopher". In the *Divine Comedy*, Dante places him in Limbo, among the virtuous non-Christians, and calls him "Averroes, who made the great commentary". A whole movement of Latin "Averroists" grew up at the University of Paris, and the church condemned it.

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Arab reformers rediscovered him as a symbol of reason, tolerance and enlightenment within Islam. In 1997, the Egyptian director **Youssef Chahine** made a film about him, *Destiny* (*al-Masir*), as a protest against fanaticism.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: The Qur'an commands us to think

**The idea in one line:** Reflecting on the world is a religious duty, and the best kind of reflection is careful reasoning, which is what philosophy is.

*The Decisive Treatise* is written as a legal opinion. Ibn Rushd, the judge, asks a legal question: in Islamic law, is the study of philosophy **forbidden**, **permitted**, **recommended** or **obligatory**?

His answer is bold. He points to verses in the Qur'an that call on believers to reflect on creation: "Reflect, you who have vision" (59:2); "Have they not considered the kingdom of the heavens and the earth?" (7:185). The Law, he argues, commands reflection on the world, because the world points to its Maker. The better you understand the work, the better you understand the worker.

Now, what is the best form of reflection? It is **demonstration**: reasoning from true premises to certain conclusions. That is exactly what Aristotle's logic teaches. So, for those who are capable of it, studying philosophy is not just permitted. It is **obligatory**.

But what about the fact that the philosophy came from Greeks, who were not Muslims? Ibn Rushd answers with an everyday example. When we sacrifice an animal, we use a knife. Does it matter whether the knife was made by a Muslim or a non-Muslim? No. What matters is whether it is sharp. In the same way, we should use the tools of reasoning that others have developed, take what is true, and leave aside what is false, with gratitude to those who did the work before us.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the strongest arguments ever made, from inside a religious tradition, for free inquiry. It does not say "reason is fine as long as it stays away from religion". It says religion itself demands reason.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Rushd did not say everyone must study philosophy. He said it is obligatory for those who have the ability and training. For others, he thought, it could be harmful, like giving strong medicine to someone who doesn't need it.

### Idea 2: Truth does not contradict truth

**The idea in one line:** Scripture and demonstrated reason are both true, so they cannot really contradict each other; an apparent conflict means we must interpret scripture more deeply.

Here is the core of Ibn Rushd's thinking. Suppose careful, demonstrative reasoning reaches a conclusion. Suppose scripture seems to say something different. What then?

Ibn Rushd's answer: the scripture has an **outer** (apparent) meaning and an **inner** meaning. When the apparent meaning conflicts with demonstrated truth, the passage must be **interpreted** (the Arabic word is **ta'wil**) according to the inner meaning. Arabic is full of metaphor; the Qur'an speaks in images that ordinary people can understand. Reading it properly means knowing when a passage is meant literally and when it is not.

He was careful to add limits. Interpretation must follow the rules of the Arabic language. Some things are agreed by the whole community and cannot be reinterpreted. And interpretations should be offered only by those qualified to make them.

Indian philosophy reached a similar idea. The great Vedanta teacher **Adi Shankaracharya** wrote, in his commentary on the *Bhagavad Gita*, that even a hundred scriptures declaring that fire is cold would carry no authority, because scripture's job is to tell us what we cannot learn by perception and reason, not to contradict them. Where a scripture seems to, it must have another meaning.

Galileo said something very close in 1615, in a letter defending his astronomy: the Bible teaches "how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go".

**Why it matters:** This principle has allowed thinkers in every religious tradition to accept new discoveries in science, from the movement of the earth to evolution, without abandoning their faith. Many modern believers hold it without knowing where it comes from.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Rushd is often said to have taught a **"double truth"**: that something can be true in philosophy and false in religion at the same time. He never said this. The doctrine was later attributed to Latin "Averroists" by their critics, and it is the opposite of his view. For him there is **one** truth, reached in different ways.

### Idea 3: Three kinds of people, three kinds of argument

**The idea in one line:** People are persuaded in different ways, by proof, by debate or by stories, and religion speaks to all of them.

Following Aristotle's logic, Ibn Rushd divided arguments into three kinds:

1. **Demonstrative:** strict proof from certain premises. Only a few people, the philosophers, are trained and able to follow it.
2. **Dialectical:** argument from commonly accepted opinions, used in debates. This is the method of the theologians.
3. **Rhetorical:** persuasion through examples, images, stories and appeals to feeling. This is how most people come to believe things.

The genius of scripture, he said, is that it speaks to all three at once. The same verse gives an image to the many, an argument to the debater, and a pointer to the deeper truth for the philosopher.

From this follows a sensitive conclusion. Philosophers should **not** share their deep interpretations with ordinary people, who might be confused and lose their faith without gaining the philosophy to replace it. And theologians, he said, did great harm when they took half-understood philosophical arguments to the public. Neither the many nor the few were helped.

Think of how a doctor talks about a disease. To a medical conference, she presents data and statistics. To a patient's family, she uses simple images. To a public health campaign, she uses a catchy slogan. The truth is the same; the way of communicating it changes with the audience.

**Why it matters:** Every teacher, journalist, preacher and politician faces Ibn Rushd's question: how do you speak the truth to people who think in different ways? He recognised long ago that communication has to fit the audience, an idea now central to education and science communication.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Rushd's view can sound arrogant, as if he thought most people were stupid. His point was about training and time, not worth. He also insisted that the simple believer who follows the clear meaning of scripture is not in error and will be saved.

### Idea 4: Causes are real, and denying them destroys knowledge

**The idea in one line:** Fire really does burn cotton; if we say things have no real causes, we can no longer know anything.

Al-Ghazali had made a famous argument. When cotton touches a flame, we see the cotton burn. But do we actually see the fire **causing** the burning? No, he said; we only see one thing following another. The real cause is God, who creates the burning each time. Fire has no power of its own. God usually makes cotton burn when it meets fire, out of habit, but he could choose otherwise, and that is how miracles happen.

(Seven centuries later, the Scottish philosopher **David Hume** made a strikingly similar argument about cause and effect, though for very different reasons.)

Ibn Rushd replied with force. If things have no natures and no real powers, then nothing has a definite identity. Fire would not be fire, because what makes fire fire is that it burns. And if there are no causes, there is no knowledge, because to know something is to know its causes. **He who denies causes**, he wrote in effect, **denies the intellect**.

Imagine a doctor who said, "This medicine doesn't really cure fevers. God just usually cures fevers after people swallow it." Could medicine be a science? Could the doctor learn which medicine works better? Ibn Rushd, the physician, thought not.

**Why it matters:** Science rests on the belief that the world has a stable order, that the same causes produce the same effects, and that we can discover them. Ibn Rushd defended this belief from within a religious framework: God created a world with real natures and real causes, and studying them honours God.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Rushd did not deny miracles or God's power. He said God's power works through the natures God created, and that the philosophers did not deny miracles but thought their causes were beyond our full understanding.

### Idea 5: Answering al-Ghazali point by point

**The idea in one line:** Instead of condemning his opponent, he answered him argument by argument, and showed that the dispute was often about words.

On the three charges of unbelief, Ibn Rushd's answers were careful:

- **The eternity of the world.** The dispute, he said, is partly about words. Everyone agrees the world is made by God. The philosophers say God's act of creating is eternal; the theologians say the world began in time. But the Qur'an itself does not clearly say that time had a beginning before which there was nothing. It speaks of God's throne being on the waters, and of the heavens being made from smoke. So the issue is open to interpretation, and no one should be called an unbeliever over it.
- **God's knowledge of particulars.** The philosophers did not say God is ignorant of particular things. They said God's knowledge is not like ours. We know things because they exist and affect us; things exist because God knows them. God's knowledge is the cause of things, not their effect. So it is neither "universal" nor "particular" in our sense.
- **Bodily resurrection.** He insisted the philosophers did not deny the afterlife. They differed on how to understand it, and on this question too, interpretation was legitimate.

But just as important as his answers was his method. *The Incoherence of the Incoherence* quotes al-Ghazali's arguments, in full, before replying to each. A reader can see both sides. And he concluded that a learned person who makes an error in interpreting difficult questions, after sincere effort, should be excused, not punished.

**Why it matters:** This is a model of intellectual honesty: quote your opponent fairly, answer the argument, not the person, and do not call someone a traitor or an unbeliever for reaching a different conclusion after honest effort.

**A common misunderstanding:** It's sometimes said that al-Ghazali "killed philosophy" in the Islamic world and Ibn Rushd lost the argument. The truth is more complex. Al-Ghazali himself used Aristotle's logic and wanted it taught, and philosophy continued to flourish in the Islamic east, especially in Persia. What declined in the Islamic west was Ibn Rushd's particular kind of Aristotelian philosophy.

### Idea 6: One intellect for all humanity

**The idea in one line:** Ibn Rushd argued that the power by which humans understand universal truths is one and the same for all human beings.

This is the hardest of his ideas, and the one that caused the most trouble in Europe.

Aristotle had said that the mind can understand universal truths, like "a triangle has three sides" or "all humans are mortal". These truths are not tied to any particular body, place or time. So the power that grasps them, Aristotle hinted, cannot itself be a bodily organ.

Ibn Rushd drew a radical conclusion. If the understanding mind (he called it the **material intellect**) is not part of any body, then it cannot be divided between bodies. So there is only **one** such intellect, shared by all humanity. When you and I both understand that two plus two is four, we are sharing in the same intellect, just as many people can see by the same sunlight. What is individual to each of us is our imagination, memory and sensations, which feed the shared intellect with material.

The consequence troubled Christians and Muslims alike. If the intellect is shared, what survives death? Not an individual thinking soul, it seemed, but only the one intellect of humanity.

Think of it like the internet. Each of us has our own device, our own photos and messages. But the knowledge we draw on is, in a sense, one shared store. Ibn Rushd thought human understanding worked something like that.

**Why it matters:** The idea provoked one of the great debates of medieval Europe. Thomas Aquinas wrote a whole treatise against it, *On the Unity of the Intellect, against the Averroists* (1270). His key reply: "**this** human being understands". Thinking is something I do, not something done in me by a shared intellect. The debate pushed European thought towards the idea of the individual thinking self.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Rushd's view on this point changed over his life, and scholars still debate exactly what he meant. He did not teach that individuals don't think. He was trying to explain how many different people can grasp one and the same truth.

### Idea 7: Women's abilities are wasted

**The idea in one line:** In his commentary on Plato's *Republic*, Ibn Rushd argued that women can do the work men do, and that a society which confines them to childbearing and nursing makes itself poor.

Plato's *Republic* had argued that women could be guardians and rulers alongside men. Ibn Rushd, commenting on this, agreed in principle. Women, he said, differ from men in degree, not in nature; some women have outstanding abilities, and there have been women rulers and warriors.

Then he turned to his own society. In our cities, he observed, women are kept only for bearing children and serving their husbands. Their abilities are not known, because they are never given the chance to use them. They are like plants. Because they are not productive in the economy, they are a burden on the men, and this is one of the causes of the poverty of these cities.

**Why it matters:** This is a remarkable passage for the twelfth century, in any civilisation. It is an economic argument for women's participation, of the kind made today by economists who calculate how much a country's income would grow if more women joined the paid workforce. India's female labour force participation has long been among the lowest of the major economies, and Ibn Rushd's diagnosis would not be out of place in a modern policy report.

**A common misunderstanding:** Ibn Rushd was not a modern feminist. He still accepted that men were, on average, superior in most tasks, and his argument is mainly about the waste of human resources. But in a world where most writers took women's confinement for granted, he named it as a loss.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Aristotle: the master

Ibn Rushd revered Aristotle. He called him the man in whom human perfection reached its highest point, the one through whom God had shown humanity the limit of human understanding. Some critics say this reverence went too far: that he treated Aristotle as almost infallible. But his commentaries were so clear and careful that, for centuries, Europeans read Aristotle through Ibn Rushd's eyes.

### Plato: a substitute teacher

Because he could not find Aristotle's *Politics*, Ibn Rushd wrote on Plato's *Republic*. He stripped out Plato's myths and dialogue and kept the arguments, applying them to the Islamic cities he knew. He compared the decline of the ideal state in Plato's account to the decline of the Almohad dynasty, which had begun with a virtuous government and was turning towards love of wealth and honour.

### Al-Ghazali: the great opponent

Al-Ghazali is not in this Hall, but he is Ibn Rushd's great partner in argument. Imagine them in conversation:

*Al-Ghazali:* The philosophers claim to have proofs, but their proofs about God fail. And on three points they contradict the faith.

*Ibn Rushd:* Some of their arguments fail, I agree. But you have attacked their weakest versions, and on the three points you misunderstand them.

*Al-Ghazali:* Fire does not burn by its own power. God burns the cotton.

*Ibn Rushd:* Then you have abolished the nature of fire, and with it all knowledge. Whoever denies causes denies the intellect.

*Al-Ghazali:* I am protecting the faith of ordinary people from confusion.

*Ibn Rushd:* Then why bring these difficult questions before the public at all? It was the theologians, not the philosophers, who carried them into the marketplace.

### Thomas Aquinas: borrower and critic

**Thomas Aquinas** relied on Ibn Rushd's commentaries constantly and called him "the Commentator". He shared Ibn Rushd's conviction that faith and reason cannot really conflict. But he fought fiercely against the unity of the intellect, and against the "Averroists" in Paris who, he thought, were teaching that philosophy could prove things the faith denied. In a famous painting in Pisa, Aquinas is shown in glory, with Ibn Rushd lying defeated at his feet. The image is unfair: Aquinas owed him far more than it suggests.

### Ibn Khaldun: the doubting heir

**Ibn Khaldun**, born in Tunis a century and a half later, wrote summaries of Ibn Rushd's works as a young man. But in his great *Muqaddimah*, he argued that the philosophers' attempt to reach metaphysical truths by reason alone was a mistake: reason is powerful for understanding the world, but not for proving what lies beyond it.

### Maimonides: the Jewish parallel

**Moses Maimonides**, born in the same city twelve years later, did for Judaism what Ibn Rushd did for Islam: he argued in his *Guide for the Perplexed* that scripture and Aristotelian philosophy could be reconciled through interpretation. The two never met, but their projects ran in parallel, and their works were read together by later Jewish scholars.

### Later voices

- **Siger of Brabant** led the Latin Averroists at Paris. Their views were condemned by the Bishop of Paris in 1270 and again in 1277.
- **Dante** honoured him in the *Divine Comedy* as the great commentator.
- **Ernest Renan**, the French scholar, wrote a famous study, *Averroès et l'averroïsme* (1852), that made him a symbol of free thought.
- Modern Arab philosophers, such as **Mohammed Abed al-Jabri** of Morocco, called for a return to Ibn Rushd's rationalism as the way forward for Arab thought.

## See it around you

**Science and faith in one person.** The ISRO scientist who prays before a launch; the doctor who goes to the gurdwara after surgery; the Jesuit astronomer at the Vatican Observatory. Each is living out Ibn Rushd's claim that truth doesn't contradict truth.

**"Scientific temper" in the Constitution.** Article 51A(h) of the Indian Constitution makes it a fundamental duty of every citizen "to develop the scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform". It does not ask anyone to give up faith. It asks everyone to think, which is exactly what Ibn Rushd said religion itself requires.

**Evolution and the scriptures.** Debates over teaching evolution in schools, in the United States, in Turkey and elsewhere, replay the old question. Those who say "the creation story must be read as poetry, not biology" are, knowingly or not, using Ibn Rushd's method of interpretation.

**Calling people "anti-national" or "unbelievers".** When a scholar, writer or comedian is branded a traitor or blasphemer for an honest argument, we are watching the opposite of Ibn Rushd's principle that an honest error after sincere effort deserves to be excused, not punished.

**Book bans and burnings.** From the burning of Ibn Rushd's books in Córdoba to modern bans on books and films, the impulse to destroy ideas rather than answer them keeps returning.

**Explaining science to the public.** A vaccine campaign uses simple slogans; a medical journal uses statistics; a TV debate uses arguments. Ibn Rushd's three kinds of persuasion are alive in every public health campaign.

**Women and work.** Every report on how much India's economy would grow if more women worked outside the home echoes his complaint that a city that keeps women only for childbearing makes itself poor.

**Translation.** Many of Ibn Rushd's works survive only because Jewish and Christian scholars translated them. Every time you read a book in translation, you depend on the bridge-builders he depended on.

## Use it

**1. When faith and evidence seem to clash, slow down.** Before deciding that either your faith or the evidence must be wrong, ask Ibn Rushd's question: have I understood both correctly? Is the scripture meant literally here? Is the evidence really conclusive?

**2. Take good tools from anywhere.** Ibn Rushd's knife doesn't care who made it. Learn from people of other religions, nations and political views. Judge an idea by its truth, not by its origin.

**3. Quote your opponent fairly.** Before you reply to an argument, state it in full, in its strongest form, as Ibn Rushd did with al-Ghazali. You'll often find the disagreement is smaller, or different, than you thought.

**4. Ask whether the dispute is about words.** Many fierce arguments, about religion, politics or even family matters, turn out to be about what a word means. Define your terms before you fight.

**5. Fit your message to your audience.** Explaining something to a child, a colleague and an expert needs three different approaches. Choose the right one; the truth stays the same.

**6. Excuse honest error.** When someone reaches a different conclusion after sincere effort, disagree with them, but don't call them evil, traitors or unbelievers.

**7. Respect causes.** When something goes wrong in your life, a failed exam, an illness, a business loss, look for the real causes before blaming luck or fate. Knowledge, said Ibn Rushd, is knowledge of causes.

**8. Don't waste half the talent in the room.** In your family, office or community, ask whose abilities are unknown because they were never given a chance.

## Where he went wrong

**1. Too much reverence for Aristotle.** Ibn Rushd sometimes treated Aristotle as almost beyond error, defending his physics and astronomy where they were wrong. His loyalty to Aristotle's model of the heavens, for example, led him to reject Ptolemy's more accurate system on philosophical grounds. Later science would show that Aristotle could be wrong about a great deal.

**2. An elite view of truth.** His division of people into the philosophical few and the many who must be taught through images can justify keeping people in ignorance "for their own good". Modern democracies rest on the opposite idea: that ordinary people can and should be trusted with the full truth.

**3. Limits on women's equality.** His remarkable passage on women still accepted that they were, in general, inferior in most tasks. He saw their confinement as wasteful, not as unjust to them as persons.

**4. Not tolerance in the modern sense.** Ibn Rushd defended the freedom of qualified scholars to interpret scripture. He did not defend the right of anyone to believe or preach anything. As a judge, he applied Islamic law, including its penalties, and he held that those who stir up doubt about the foundations of religion among ordinary people should be restrained, and could be punished. His "tolerance" was tolerance for honest scholarly error, not freedom of religion for all.

**5. The unity of the intellect.** Many critics, from Aquinas onwards, argued that his doctrine of one intellect for all humanity makes no sense of the plain fact that **I** think, and threatens the value of the individual person.

**6. The romantic myth.** This is less his mistake than ours. Modern writers sometimes turn Ibn Rushd into a secular liberal hero, crushed by fanatics. He was nothing of the kind: he was a devout Muslim judge whose whole project was to show that philosophy served the faith. Reading him well means taking his faith as seriously as his reason.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought that faith and reason are natural enemies, that a thinking person must choose between science and religion, and that philosophy was a purely Western story running from Greece straight to Europe. You may never have heard of Ibn Rushd, or known him only as an exotic name, "Averroes", in a list of medieval thinkers.

**Now,** you can see that one of the strongest defences of reason ever written came from inside Islamic law: that reflection on the world is a religious duty, and that truth does not contradict truth. You can see that Greek philosophy reached modern Europe through Baghdad and Córdoba, carried by Muslim, Jewish and Christian scholars together. You can see the value of answering an opponent point by point, of excusing honest error, of recognising when a fight is really about words. And you can see the limits: his reverence for Aristotle, his elite idea of who should know the truth, and the fact that his books were burned in his own lifetime.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A scientist praying before a launch** no longer looks like a contradiction, but like a person holding two kinds of truth together.
- **A heated religious debate on television** makes you ask: are they quoting each other fairly? Is the fight about words?
- **A book ban** reminds you of a mule in Córdoba carrying a coffin on one side and books on the other.
- **The Arabic numerals on your phone** remind you that knowledge has always travelled between civilisations.
- **A room where some people never speak** makes you wonder whose abilities remain unknown.
- **"Truth does not contradict truth"** becomes a test you can apply to any argument that asks you to choose between your mind and your heart.

A judge in Córdoba, devoted to his faith and to the most careful reasoning he could find, refused to believe he had to choose between them. The world he lived in burned his books. The world that came after read them, and became modern.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Ibn Rushd, known in Europe as Averroes (1126–1198), Andalusian Muslim philosopher, judge and physician; "the Commentator" on Aristotle.
- **Context:** Muslim Spain (al-Andalus) and the Almohad dynasty; the Baghdad translation movement; al-Ghazali's attack on the philosophers; the Christian Reconquista.
- **Life:** born in Córdoba to a family of judges; introduced to the caliph by Ibn Tufayl (c. 1168–69); qadi of Seville and chief qadi of Córdoba; caliph's physician (1182); disgraced and exiled to Lucena, books burned (1195); died in Marrakesh (1198).
- **Philosophy is commanded:** the Qur'an calls on believers to reflect; for those able, philosophy is obligatory.
- **Truth does not contradict truth:** apparent conflicts are resolved by interpretation (ta'wil).
- **Three kinds of argument:** demonstrative, dialectical and rhetorical; scripture speaks to all.
- **Causes are real:** against al-Ghazali, denying causes destroys knowledge.
- **The Incoherence of the Incoherence:** point-by-point reply; honest error should be excused.
- **Unity of the intellect:** one material intellect for all humanity, opposed by Aquinas.
- **Women:** their abilities are wasted, and this impoverishes cities.
- **Criticisms:** excessive reverence for Aristotle, elitism about truth, limited view of tolerance and of women's equality, the puzzle of the shared intellect.

**Key terms**

- **Al-Andalus:** the parts of the Iberian peninsula under Muslim rule (711–1492).
- **Falsafa:** philosophy in the Greek tradition, written in Arabic.
- **Qadi:** a judge applying Islamic law.
- **Ta'wil:** interpretation of scripture beyond its apparent meaning.
- **Demonstration:** strict proof from certain premises to certain conclusions.
- **The Commentator:** Ibn Rushd's title in Latin Europe, as Aristotle was "the Philosopher".
- **Material intellect:** the power of understanding universal truths, which Ibn Rushd held is one for all humanity.
- **Latin Averroism:** the movement at the University of Paris inspired by his commentaries, condemned in 1270 and 1277.
- **Double truth:** the view, wrongly attributed to him, that something can be true in philosophy and false in religion.
