## Start here

Imagine you are the District Collector of a flood-hit district. You have one rescue helicopter, three hours of daylight, and reports from two villages. In the first village, forty people are stranded on rooftops. In the second, five people are stranded, and one of them is your old school teacher, the man who changed your life.

Where do you send the helicopter?

Most of you answered almost immediately: the first village. Forty lives over five. And if someone asked why, you would say something like, "Because it saves more people. Because more suffering is prevented."

Now notice what you just did. You didn't open a holy book. You didn't ask what tradition says, or what your caste or community expects, or what feels loyal. You asked one question: **which choice produces the most good and the least suffering, counting every person equally?**

That question has a name. It is called **utilitarianism**. And the man who turned it into a complete system, and then tried to rebuild the entire law of England around it, was a strange, brilliant, tireless Londoner named **Jeremy Bentham**.

Bentham thought that this one question, asked honestly and applied everywhere, could clean out centuries of cruelty, superstition and nonsense from law and government. Why do we hang a man for stealing a sheep? Does it reduce suffering, or add to it? Why can't women vote? Why are people imprisoned for loving someone of the same sex? Why are animals treated as things? Why are laws written in language nobody can understand? Ask the question, he said, and watch the excuses collapse.

But here is the problem, and you probably felt it already. Change the story a little. What if the forty people in the first village could be saved only by **pushing** one innocent person off a bridge to block a flood channel? The numbers are the same, forty against one. Yet suddenly your stomach turns. Something in you says: *no, you can't use a person like that.*

So is morality really just arithmetic of happiness? Or is there something the arithmetic misses? By the end of this page you'll understand Bentham's answer, why it transformed the modern world, from prisons to welfare schemes to animal rights, and where it runs into trouble. And you'll never look at a government scheme, a traffic fine or a CCTV camera the same way again.

## The world he was born into

### A legal jungle

Bentham was born in **London in 1748**. To understand him, you must first understand the thing he hated most: **English law** as it existed in the eighteenth century.

English law was mostly **common law**, law built up over centuries from the decisions of judges in particular cases. There was no single code you could read. To know the law, you had to know thousands of old cases, written in technical language, some in Latin or "law French". Lawyers loved this. It made them indispensable. Ordinary people could not understand the rules they lived under.

And the rules were often savage. Historians call it the "**Bloody Code**": by the end of the eighteenth century, English law listed around two hundred crimes punishable by **death**, many of them crimes against property, like stealing goods above a small value, poaching, or cutting down certain trees. At the same time, courts were slow, expensive and full of fictions that lawyers used to get around their own rules.

Now imagine a sharp young man looking at this and asking: *Why? Who decided this? What good does it do?* The usual answer was: *This is how it has always been. This is the wisdom of our ancestors. This is the glorious English constitution.*

The most famous defender of this system was **William Blackstone**, whose *Commentaries on the Laws of England* (1765–69) presented English law as a wise and nearly perfect structure. Bentham heard Blackstone lecture at Oxford as a teenager, and said later that even then he could detect the fallacies.

### The age of revolutions

Bentham's long life (1748–1832) stretched across a world turned upside down:

- The **American Revolution** (1776) and its Declaration that all men have "unalienable rights".
- The **French Revolution** (1789) and its Declaration of the Rights of Man.
- The **Industrial Revolution**, crowding the poor into factories and slums.
- The British conquest of **India**, which turned a trading company into a government ruling millions.

Everyone was talking about rights, liberty, reform, revolution. Bentham's question cut through all the slogans: *never mind what sounds grand; what actually makes people's lives better?*

### The Enlightenment's tools

Bentham inherited tools from the **Enlightenment**, the eighteenth-century movement that tried to replace tradition and authority with reason and evidence. Three influences matter most:

- **David Hume** taught that morality is not handed down from heaven; it grows out of human feelings and what is useful to human life.
- The French philosopher **Helvétius** argued that people act from pleasure and pain, and that good laws can shape behaviour by arranging rewards and punishments.
- The Italian reformer **Cesare Beccaria**, in *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764), attacked torture and the death penalty and spoke of "the greatest happiness shared among the greatest number".

Bentham took these scattered ideas and did something no one had done: he built them into a complete **system**, and then spent sixty years applying it to everything.

## The life, as a story

### A child prodigy who hated fairy tales

Jeremy Bentham was born on **15 February 1748** in Houndsditch, London, the son of **Jeremiah Bentham**, a prosperous attorney who wanted his son to become a great lawyer, perhaps Lord Chancellor.

The boy was astonishingly precocious. Family stories say he was found reading a large history of England at the age of three, and that he was learning Latin very young. He was small, sickly and shy. He was also, by his own account, terrified of ghosts all his life, because servants had told him frightening stories when he was little. Remember this: a man who spent his life attacking "fictions" was haunted by them as a child.

At **twelve**, he was sent to **Queen's College, Oxford**. He found it dull and the other students rough. He graduated at fifteen.

### The lawyer who refused to practise

His father sent him to train as a lawyer at **Lincoln's Inn**, and he was called to the bar. But when he looked closely at the law, he was disgusted. He found it confused, cruel, expensive and designed for the benefit of lawyers, not the public. He took almost no cases. He told a story about one early case in which he advised a client to settle rather than waste money on lawyers. His father was heartbroken.

Instead of practising law, Bentham decided to **reform** it. That became his life.

### "The greatest happiness of the greatest number"

In **1776**, the year of the American Declaration of Independence and Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations*, Bentham published, anonymously, ***A Fragment on Government***, a sharp attack on Blackstone. It was so good that people guessed it had been written by a famous lawyer or politician. (When his proud father let slip that his son was the author, interest in the book fell.)

In the preface he wrote the sentence that would become the motto of a movement:

"It is the greatest happiness of the greatest number that is the measure of right and wrong."

### The principles book

Bentham then worked on a huge plan for a complete code of criminal law. As an introduction, he wrote ***An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation***, printed in **1780** but not published until **1789**. Its first chapter begins with one of the most famous openings in moral philosophy, which we'll study below.

### Russia and the Panopticon

In **1785–87** Bentham travelled to **Russia** to visit his younger brother **Samuel**, a talented engineer who was managing a large estate at Krichev (in present-day Belarus) for Prince Potemkin. Samuel had designed a workshop in which a supervisor at the centre could watch many workers at once.

Jeremy saw something bigger in this idea. He developed it into the **Panopticon** (Greek for "all-seeing"): a circular building with cells around the outside and an inspection tower in the middle, from which a single inspector could watch every inmate, while the inmates could never tell whether they were being watched at that moment.

For the next twenty years, Bentham poured his energy and much of his fortune into persuading the British government to build a Panopticon prison. In 1794 Parliament approved the plan, and land was even bought. Then the project stalled, was delayed, and was finally abandoned. In **1813** Parliament paid him **£23,000** in compensation. He felt betrayed, and the bitterness had a big effect on him: he concluded that the people in power were not interested in the public good at all, only in their own interests. This pushed him toward **democracy**.

### The radical

Until then, Bentham had hoped that wise rulers would adopt his reforms. Now he decided that rulers would only serve the people if the people could control them. From about **1809** onward, he became a **democrat** and a **radical**: he argued for a much wider vote, the **secret ballot**, annual elections, and eventually, in principle, the vote for **women**.

He gathered around him a group of followers known as the **Philosophical Radicals**, including the economist **David Ricardo**, the historian **James Mill**, and later James Mill's son, **John Stuart Mill**. In **1824** he founded the ***Westminster Review*** to spread radical ideas.

### The hermit of Queen Square Place

Bentham lived for decades in a house at **Queen Square Place** in Westminster, which he called his "Hermitage". He wrote every day, often around **ten to fifteen pages**, sometimes many more. Much of it was never published in his lifetime; the **Bentham Project** at University College London is still editing his tens of thousands of manuscript pages today.

He had his eccentricities. He named his walking stick "**Dapple**" and his teapot "**Dick**". He went on what he called his "ante-prandial circumambulation", a walk before dinner, and his "post-prandial vibration", a walk after. He was fond of cats and kept a cat that he jokingly called "**Sir John Langbourne**", later "the Reverend Sir John Langbourne, D.D." He hated being interrupted.

### A citizen of the world

His fame spread across the world. In **1792** the French National Assembly made him an **honorary citizen** of France. His works, translated and popularised in French by his Swiss editor **Étienne Dumont**, were read in Spain, Portugal, Russia and the new republics of Latin America. Reformers wrote to him asking for constitutions. He offered to write legal codes for the United States, Russia and others. He coined the word "**international**", along with "maximise", "minimise" and "codification".

### Bentham and India

Bentham never visited India, but India mattered to him, and he mattered to India.

James Mill wrote a *History of British India* (1817) and worked at the East India Company in London, where his ideas shaped policy. According to a well-known account, when **Lord William Bentinck** was about to sail to India as Governor-General in 1827, he told Bentham that it was really Bentham who would be governor. Bentinck's administration later ended many practices in the name of reform.

Utilitarian ideas also shaped the **Indian Penal Code**, drafted in the 1830s by a commission led by **Thomas Babington Macaulay** and enacted in **1860**. The very idea of a single, clear, written criminal code, instead of a tangle of customs and cases, is deeply Benthamite. (The IPC governed Indian criminal law for over 160 years, until the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita** replaced it in 2024.)

And when the great Indian reformer **Raja Rammohun Roy** came to England in 1831, Bentham, by then very old, greeted him with warm admiration, as a fellow fighter against superstition and for reform.

### Death and the auto-icon

Bentham died in London on **6 June 1832**, aged eighty-four. The very next day, the **Great Reform Act**, which began to reform Britain's corrupt electoral system, received royal assent. He just missed it.

His last wish was characteristically practical and strange. He had left instructions that his body should be **publicly dissected** for the benefit of medical science, at a time when people feared dissection and bodies for anatomy were scarce. His friend Dr Thomas Southwood Smith lectured over the body. Then the skeleton was preserved, dressed in Bentham's own clothes, seated in a chair with his walking stick Dapple, and topped with a **wax head** (the attempt to preserve his real head went badly wrong). He called this the "**auto-icon**", the self-image.

You can still see it today at **University College London**, which honours him as a spiritual founder because it was the first English university to admit students regardless of religion, a cause he championed. A man who spent his life asking "what is useful?" made even his corpse useful.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: The two sovereign masters

**The idea in one line:** Human beings are governed by pleasure and pain; these explain what we do and also tell us what we ought to do.

Here is the opening of the *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*:

"Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do."

Notice that he makes **two claims** here.

1. **A claim about how we behave** (psychology): everything we do, we do to get pleasure or avoid pain. The student studying all night is avoiding the pain of failure and seeking the pleasure of success. The mother sacrificing for her child gets deep satisfaction from the child's happiness, and feels pain at its suffering. Even the saint fasting in the forest is moved by hope of a deeper peace.

2. **A claim about what is good** (ethics): the only thing good in itself is pleasure (or happiness), and the only thing bad in itself is pain (or suffering). Everything else, money, honour, power, even virtue, is good only because it leads to happiness.

Take a simple test. Ask someone: "Why do you want a job?" "For money." "Why money?" "To buy a house." "Why a house?" "For security and comfort." "Why comfort?" At some point, the answer is: "Because it makes me happy," or "Because I don't want to suffer." And then the questions stop. **Happiness is where the "why" ends.** That is Bentham's insight.

**Why it matters:** Bentham gave morality a solid, earthly foundation. Not God's command, which different religions disagree about, not the king's will, not tradition, but something every human being shares and understands: the difference between suffering and well-being. A Hindu, a Muslim, a Christian and an atheist may argue forever about God, but they all know what it is to be in pain.

**A common misunderstanding:** "So Bentham was a selfish pleasure-seeker." No. Bentham was talking about **everyone's** pleasure and pain, not just your own. The moral question is never "what makes *me* happy?" but "what produces the most happiness *overall*?" And "pleasure" for him included every kind of satisfaction: friendship, knowledge, health, security, the joy of helping others. Bentham himself lived an almost monkish life of work.

### Idea 2: The principle of utility

**The idea in one line:** The right action, law or policy is the one that produces the greatest happiness, the greatest balance of pleasure over pain, for everyone affected.

Bentham called this the **principle of utility**. "Utility" here means the tendency of something to produce benefit, pleasure, good or happiness, or to prevent pain and unhappiness.

The test is simple to state. For any action or law:

1. Identify everyone affected.
2. Estimate the happiness and suffering it will cause each of them.
3. Add it up.
4. Choose the option with the best total.

Back to our helicopter. Forty lives saved produce far more well-being than five. So, go to the first village.

Now consider a law. Should a city ban firecrackers during Diwali? A utilitarian doesn't start with "it's our tradition" or "it's an attack on our culture". She asks: How much joy do firecrackers bring, and to how many? How much suffering does the smoke cause, to asthma patients, children, old people, animals, people across the whole city for weeks? What about the workers in firecracker factories and their safety? Lay it all out, count everyone, and see which way the scale tips. You may reach different conclusions, but notice how the question changes the conversation. It moves us from **identity** to **consequences**.

This is why utilitarianism is called a form of **consequentialism**: the rightness of an act depends entirely on its **consequences**.

**Why it matters:** This principle is behind almost every modern policy debate. When the government compares the cost of building a metro with the time and pollution it will save; when a hospital decides which treatments to fund; when an economist does a "cost-benefit analysis" of a dam; they are, knowingly or not, thinking like Bentham.

**A common misunderstanding:** "The greatest happiness of the greatest number" sounds like "whatever the majority wants". But it isn't majority rule. If ninety people each get a small pleasure from tormenting ten people who suffer terribly, the suffering of the ten may far outweigh the small pleasures of the ninety. Bentham was counting **amounts** of happiness, not votes. (Still, as we'll see, critics worry that the arithmetic can sometimes crush minorities.)

### Idea 3: Everyone counts for one

**The idea in one line:** In the calculation of happiness, every person's happiness counts equally, whether king or beggar.

John Stuart Mill summarised Bentham's position with the formula "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one".

Think how radical this was. In Bentham's England, a lord's comfort counted for far more, in law and in practice, than a labourer's life. In many societies, including caste-bound India, people were born into different values: the suffering of a Dalit was not counted as equal to that of a Brahmin. Utilitarianism says: **pain is pain, whoever feels it.** A rich man's headache does not count more than a poor man's.

Put this alongside B.R. Ambedkar's fierce insistence that a society must be judged by how it treats its weakest members, and you can see why utilitarian thinking, with its equal counting, became a powerful tool for reformers. If everyone counts, you can't simply ignore the landless labourer, the manual scavenger, the woman shut inside the house.

**Why it matters:** Equal counting is the hidden root of welfare states, public health and anti-poverty programmes. A rupee given to a starving family produces far more happiness than a rupee given to a billionaire (economists call this "**diminishing marginal utility**", and it flows directly from Bentham). So utilitarian logic tends to push toward helping the poor.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Everyone counts equally" doesn't mean everyone gets the same outcome. It means everyone's happiness gets the same **weight** in the calculation. Sometimes, that leads to treating people differently: a hungry person needs food more than a full one.

### Idea 4: The felicific calculus

**The idea in one line:** Pleasures and pains can be measured along several dimensions, so that we can calculate which action does the most good.

Bentham was a man who wanted precision. Moral arguments, he complained, were full of vague words and emotional slogans. He wanted to make morality almost like **arithmetic**. So he proposed what later writers called the "**felicific calculus**" (from the Latin *felicitas*, happiness).

To measure a pleasure or pain, he said, consider its:

1. **Intensity**: how strong is it?
2. **Duration**: how long does it last?
3. **Certainty**: how likely is it to happen?
4. **Propinquity** (nearness): how soon will it come?
5. **Fecundity**: will it lead to more pleasures (or more pains)?
6. **Purity**: is it free of pain mixed in? (Or, for a pain, of pleasure mixed in?)
7. **Extent**: how many people does it affect?

He even wrote a little rhyme to help students remember:

"Intense, long, certain, speedy, fruitful, pure—
Such marks in pleasures and in pains endure."

Let's try it. You're a college student, and it's the night before an exam. Option A: watch a new web series all night. Option B: study.

- **Intensity:** the series is more enjoyable tonight.
- **Duration:** that pleasure lasts a few hours; the satisfaction of a good result may last much longer.
- **Certainty:** watching gives certain pleasure; studying gives a likely, but not certain, better mark.
- **Propinquity:** the series' pleasure is immediate; the exam reward is days away. (This is why we procrastinate! Near pleasures feel bigger.)
- **Fecundity:** studying leads to more good things, a degree, a job, confidence. The series leads to... more episodes.
- **Purity:** the series pleasure is mixed with guilt and panic the next morning. Impure!
- **Extent:** your result affects your parents' happiness too.

Add it up, and studying wins, though you probably knew that already. Bentham's point was that this kind of thinking can be done more carefully, especially for **laws**, which affect millions.

**Why it matters:** The felicific calculus is the ancestor of modern **cost-benefit analysis**, **welfare economics**, and the measurement of things like "**quality-adjusted life years**" (QALYs), which health systems around the world use to decide which treatments to fund. When an economist calculates how many "disability-adjusted life years" a vaccination programme will save, she's doing Bentham's calculus with better data.

**A common misunderstanding:** Bentham didn't think we should sit down with a calculator before every action. He wrote that the calculation should be "kept in view" as a guide, especially for legislators, not performed exactly every time. Most of the time, rules of thumb based on experience will do.

### Idea 5: Push-pin and poetry

**The idea in one line:** Pleasures differ only in quantity, not in quality; no pleasure is "higher" in itself.

This is one of Bentham's most provocative claims. "Push-pin" was a simple children's game, a bit like flicking pins on a table. Bentham wrote, in *The Rationale of Reward*:

"Prejudice apart, the game of push-pin is of equal value with the arts and sciences of music and poetry."

His point: if a game gives as much pleasure as a poem, it is just as valuable. Who are the cultured elites to say that their pleasures, opera, philosophy, classical music, are "higher" than the pleasures of ordinary people, cricket, film songs, street food?

Think about the snobbery in our own society. Some people sneer at Bollywood, at reels, at people who enjoy masala films or a game of carrom. Bentham would say: if it makes people happy and harms no one, by what right do you call it inferior?

**Why it matters:** This is a deeply **democratic** idea. It refuses to rank people's ways of being happy according to class or education. It also makes the calculus possible: if all pleasures are of one kind, they can be added and compared.

**A common misunderstanding:** Bentham did not say poetry is worthless. He said its value lies in the pleasure it gives, and only in that. Indeed, he thought poetry often has bad "fecundity", because poets, he grumbled, deal in exaggeration and misrepresentation. But this view became the target of his most famous follower: John Stuart Mill would argue that some pleasures are of **higher quality**, and that it is "better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied". We'll see that debate shortly.

### Idea 6: Natural rights are "nonsense upon stilts"

**The idea in one line:** Rights don't exist in nature; they are created by laws, and should be judged by their consequences.

The American and French revolutionaries declared that human beings have **natural rights**, rights that exist before any government, by nature or from God, that no law can take away. John Locke had argued this a century earlier.

Bentham thought this was dangerous nonsense. In his essay attacking the French Declaration of Rights (known as *Anarchical Fallacies*), he wrote:

"Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense, nonsense upon stilts."

("Imprescriptible" means cannot be taken away. "On stilts" means nonsense raised up high, nonsense pretending to be grand.)

Why so harsh? Bentham's argument goes like this:

- A **right** is a claim that others must respect, backed by enforcement. Where does that enforcement come from? From **law**. "Right is the child of law," he said.
- If you say you have a "natural right" to something, but no law protects it, you have, in reality, only a **wish** that you had a right.
- Worse, natural-rights talk makes every political disagreement into an absolute, non-negotiable conflict. If I claim my "natural right" to property, and you claim your "natural right" to food, and these clash, there's no way to settle it except by fighting. Bentham watched the French Revolution, with its grand declarations of rights, collapse into the Terror, and felt confirmed.

Instead, he said, ask: *what rights should the law create? Which rights, protected by law, will produce the most happiness?* This makes rights a matter of argument and evidence, not of shouting.

**Why it matters:** Look at the **Indian Constitution**. Its **Fundamental Rights** are not absolute. Article 19 grants freedom of speech, but allows "reasonable restrictions" in the interests of public order, decency, the security of the state and so on. Courts constantly balance rights against social consequences. That is much closer to Bentham's spirit than to the idea of absolute natural rights. On the other hand, the Supreme Court's "**basic structure**" doctrine treats some features of the Constitution as beyond the reach of any majority, which is a more rights-first idea. India lives in the tension between the two.

**A common misunderstanding:** Bentham was not against rights. He wanted the law to give people **more** rights: free speech, the vote, legal equality, protection from cruelty. He just wanted to justify these rights by their consequences, not by an appeal to "nature". In a sense, he thought the natural-rights crowd were defending good things with bad arguments.

### Idea 7: Punishment is itself an evil

**The idea in one line:** Every punishment causes suffering, so it can be justified only if it prevents greater suffering.

Bentham wrote:

"But all punishment is mischief: all punishment in itself is evil. Upon the principle of utility, if it ought at all to be admitted, it ought only to be admitted in as far as it promises to exclude some greater evil."

This sounds simple. But think how revolutionary it was. Most people then (and many now) think of punishment as **revenge**, or as giving people what they "deserve". The thief deserves to suffer. Hang him!

Bentham said: wait. Hanging the thief adds one more suffering to the world. Unless it prevents more suffering, by deterring others, protecting society, or reforming the criminal, it is just cruelty with paperwork.

He then listed cases where punishment should **not** be inflicted:

- Where it is **groundless**: no real harm was done.
- Where it is **inefficacious**: it cannot prevent the crime (for example, punishing someone who was insane, or acted under compulsion).
- Where it is **unprofitable**, or "too expensive": the suffering it causes is greater than the harm it prevents.
- Where it is **needless**: the harm can be prevented more cheaply some other way, by education, for instance.

And punishment should be **proportionate**: just enough to deter, no more. If stealing a loaf of bread and murder are both punished by death, the thief has no reason not to kill the witness.

**Why it matters:** This logic drives modern debates about the death penalty, prison reform, juvenile justice and alternatives to imprisonment. When India's Supreme Court limited the death penalty to the "**rarest of rare**" cases (*Bachan Singh*, 1980), or when reformers argue that undertrials should not rot in jail for minor offences, or that drug users need treatment rather than prison, they echo Bentham. The question shifts from "what does this person deserve?" to "what will actually reduce harm?"

**A common misunderstanding:** Bentham was not soft on crime. He wanted punishment to be **certain** and **swift**, because he believed certainty deters better than severity. A thief who knows he will certainly be caught and fined is more deterred than one who knows there's a tiny chance of being hanged. This is why, today, criminologists say the certainty of being caught matters more than the harshness of the sentence.

### Idea 8: The Panopticon

**The idea in one line:** Design can make people behave well, by making them feel they might always be watched.

Picture the Panopticon. A circular building. Cells around the edge, each with a window to the outside and a barred opening facing inward. In the middle, a tower, with blinds or shutters, so that the inspector can see into every cell but the inmates can't see whether the inspector is there.

The genius, Bentham thought, was this: **the inmates never know whether they are being watched at any given moment, so they must behave as though they are always watched.** One inspector could control hundreds. Eventually, they might not need an inspector at all; the inmates would watch themselves.

Bentham was ecstatic. He described the plan's benefits in a breathless list, ending: "all by a simple idea in Architecture!" He thought it could be used for prisons, factories, workhouses, hospitals and schools. He planned that the prison would be run by a contractor (himself) who would profit from the inmates' work, but who would also pay a fine for every prisoner who died, giving him an incentive to keep them healthy.

**Why it matters:** The Panopticon became famous again in the twentieth century, but not as Bentham hoped. The French philosopher **Michel Foucault**, in *Discipline and Punish* (1975), made it the symbol of **modern power**: a society where people are constantly observed, measured, recorded and examined, until they discipline themselves. Schools with exams and attendance registers, factories with productivity monitoring, offices with keystroke trackers, cities full of CCTV, and above all smartphones and social media that track everything we do. Today, the Panopticon is in your pocket.

Think about how you behave when you know a CCTV camera is on. Or how some delivery workers, monitored by an app that times every stop, feel they can never relax. Or how, on social media, people perform their lives as if an invisible audience were always watching. That is Bentham's architecture turned into everyday life.

**A common misunderstanding:** Bentham did not intend the Panopticon to be sinister. He meant it as a **humane** reform: cleaner, healthier and less brutal than the filthy, chaotic prisons of his day, where inmates were chained and beaten. He also wanted the inspection tower itself to be open to the public, so that the inspectors would be watched too ("who watches the watchmen?" answered by: the public). But his idea shows how a reform aimed at kindness can create new forms of control.

### Idea 9: Interests, sinister and otherwise

**The idea in one line:** People in power, like everyone else, pursue their own interests, so institutions must be designed to align their interests with the public's.

After the Panopticon failed, Bentham developed a theory that explains a great deal about politics. He noticed that rulers, judges, lawyers and officials all have personal interests that may differ from the public interest. He called these "**sinister interests**".

Why was the law so complicated? Because complicated law benefits **lawyers**. Why were elections corrupt? Because corruption benefits the **aristocrats** who controlled Parliament. Why was church wealth untaxed? Because it benefited **bishops**. The problem isn't that these people are uniquely evil. It's that the **system** rewards them for acting against the public.

His solution: design institutions so that it is in officials' interests to serve the public. Make them accountable through elections, publicity, a free press, and transparency. "**Publicity**", openness to public view, was his great weapon. He wanted government accounts, court proceedings and official decisions to be visible to all.

**Why it matters:** This is the foundation of modern thinking about **accountability**, **transparency** and **institutional design**. India's **Right to Information Act** (2005), which lets any citizen demand information from public authorities, is a deeply Benthamite law. So are public audits, the **CAG**, social audits of MGNREGA in villages, live-streamed court proceedings, and asset declarations by candidates. The idea is not to hope for saintly officials but to build systems where honesty pays and corruption is exposed.

**A common misunderstanding:** Bentham wasn't cynical about human nature. He simply thought that if you build institutions assuming everyone will be a saint, you'll be disappointed, while if you design them assuming people follow their interests, you can make the system work even with ordinary people. James Madison, in the American *Federalist Papers*, made a similar point: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary."

### Idea 10: Following the principle wherever it leads

**The idea in one line:** Applied honestly, the happiness principle demands reforms that shock the comfortable.

This is perhaps the most impressive thing about Bentham. He followed his principle wherever it led, even against the prejudices of his time. And it led to astonishing places. He argued for:

- **Universal suffrage**, and in principle, **votes for women**, at a time when only a small fraction of men could vote.
- The **secret ballot**, so voters could not be bullied by landlords.
- **Abolition of slavery.**
- **Abolition of the death penalty** (in his later years).
- **Separation of church and state.**
- **Freedom of speech and of the press.**
- **Equal rights for women** in many areas of life.
- **Decriminalisation of homosexuality**: in essays written around **1785** (unpublished for nearly two centuries), he argued that sex between consenting men harmed no one, and that punishing it, by death at the time, caused only suffering. The arguments of disgust, "unnatural", "God forbids", did not count, because they pointed to no actual harm.
- **Animal welfare**, which we'll see next.
- **Reform of usury laws**, interest rate caps, which he argued against in *Defence of Usury* (1787).
- **Public health, sanitation and education**, and a **civil service** chosen by merit through examinations.

Now place this list next to the history of India. When the Supreme Court decriminalised homosexuality in ***Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India*** (2018), it struck down part of Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, a law imported from Victorian England. Bentham had argued against that kind of law more than two hundred years earlier, using exactly the question: **where is the harm?**

**Why it matters:** Bentham shows the power of a principle consistently applied. Many people have principles but abandon them when they become inconvenient. Bentham asked the same question of every custom, however sacred, and accepted the answer even when it embarrassed him. That's why his conclusions were often a century or two ahead of his society.

**A common misunderstanding:** Some people think utilitarianism is a cold, conservative, "business" philosophy. Bentham's own list shows the opposite: he was one of the most radical reformers in history. Utilitarianism can be cold in some applications, but in his hands it was a battering ram against cruelty.

### Idea 11: Can they suffer?

**The idea in one line:** What matters morally is the capacity to suffer, not the ability to reason, so animals count too.

In a footnote in the *Introduction*, Bentham wrote one of the most influential sentences in the history of ethics:

"The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

His argument was simple. Philosophers had long said that animals don't matter morally because they lack reason or language. But if the only thing bad in itself is suffering, then the question is whether a being can feel pain. A dog, a cow, a horse can obviously suffer. So their suffering counts.

He even compared the treatment of animals to slavery: just as the colour of a person's skin is no reason to abandon them to torment, the number of legs or the hairiness of skin is no reason to abandon a sentient being. A full-grown horse or dog, he said, is more rational and conversable than an infant a few days old.

**Why it matters:** This sentence inspired **Peter Singer's *Animal Liberation*** (1975) and the modern animal welfare movement. It lies behind laws against cruelty, campaigns about factory farming and animal testing. India, of course, has a far older tradition here: **ahimsa** in Jainism, Buddhism and Hinduism, the Buddha's compassion for all sentient beings, and Article 51A(g) of the Constitution, which makes it a duty of every citizen "to have compassion for living creatures". Bentham reached, by arithmetic, a conclusion that the Buddha and Mahavira had reached by compassion.

**A common misunderstanding:** Bentham did not say animals and humans are equal in every way. He accepted killing animals for food, provided it was done without needless suffering, since he thought animals didn't suffer from anticipating death as we do. Singer and later utilitarians pushed his logic further. But the key move, counting suffering wherever it occurs, was his.

### Idea 12: Fictions and clear language

**The idea in one line:** Much bad thinking hides behind vague words; clear language exposes it.

Bentham was obsessed with language. He believed many words we use, "rights", "duty", "the nation", "nature", "the constitution", are **fictions**: words that seem to name things but don't, unless they can be translated into statements about real pleasures, pains and actions.

For example, "duty". What does it actually mean to say "you have a duty to pay taxes"? Bentham would say: it means that if you don't pay, you are likely to suffer a penalty. That's a real, checkable claim. Without such translation, "duty" just floats in the air, and anyone can use it to make you obey.

He also hated legal jargon, which he saw as a way for lawyers to keep the public ignorant. He wanted laws written so that every citizen could read and understand them. That's why he pushed for **codification**: replacing scattered case law with clear written codes.

**Why it matters:** Think of the fine print on a loan agreement, an insurance policy, a government circular full of jargon that ordinary people cannot follow. Who benefits from that confusion? Bentham's answer: usually the powerful. Movements for "plain language" in law and government, and India's efforts to translate laws into regional languages and explain schemes simply, follow his spirit.

**A common misunderstanding:** Bentham did not want to ban useful abstractions. He wanted them **cashed out**: every abstract word must eventually be connected to real experiences. It's a demand for honesty in language.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Hume: the scales fall

Bentham said that when he read the third book of **David Hume's** *Treatise of Human Nature*, on morals, he felt "as if scales had fallen from my eyes". Hume had shown that what we call virtue is closely tied to what is **useful** and agreeable to human beings. Bentham took this observation and made it into a single, sharp principle, and then a programme for reform. Hume was a careful observer of human nature; Bentham was a reformer who wanted to change it.

### Locke and the revolutionaries: no natural rights

**John Locke** grounded government in natural rights to "life, liberty and estate", and the American and French revolutionaries built on him. Bentham rejected the foundation while often agreeing with the conclusions. Picture them arguing about free speech:

*Locke:* People have a natural right to liberty that no government may violate.

*Bentham:* Show me this right. Where is it written? Who enforces it? There is no right without law. But let's make a law protecting free speech, because free speech produces enormous happiness: it exposes corrupt officials, spreads knowledge and lets people pursue their own good.

*Locke:* So you'd protect speech only as long as it's useful? What if the government decides suppressing speech is more useful?

*Bentham:* Then I'll argue with evidence that they're wrong.

*Locke:* And if they don't listen? With natural rights, I can say they've broken a moral limit. With your arithmetic, you can only say they've miscalculated.

That exchange captures a debate still alive today.

### Burke: strange allies

**Edmund Burke** was a conservative who revered tradition; Bentham was a radical who mocked it. Yet both attacked the French Declaration of the Rights of Man. Burke said abstract rights ignored the complex, inherited wisdom of real societies. Bentham said they were meaningless and dangerous. Both feared that grand abstractions could justify terror. But they drew opposite lessons: Burke defended old institutions; Bentham wanted to rebuild them from the ground up using reason and calculation. The "wisdom of our ancestors", Bentham said, was usually the folly of our ancestors.

### Adam Smith: be consistent

Bentham admired **Adam Smith**, but in his *Defence of Usury* (1787) he pointed out an inconsistency. Smith had supported free markets generally, but had also supported a legal **cap on interest rates**, so that money would not go to "prodigals and projectors" (spendthrifts and risky speculators). Bentham argued: if adults can freely buy and sell everything else, why not money? Interest caps harm the very borrowers they claim to protect, because lenders simply refuse to lend to them, driving them to illegal moneylenders. The debate about interest caps on microfinance and payday loans continues today.

### Kant: the great rival tradition

Bentham and **Immanuel Kant** were contemporaries who never engaged each other directly, but they represent the two great rival schools of modern ethics. For Kant, morality is about **duty** and treating every person as an **end in themselves**, never merely as a means. For Bentham, morality is about **consequences**, the total of happiness. In the "push someone off the bridge" case from our opening, Kant says: you may never use an innocent person as a tool, whatever the numbers. Bentham's arithmetic seems to say: one death against forty, push. Almost every argument in modern ethics is, in one way or another, a conversation between these two.

### John Stuart Mill: the heir who rebelled

**John Stuart Mill** was raised, by his father James Mill, as the heir of Benthamism. After a mental crisis, he concluded that Bentham's picture of human nature was too narrow: it left out feeling, imagination, poetry and the cultivation of character. In his essay on Bentham (1838), Mill praised him as "the great questioner of things established" but said that he lacked imagination and understanding of the inner life. Mill revised utilitarianism by distinguishing **higher and lower pleasures** and by giving liberty a central place. He kept the master's principle but softened his edges.

### Marx: the shopkeeper's philosopher

**Karl Marx** despised Bentham. In *Capital* he called him "the arch-philistine" and "a genius in the way of bourgeois stupidity", accusing him of taking the modern English shopkeeper as the model of the "normal man" and judging everything by that measure. For Marx, Bentham's calculus ignored the structures of class and exploitation: you can't add up happiness while ignoring who owns the factories. Interestingly, Marx and Bentham shared a hatred of hypocrisy and a desire to unmask the interests hidden behind grand words. They disagreed about whose interests mattered and how to change the system.

### Later voices

- **Henry Sidgwick** (1838–1900) refined utilitarianism into its most rigorous classical form.
- **Peter Singer** (born 1946) extended Bentham's concern for animals and argued that the comfortable have strong duties to help the global poor.
- **John Rawls** (1921–2002) criticised utilitarianism for not taking seriously "the distinctness of persons": adding up happiness across people treats society as if it were one big person, so that one person's suffering can be "balanced" by another's gain.
- **Michel Foucault** made the Panopticon a symbol of modern disciplinary power.
- **Amartya Sen** criticised the reduction of well-being to pleasure, arguing that we should look at people's **capabilities**, what they are actually able to do and be.

## See it around you

Once you understand Bentham, you start seeing his question everywhere.

**Cost-benefit analysis in government.** When the government decides whether to build an expressway, dam or airport, it estimates the costs (money, displacement, environmental damage) and benefits (time saved, jobs, trade). Project reports include figures like "economic rate of return". That is the felicific calculus in a modern suit. And the arguments over the **Narmada dams**, where activists like Medha Patkar argued that the suffering of displaced Adivasis was being undercounted, were arguments about whether the calculation had counted everyone properly.

**Triage in hospitals and disasters.** In an emergency ward, doctors sort patients by who can be saved and who needs care most urgently. During the COVID-19 vaccine rollout, India prioritised health workers, the elderly and those with comorbidities first. Why? Because that order would save the most lives. Pure Bentham.

**Welfare schemes and targeting.** Free rations under the National Food Security Act, direct benefit transfers, subsidised LPG for poor households: these rest on the idea that the same rupee produces more happiness for the poor than for the rich. Debates over "freebies" are often debates about utility: does this spending produce more welfare than investing in roads or schools?

**Traffic fines and deterrence.** When the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act of 2019 sharply increased traffic fines, the argument was Benthamite: make the expected pain of breaking the rules greater than the pleasure of breaking them. And the observation that people drive carefully near cameras but not elsewhere is Bentham's point about **certainty** beating severity.

**The CCTV and the app.** Cameras at traffic signals, attendance tracked by biometric machines, delivery partners rated and timed by algorithms, social media where everyone is watched by everyone: these are the Panopticon spread through society. Ask yourself, when you behave differently because you might be recorded, who designed that feeling, and who benefits?

**RTI and transparency.** Every RTI application is an act of Benthamite "publicity", dragging the decisions of officials into the light where sinister interests struggle to hide.

**Animal welfare.** Debates about stray dogs, cattle in shelters, circus animals, cosmetics testing (India banned testing cosmetics on animals in 2014) and the conditions of poultry farms all turn on Bentham's question: can they suffer?

**Effective altruism and charity.** A movement called "**effective altruism**", inspired by Peter Singer, asks donors to give where their money will reduce the most suffering: for example, malaria nets that save lives cheaply. Choosing a charity by evidence of impact, not just by emotion, is Bentham's calculus applied to kindness.

**Your own daily choices.** Whether to take the job with more money or the one near your parents; whether to tell a friend a painful truth; whether to spend on a wedding feast for a thousand guests or on your children's education. Whenever you weigh consequences for everyone involved, you're thinking with Bentham.

## Use it

Here are practical ways to use Bentham's thinking, and to use it wisely.

**1. Ask "who is affected, and how much?"** Before a big decision, list everyone it will affect, not just yourself. Your family, colleagues, neighbours, strangers, animals, future people. People who are not in the room are the easiest to forget, and Bentham's equal counting forces you to remember them.

**2. Run the seven-point check.** For a tricky choice, run through Bentham's dimensions: How intense? How long? How certain? How soon? Will it lead to more good or more bad? Is it mixed with harm? How many people? You'll be surprised how often "how long" and "will it lead to more" change your answer. Short pleasures with long pains (the extra drink, the angry message, the impulsive purchase on EMI) look different once you count them properly.

**3. When someone appeals to tradition, ask "what good does it do?"** "We've always done it this way" is not a reason. Neither is "it's unnatural" or "it's against our culture". Ask: what real harm or benefit does this custom produce, and for whom? Sometimes traditions survive the test (they may contain hard-won wisdom); sometimes they don't. Dowry, child marriage and caste discrimination did not survive Bentham's question.

**4. Look for the sinister interest.** When a rule is confusing, slow or unfair, ask: who benefits from it being this way? The middleman, the official, the lawyer, the company? Then think about how to change the incentives, not just complain about bad people.

**5. Prefer certainty over severity.** If you're a parent, teacher or manager, small, consistent, certain consequences work better than rare, explosive ones. A child who knows that screen time will definitely be reduced for breaking a rule learns faster than one threatened with a punishment that never comes.

**6. Translate the big words.** When a politician, boss or advertisement uses grand words, "national pride", "family values", "the future", ask what they mean in terms of real experiences: who will be better off, who will suffer, and how? If no answer comes, you may be looking at one of Bentham's fictions.

**7. Count the voiceless.** Animals, children, future generations, people far away. They can't lobby, vote or complain loudly, but they can suffer. Make a habit of asking, "And what about them?"

**8. And then check the arithmetic against your conscience.** If your calculation tells you to do something that sacrifices an innocent person, betrays a promise or treats someone merely as a tool, stop. That sense of horror is information. It may mean you've left something important out of the calculation, like trust, dignity or justice. Use Bentham to widen your view, not to switch off your moral sense.

## Where he went wrong

**1. The innocent scapegoat.** The most famous objection: utilitarianism seems to allow terrible acts if the numbers work out. Imagine a town where riots will kill many people unless the police find a culprit for a crime. The sheriff could frame and hang an innocent man to calm the mob. The total happiness might go up. Yet almost everyone feels that this would be monstrously unjust. Utilitarians reply that such acts, if known, would destroy trust in justice and cause more harm in the long run. But critics say the theory still gets the reason wrong: framing the innocent is wrong because it is **unjust**, not because it might be discovered.

**2. Minorities and the tyranny of the sum.** If the pleasures of a large majority outweigh the suffering of a small minority, does the minority lose? History is full of majorities that enjoyed persecuting minorities. Rawls's objection, that utilitarianism ignores "the distinctness of persons", strikes here: my suffering is not cancelled by your pleasure. This is why constitutions protect fundamental rights that majorities cannot override by vote.

**3. Can happiness really be measured?** How do you compare one person's pleasure in a cricket win with another's grief at losing a pet? Who decides how intense a pleasure is? Bentham's calculus promised precision but was never actually performed in the form he imagined. Modern economics uses money or preferences as stand-ins, but these have their own biases, since the rich can express their preferences with more money.

**4. Push-pin and the flatness of pleasure.** Many people feel that Bentham's view of happiness is too thin. Is a life of pleasant intoxication as good as a life of love, achievement and understanding? Mill thought not. And thinkers like Aristotle and the Buddha would say that real well-being is about flourishing, wisdom or freedom from craving, not maximising pleasant sensations. Amartya Sen argues that a deprived person may learn to be content with very little, and that counting only their "happiness" would hide their deprivation.

**5. Rights as mere tools.** By treating rights as useful conventions, Bentham may leave them too fragile. If rights are only valuable when they produce good results, then whenever a government can argue that violating them would produce better results, for "security", "development" or "national interest", they're vulnerable. The human rights movement after the Second World War returned to something like natural rights precisely because the horrors of the century showed how easily "useful" arguments could justify atrocities.

**6. Surveillance and control.** The Panopticon shows the dark side of a philosophy focused on managing behaviour. A world designed to make people behave well by constant observation can become a world without privacy, trust or freedom. Bentham was so sure of his good intentions that he sometimes didn't see how his tools could be used by others.

**7. Empire and the improver's arrogance.** Utilitarian reformers in India often believed they were bringing reason to a "backward" society. James Mill's *History of British India*, written without visiting India or knowing its languages, dismissed Indian civilisation with contempt. The reformist urge produced some real goods, such as a codified criminal law and campaigns against some cruel practices, but it also justified colonial rule as "improvement", ignoring Indians' own voices and the right of a people to govern themselves.

**8. Psychology too simple.** Is it really true that everything we do is to gain pleasure or avoid pain? A soldier who dies for comrades, a mother who suffers for her child, a person who keeps a promise at great cost: we can force their motives into "pleasure and pain", but it begins to sound like a word game. Human motivation includes duty, love, meaning and identity in ways that a pleasure-pain machine doesn't easily capture.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought of morality as a set of rules handed down, by religion, family or tradition, and of law as something fixed that you simply obey. You may have assumed that "the way things are" had some good reason behind it.

**Now,** you can see morality and law as **tools** for reducing suffering and increasing well-being, tools that can be tested, measured and improved. You can see that behind many rules lie hidden interests, that every punishment is itself a harm to be justified, and that the suffering of the poor, the despised and even animals counts exactly as much as the comfort of the powerful. And you can also see the danger: that a calculation which forgets dignity, justice and the separateness of persons can justify cruelty in the name of the greater good.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A government scheme** becomes a question: who gains, who loses, and was everyone counted?
- **A harsh punishment**, in school, at home or in law, prompts you to ask whether it actually prevents harm, or just expresses anger.
- **A tradition defended only as tradition** loses its automatic authority over you.
- **A confusing form or law** makes you wonder whose interest the confusion serves.
- **A CCTV camera or a tracking app** reminds you of the Panopticon, and of how being watched changes us.
- **A stray dog shivering in the rain** makes Bentham's question echo: can they suffer?
- **A "greater good" argument** makes you check both the arithmetic and the conscience, because a sum that sacrifices the innocent has left something out.

Jeremy Bentham was afraid of ghosts, named his teapot, and still sits in a glass case in London wearing his own clothes. He also asked, with relentless honesty, a question that has freed millions of people from cruel laws and stupid customs: *does this actually make anyone's life better?* Once you have learned to ask it, you can't stop.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), English philosopher, legal reformer and founder of classical utilitarianism; trained as a lawyer but devoted his life to reforming the law.
- **Context:** a cruel, chaotic common law (the "Bloody Code"), Blackstone's complacent defence of it, the age of revolutions and natural rights, Britain's growing empire in India.
- **Foundation:** "two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure"; the only things good or bad in themselves.
- **Principle of utility:** right actions and laws produce the greatest happiness for all affected, each counting for one.
- **Felicific calculus:** intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent.
- **Rights:** created by law; natural rights are "nonsense upon stilts".
- **Punishment:** itself an evil, justified only if it prevents greater harm; should be proportionate and certain.
- **Panopticon:** the all-seeing prison; later a symbol of surveillance society (Foucault).
- **Radical reforms:** wider suffrage, secret ballot, votes for women in principle, free speech, abolition of slavery, decriminalisation of homosexuality, animal welfare ("Can they suffer?"), codification, transparency.
- **Influence:** J.S. Mill, Indian Penal Code via Macaulay, welfare economics, cost-benefit analysis, Peter Singer, effective altruism.
- **Criticisms:** sacrifices innocents and minorities, ignores the distinctness of persons, can't truly measure happiness, too flat a view of pleasure, leaves rights fragile, enables surveillance and colonial "improvement".

**Key terms**

- **Utilitarianism:** the theory that the right action produces the greatest total happiness.
- **Principle of utility:** judge every action or law by its tendency to increase or decrease happiness.
- **Consequentialism:** the view that rightness depends only on consequences.
- **Felicific calculus:** Bentham's method for measuring pleasures and pains.
- **Push-pin:** a children's game Bentham used to argue that pleasures differ only in quantity.
- **Natural rights:** rights supposed to exist before and independent of law; Bentham rejected them.
- **Sinister interest:** the private interest of officials or groups that conflicts with the public good.
- **Panopticon:** a prison design in which inmates can always be watched without knowing when.
- **Codification:** replacing scattered case law with a clear written code.
- **Auto-icon:** Bentham's preserved skeleton, dressed and displayed at University College London.
