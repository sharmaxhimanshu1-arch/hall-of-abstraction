## Life

Jeremy Bentham was born in London in 1748, the son of a prosperous attorney. A precocious child, he was reading Latin at four and entered Queen's College, Oxford, at twelve. He trained for the bar at Lincoln's Inn, but legal practice disgusted him. As a young man he heard the lectures of William Blackstone, the great authority on English law, and was appalled by what he saw as complacent defences of an irrational system.

His first major publication, *A Fragment on Government* (1776), was an anonymous attack on Blackstone. In it he announced the principle that would guide the rest of his life: "it is the greatest happiness of the greatest number that is the measure of right and wrong." His *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* was printed in 1780 and published in 1789.

While visiting his brother Samuel in Russia in the 1780s, Bentham developed the idea of the **Panopticon**, a circular prison in which a single watchman could observe every inmate without their knowing whether they were being watched. He spent some two decades and much of his fortune trying to have it built in Britain. Parliament eventually abandoned the scheme and paid him compensation.

Bentham lived for most of his life in London, writing obsessively, often tens of thousands of words a month, on law, politics, economics, religion, language and logic. Much of it remained in manuscript. He gathered around him the "Philosophical Radicals", including James Mill and his son John Stuart Mill, who campaigned for democratic reform. The French Republic made him an honorary citizen in 1792. He died in June 1832, the day before the Great Reform Act received royal assent. At his request his body was dissected for medical science, and his preserved skeleton, dressed in his clothes and topped with a wax head, sits in a glass case at University College London as his "auto-icon".

## Key ideas

**The principle of utility.** Humans are governed by pleasure and pain. The right action, law or policy is the one that produces the greatest happiness, the greatest balance of pleasure over pain, for all those affected.

**The felicific calculus.** Bentham proposed measuring pleasures and pains by their intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (nearness), fecundity, purity and extent. In principle, legislators could calculate which course of action does the most good.

**Against natural rights.** Rights are created by laws. Talk of rights existing independently of law is fiction: "Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense, nonsense upon stilts."

**Law reform.** Bentham wanted to replace the tangle of English common law with clear, rational, codified law. He coined the words "codification", "international" and "maximise". Punishment, he argued, is itself an evil, justified only to the extent that it prevents greater harm.

**Radical causes.** Following his principle wherever it led, Bentham advocated universal suffrage including for women, the secret ballot, the separation of church and state, freedom of expression, the abolition of slavery and of the death penalty, and, in essays unpublished in his lifetime, the decriminalisation of homosexual acts. On animals he wrote: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

## Influence and legacy

Bentham's influence on law and public administration in Britain was immense: prison reform, sanitary reform, the civil service, and the extension of the franchise all bear traces of utilitarian thinking. He inspired, though he did not found, University College London, the first English university open to students regardless of religion.

The Panopticon became, through Michel Foucault's *Discipline and Punish* (1975), a symbol of modern surveillance. In ethics, his principle remains the starting point of consequentialism, and his concern for animal suffering inspired Peter Singer's *Animal Liberation*.
