## Start here

Think about the last time you posted something online and kept checking how many people had liked it.

Or the last wedding you went to where the family had borrowed money they didn't have, because "what will people think" if the feast was smaller than the neighbour's. Or the student who chooses a career not because she loves it but because it sounds impressive to relatives. Or the person who earns more than he ever dreamed of as a boy, and still feels poor, because his colleague has a bigger car.

Here is a strange thought. Most of these unhappinesses don't come from hunger, cold or danger. They come from **comparison**: from living in the eyes of others. We are not satisfied with having enough; we need to have **more than someone else**, and to be **seen** to have it.

Where did this come from? Is it human nature? Or is it something society has done to us?

A runaway watchmaker's son from Geneva, with no university education, asked exactly this question in the 1750s. His answer shocked Europe. Human beings, he said, are naturally good, free and self-sufficient. It is **society**, with its property, its inequality and its endless comparison, that has made us vain, anxious, dependent and cruel. "**Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains.**"

His name was **Jean-Jacques Rousseau**. He wrote a novel that made all of Europe weep, a book on education that transformed how parents and teachers think about children, and a short book on politics, ***The Social Contract***, that became the bible of the French Revolution. His books were burned in Paris and Geneva; he was hunted from country to country; he quarrelled with nearly every friend he had. Kant kept only one picture on his study wall: Rousseau's.

He also gave his own five children away to an orphanage.

By the end of this page, you will understand his explosive ideas about inequality, freedom, democracy and education, why they inspired both democrats and dictators, and how they help explain the comparing, competing, never-satisfied world we live in.

## The world he was born into

### Geneva, a small republic

Rousseau was born in **1712** in **Geneva**, a small, independent, Protestant city-republic in the Alps. Geneva was the city of **John Calvin**, strict in religion and morals. It was governed, in theory, by its citizens; in practice, by a small group of wealthy families in the councils. Ordinary citizens, like Rousseau's watchmaker father, were proud of being "citizens of Geneva", but resented the patricians who held real power.

Rousseau always signed himself "**Citizen of Geneva**". The ideal of a small republic of equal, virtuous citizens governing themselves stayed with him all his life.

### France and the old regime

Most of his adult life was spent in **France**, the largest and richest kingdom in Europe, governed by an absolute monarch, **Louis XV**. French society was a strict hierarchy: the clergy and nobility enjoyed privileges and paid few taxes, while peasants and workers carried the burden. In Paris, an elegant world of salons, theatres, fashion and wit flourished, built on the labour of the poor.

### The Enlightenment

This was the age of the **Enlightenment**, when the **philosophes**, Voltaire, Diderot, d'Alembert and others, attacked superstition, religious intolerance and arbitrary power, and celebrated reason, science and progress. Diderot and d'Alembert's great ***Encyclopédie*** gathered all human knowledge to spread enlightenment.

Rousseau began as a friend and collaborator of the philosophes; he wrote articles on music and political economy for the *Encyclopédie*. But he came to ask a question that horrified them: **what if all this progress has made us worse, not better?**

### Hobbes and Locke

In political philosophy, the great debate was over the **social contract**: **Hobbes** had argued that people agree to an absolute sovereign to escape the war of all against all; **Locke** had argued that they set up a limited government to protect their natural rights to life, liberty and property. Rousseau would challenge both.

## The life, as a story

### A motherless boy

Jean-Jacques Rousseau was born on **28 June 1712**. His mother, **Suzanne Bernard**, died nine days after his birth. He later wrote, in his *Confessions*, that his birth was the first of his misfortunes.

His father, **Isaac Rousseau**, a watchmaker, was affectionate but unstable. Father and son would sit up all night reading novels and Plutarch's *Lives* of Greek and Roman heroes, until the birds began to sing. When Jean-Jacques was ten, his father got into a quarrel and fled Geneva to avoid arrest, leaving the boy with relatives.

He was apprenticed to an engraver who beat him. He learned, he said, to lie and steal.

### Locked out of the city

In **1728**, aged sixteen, returning late from a Sunday walk in the countryside, he found Geneva's gates already shut for the night. It had happened before, and he had been punished. This time he decided not to go back. He walked away from his city, his apprenticeship and his religion, with nothing.

### Madame de Warens

He found his way to **Annecy**, in Savoy, where a Catholic priest sent him to **Madame de Warens**, a young, charming, unconventional woman who received converts. Rousseau was sent to **Turin**, where he converted to Catholicism. He worked as a footman and secretary, then returned to Madame de Warens, whom he called "**Maman**". She became his protector, his teacher and, later, his lover. In the years he spent with her, especially at a country house called **Les Charmettes**, he educated himself, reading philosophy, science, history and music.

### Paris and Thérèse

In **1742**, aged thirty, he went to **Paris** with a new system of musical notation he hoped would make his fortune. It didn't. He worked as a secretary, including for the French ambassador in Venice, and made friends with **Denis Diderot**, who became his closest friend.

He began a lifelong relationship with **Thérèse Levasseur**, a laundress at his inn, uneducated and devoted. They had five children. Rousseau left every one of them, as newborns, at the **Paris Foundling Hospital**, where most such children died young. He later defended and agonised over this decision in his *Confessions*. His enemies never let him forget it.

### The illumination on the road to Vincennes

In **1749**, Diderot was imprisoned at the castle of Vincennes for his writings. Walking out to visit him on a hot summer day, Rousseau read in a newspaper the prize question set by the **Academy of Dijon**: "*Has the restoration of the sciences and arts contributed to the purification of morals?*"

Rousseau later described what happened next as a sudden illumination. He felt dazzled, overwhelmed by a flood of ideas, and sat under a tree in tears. In a flash, he said, he saw that **man is naturally good**, and that it is **institutions** alone that make him bad.

His essay, the ***Discourse on the Sciences and Arts*** (1750), argued that the progress of the arts and sciences had corrupted morals: it encouraged luxury, vanity and idleness, and covered slavery with garlands of flowers. It won the prize, and made him instantly famous.

### Fame and the Second Discourse

His opera ***Le Devin du village*** (*The Village Soothsayer*, 1752) was performed before King Louis XV and was a huge success; the king offered him a pension. Rousseau refused to attend the audience, partly from shyness and partly, he said, to keep his independence.

He began to live by his principles: he gave up his fine clothes and his watch, and earned his living copying music by hand, page by page.

In **1755**, he published the ***Discourse on the Origin and Foundations of Inequality among Men***, another response to a Dijon prize question. It didn't win, but it became one of the most influential essays in history. (We'll look at it below.)

In **1756**, he left Paris for the countryside, to a small house called **the Hermitage**, near the forest of Montmorency, lent to him by a wealthy friend. He quarrelled bitterly with Diderot, with Madame d'Épinay, with Voltaire, and with almost everyone in the circle of the philosophes.

### Julie, The Social Contract and Emile

In a burst of creativity, he produced three great books:

- ***Julie, or the New Heloise*** (1761), a novel in letters about love, virtue and passion, which became perhaps the best-selling novel of the century. Readers wept, wrote him passionate letters, and lined up to borrow copies by the hour.
- ***The Social Contract*** (1762), his theory of legitimate government.
- ***Emile, or On Education*** (1762), a treatise on how to raise a child according to nature.

*Emile* contained a section, "**The Profession of Faith of the Savoyard Vicar**", which argued for a natural religion based on conscience and feeling, rejecting miracles, revelation and the authority of churches. This scandalised both Catholics and Protestants.

### Hunted

In **1762**, the Parlement of Paris condemned *Emile* and ordered Rousseau's arrest. The book was burned. He fled to Switzerland. In Geneva, his own city, *Emile* and *The Social Contract* were both burned, and an order was issued for his arrest. He gave up his citizenship of Geneva in protest. He moved to the Prussian-ruled village of **Môtiers**, in Neuchâtel; in **1765**, after a local pastor preached against him, villagers threw stones at his house in the night. He fled again.

### The quarrel with Hume

In **1766**, the Scottish philosopher **David Hume** invited him to England and found him a house in Derbyshire. Within months, Rousseau became convinced that Hume was part of a plot to dishonour him, and accused him publicly. Hume, deeply hurt, published an account of the quarrel. It became a scandal across Europe. Rousseau's suspicion of persecution, partly justified by real persecution, was turning into something close to paranoia.

### The last years

He returned to France in **1767**, living under a false name for a while. He married Thérèse in a private ceremony in 1768. He wrote his autobiography, the ***Confessions***, one of the first and most influential modern autobiographies, beginning with the claim that he was undertaking something never done before: to show a man in all the truth of nature, and that man was himself. He also wrote ***Rousseau, Judge of Jean-Jacques*** and his last, beautiful work, ***Reveries of the Solitary Walker***, about walks, botany and solitude.

He died on **2 July 1778**, at **Ermenonville**, north of Paris, aged sixty-six, and was buried on an island in a lake in the park, which became a place of pilgrimage. In **1794**, during the Revolution, his remains were moved with great ceremony to the **Panthéon** in Paris, the temple of the nation's great men, where his old enemy Voltaire also lies.

## The big ideas, one by one

### Idea 1: Natural goodness and the natural man

**The idea in one line:** Human beings are naturally good, peaceful and self-sufficient; it is society that corrupts them.

Hobbes had said that in the state of nature, people would be at war with each other. Rousseau replied that Hobbes had made a basic mistake. He had taken people as they are **now**, shaped by society, proud, greedy, competitive, and imagined them **without government**. But real natural man, Rousseau said, would be very different.

Imagine human beings before any society: scattered, living in forests, eating when hungry, sleeping when tired, with few needs, little language, no property and no fixed relationships. Such a being would be guided by two natural feelings:

1. **Amour de soi** (love of oneself): the natural, healthy concern for one's own survival and well-being.
2. **Pitié** (pity, compassion): a natural repugnance at seeing any sensitive being, especially a fellow human, suffer.

Such a being would have no reason to make war. With few needs and plenty of space, why fight? With no property, what to steal? With no comparison with others, what pride to defend?

Rousseau also gave natural man a special capacity: **perfectibility**, the ability to change and develop. This is what makes human history possible, and, he said, it is also the source of all our misfortunes.

**Why it matters:** This idea, that human beings are naturally good and society corrupts them, was revolutionary. If our vices are caused by society, then changing society could change us. It is the root of much modern thinking about crime, education and social reform: the idea that people are shaped by their circumstances.

**A common misunderstanding:** Rousseau is often said to have praised the "**noble savage**" and wanted us to return to the forest. He never used that phrase, and he explicitly said we cannot go back. His natural man is a thought experiment, a way of seeing what society has added to us, not a model to copy.

### Idea 2: How inequality began

**The idea in one line:** Inequality is not natural; it arose step by step with society, property, and the habit of comparing ourselves to others.

The ***Discourse on Inequality*** tells a conjectural history of how humanity got from natural freedom to modern inequality. Rousseau distinguished two kinds of inequality:

- **Natural (physical) inequality**: differences of age, health, strength and intelligence.
- **Moral (political) inequality**: differences of wealth, power and honour, created by human conventions and keeping some above others.

Natural inequalities are small and matter little. Moral inequalities are enormous, and they are **made by society**.

How did they arise? Rousseau's story goes roughly like this:

1. People begin to live near each other, in huts and families. They start to **compare** themselves: "Who sings best? Who dances best? Who is most beautiful, strongest, most skilful?" Being looked at, and being looked at more than others, becomes important. This is the birth of **vanity and contempt**, and of **shame and envy**.
2. Then come **agriculture** and **metallurgy**. Land must be cultivated; cultivation leads to the division of land; and so comes **property**.

The second part of the *Discourse* begins with this famous passage:

"The first man who, having enclosed a piece of ground, bethought himself of saying This is mine, and found people simple enough to believe him, was the real founder of civil society. From how many crimes, wars and murders, from how many horrors and misfortunes might not any one have saved mankind, by pulling up the stakes, or filling up the ditch, and crying to his fellows: Beware of listening to this impostor; you are undone if you once forget that the fruits of the earth belong to us all, and the earth itself to nobody."

3. With property comes the division between **rich and poor**, and constant conflict. The rich, fearing for their possessions, propose a clever scheme: "Let us unite to protect the weak, restrain the ambitious, and secure to everyone what belongs to him." They create **government and laws**. The poor agree, thinking they are securing their freedom; in fact, Rousseau says, "**all ran headlong to their chains**". Laws, he says, gave new fetters to the poor and new powers to the rich, and fixed inequality forever.

Think about this story when you look at a city like Mumbai: some of the most expensive real estate in the world, next to vast slums; laws that protect property titles, while millions live without them. Rousseau would say: the law is not neutral. It often protects those who already have.

**Why it matters:** The *Discourse* was one of the first works to argue that inequality is **social and political**, not natural, and that laws and governments may serve the rich. It deeply influenced **Marx**, socialists and every movement against inequality since.

**A common misunderstanding:** Rousseau did not propose abolishing property. In other writings he accepted property as necessary in society, though he wanted it limited and fairly distributed. His point was historical and moral: we should see property and inequality as human creations, not natural facts.

### Idea 3: Amour-propre, the self that lives in others' eyes

**The idea in one line:** In society, healthy self-love becomes amour-propre, a need to be esteemed above others, which makes us anxious, competitive and dependent.

This may be Rousseau's most powerful psychological insight. Natural man has **amour de soi**: simple self-love, care for one's own survival. But in society, it turns into **amour-propre**: a self-love that depends on **comparison**, on how others see us, and on being esteemed **more** than others.

Rousseau wrote that the savage lives within himself, while social man lives always outside himself, and knows how to live only in the opinion of others. He gets the sense of his own existence, so to speak, from their judgement alone.

Think of how much of modern life runs on amour-propre:

- **Social media likes and followers**, and the anxiety when a post doesn't get attention.
- **Branded clothes, phones and cars**, valued not for their use but for what they say about us.
- **Big weddings and gifts**, competing with relatives' standards.
- **Marks and ranks**, where it matters not just how much you know but whether you beat others.

The problem is that amour-propre can never be satisfied. If my worth depends on being above others, then others' success is my loss. Everyone competes, everyone envies, everyone pretends. And we become **dependent** on others' opinions, which is a kind of slavery.

**Why it matters:** This analysis anticipates modern psychology and sociology: research on status anxiety, relative deprivation, social comparison and the effect of social media on self-esteem. Economists talk of "positional goods" (goods valued because others don't have them). Rousseau saw it all in the salons of Paris.

**A common misunderstanding:** Later scholars have argued that Rousseau didn't think amour-propre is always bad. It can be healthy when it takes the form of a desire for **equal respect**, to be recognised as a person among persons, rather than to be ranked above others. The task of good education and good politics is to channel it in that direction.

### Idea 4: Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains

**The idea in one line:** Since force cannot create right, the only legitimate authority is one that free people create together and agree to obey.

***The Social Contract*** opens with perhaps the most famous first line in political philosophy:

"Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains. One thinks himself the master of others, and still remains a greater slave than they."

Then Rousseau states his question: how did this change come about? He doesn't know. What can make it **legitimate**? That, he thinks, he can answer.

He first clears away false answers. **Force** can't create right: if a robber holds a gun to my head, I may hand over my wallet, but I have no **duty** to do so. "Force does not create right." **Slavery** can't be legitimate either, because to give up your freedom is to give up your quality as a human being.

So the only legitimate basis of authority is an **agreement**. But what kind? Rousseau states the problem precisely: to find a form of association which will defend and protect, with the whole common force, the person and goods of each member, and in which each, while uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before.

That seems impossible. How can I obey the law and still obey only myself? Rousseau's answer is the **social contract**: each person gives himself entirely to the **whole community**. Since everyone does this equally, no one is above anyone else; and since each is part of the whole, in obeying the community's laws, each obeys a will of which he is himself a part.

**Why it matters:** This is the root of the modern idea of **popular sovereignty**: that the people, not a king or God, are the source of all legitimate power. When the Indian Constitution opens with "**We, the people of India**... do hereby adopt, enact and give to ourselves this Constitution", it speaks Rousseau's language.

**A common misunderstanding:** Rousseau's contract is different from Hobbes's. In Hobbes, people give up their rights to a sovereign above them. In Rousseau, the people **are** the sovereign; they give themselves to themselves, collectively.

### Idea 5: The general will

**The idea in one line:** The general will is the will of the community for the common good, which is different from the sum of everyone's private interests, and laws must express it.

This is Rousseau's most famous and most debated concept. He distinguished:

- The **will of all**: the sum of private wills, what each person wants for himself.
- The **general will** (*volonté générale*): what citizens will as members of the community, aiming at the **common good**.

Imagine a village deciding whether to build a school. Each family has private interests: one wants it near their house, another doesn't want to pay taxes, a third has no children. If you add up these private wills, you get a messy bargain, the will of all. But if each villager asks, "**What is good for our village as a whole?**", the answer they converge on is the general will.

Laws, Rousseau said, must be **general**: made by all, applying to all, aimed at the common good. A law that benefits a particular group is not a true law but a private act dressed up as law.

He also warned against **factions** and organised interest groups, which make citizens think as members of a group rather than as citizens. When society is split into powerful factions, the general will is drowned out.

**Why it matters:** The idea of the general will underlies debates about the **public interest** versus private interests and lobbying. When a law is passed because powerful businesses lobbied for it, Rousseau would say it expresses a particular will, not the general will.

**A common misunderstanding:** The general will is not the same as **majority vote**. A majority can be wrong, if voters are thinking about their private interests. Rousseau thought voting can reveal the general will only if citizens are informed, independent and asking the right question: what is good for all?

### Idea 6: Forced to be free?

**The idea in one line:** Someone who refuses to obey the general will can be compelled to obey, which Rousseau called being "forced to be free"; critics see in this the seed of tyranny.

Here is the most dangerous sentence in *The Social Contract*:

"Whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so by the whole body. This means nothing less than that he will be forced to be free."

What did Rousseau mean? His defenders say: when a citizen breaks a law, he is following his private will against his own better, general will as a citizen. Forcing him to obey the law protects the conditions of everyone's freedom, including his own. Think of a traffic law: being "forced" to stop at a red light protects your freedom to move safely.

But critics, from **Benjamin Constant** in the nineteenth century to **Isaiah Berlin** and **J. L. Talmon** in the twentieth, saw something sinister. If a government claims to know the "true will" of the people better than the people themselves, it can justify anything: censorship, prisons, terror, all in the name of "real" freedom. The Jacobins of the French Revolution, who idolised Rousseau, used similar language during the **Terror** (1793–94). Talmon called this "**totalitarian democracy**".

**Why it matters:** Whenever a leader or party says "I am the voice of the people" and treats critics as enemies of the people, the danger Rousseau's critics saw is present. It is a reminder that popular sovereignty needs protections for minorities and dissenters, which Rousseau's system did not clearly provide.

**A common misunderstanding:** Rousseau himself was not a totalitarian; he loved small republics, opposed tyranny, and valued conscience. But his concepts, taken out of their context, could be used to justify oppression.

### Idea 7: Sovereignty cannot be represented

**The idea in one line:** True freedom means making the laws yourself; electing representatives who make laws for you is not freedom.

Rousseau believed sovereignty, the power to make laws, belongs to the people and **cannot be transferred or represented**. Representatives can carry out laws, but the laws themselves must be approved by the assembled people.

He mocked the English, who prided themselves on their liberty:

"The people of England regards itself as free; but it is grossly mistaken; it is free only during the election of members of parliament. As soon as they are elected, slavery overtakes it, and it is nothing."

That's why Rousseau thought true republics must be **small**, like Geneva or the ancient city-states, where all citizens could gather and vote on laws.

**Why it matters:** This is the root of ideals of **direct democracy** and **participatory democracy**: referendums, citizens' assemblies, town meetings. In India, the **gram sabha**, the assembly of all adult voters in a village, which has powers under the Panchayati Raj system to approve plans and review accounts, is a small piece of Rousseau's ideal. So, in a different way, are **social audits** of welfare schemes, where villagers examine how money was spent.

**A common misunderstanding:** Rousseau was not naive about large states. He thought large nations would struggle to be truly free, and offered practical proposals (for Poland, for Corsica) that used representation with strict controls, such as binding instructions and frequent elections.

### Idea 8: Education according to nature

**The idea in one line:** Children should be allowed to develop naturally, learning from experience and things, not from books, lectures and punishments, at the pace of their own growth.

***Emile*** begins:

"Everything is good as it leaves the hands of the Author of things; everything degenerates in the hands of man."

Rousseau imagines a tutor raising a boy, Emile, from birth to adulthood. His key ideas:

- **Childhood has its own nature.** Children are not small adults. Each stage of development has its own needs and capacities.
- **Negative education** in the early years: don't stuff the child with words, rules and moral lectures; protect him from vice and error, and let him learn from things.
- **Learning by experience.** Emile learns geography by getting lost in a forest and finding his way home by the sun; he learns physics by playing with objects. Books come late; Rousseau says the first book Emile reads should be *Robinson Crusoe*, a story of practical self-reliance.
- **Natural consequences** instead of punishment: if Emile breaks a window, let him feel the cold for a while.
- **A trade.** Every child should learn a manual trade (Emile becomes a carpenter), to be independent and to respect labour.
- **Delay amour-propre.** Keep the child from comparison and vanity for as long as possible.

**Why it matters:** *Emile* transformed education. It influenced **Pestalozzi**, **Froebel** (who created the kindergarten), **Montessori**, **John Dewey**, and progressive schools across the world. In India, **Tagore**'s Santiniketan and Gandhi's **Nai Talim** (education through craft) share much of its spirit, and every discussion of "child-centred", "activity-based" or "experiential" learning in Indian education policy carries an echo of *Emile*.

**A common misunderstanding:** Rousseau's tutor is not absent. He controls Emile's environment carefully, arranging experiences so that Emile learns the right lessons, while believing himself free. Critics point out that this is a subtle form of manipulation. And Rousseau's ideal is a single tutor with a single child, impossible in a classroom of forty.

### Idea 9: Civil religion and the citizen

**The idea in one line:** A republic needs citizens who love the laws and one another; Rousseau proposed a simple "civil religion" to support this.

In the last chapter of *The Social Contract*, Rousseau proposed a **civil religion** with a few simple dogmas: the existence of God, the life to come, the happiness of the just and punishment of the wicked, the sanctity of the social contract and the laws, and **tolerance** of all religions that tolerate others. He wanted citizens to love their duties, and feared that religions which taught people to care only about the next world, or to obey a priesthood rather than the state, weakened the republic.

**Why it matters:** Modern states, including secular ones, use something like civil religion: national anthems, flags, Republic Day parades, reverence for the Constitution and national heroes. Rousseau understood that laws alone don't hold a people together; shared sentiments do.

**A common misunderstanding:** Rousseau's civil religion included a disturbing clause: anyone who publicly accepted its dogmas and then behaved as if he didn't believe them could be punished with death. This shows the authoritarian edge in his republicanism, which critics have rightly noted.

## In conversation: who agreed, who argued back

### Hobbes: the state of nature reconsidered

**Thomas Hobbes** described a state of nature as a war of all against all. Rousseau replied that Hobbes had painted civilised man and called him natural. The pride, greed and fear Hobbes saw were products of society. Natural man would be peaceful, because he would have few needs and feel pity.

Imagine them meeting:

*Hobbes:* Without a sovereign to keep them in awe, men will compete, distrust and seek glory. Their lives will be nasty and short.

*Rousseau:* Those are the passions of men in your London, sir, not of men in the forest. Give a man few needs and no neighbour to envy, and he has nothing to fight about.

*Hobbes:* And yet men do live together, and do fight.

*Rousseau:* Yes, and that is the tragedy. Society made them fight, and then society offered them chains as protection.

### Locke: property questioned

**John Locke** argued that property is a natural right, created when people mix their labour with nature, and that government exists mainly to protect it. Rousseau turned this upside down: property was the beginning of inequality, and government was a trick by which the rich made their gains permanent.

### Montesquieu: the spirit of laws

From **Montesquieu**, Rousseau took the idea that laws must suit a people's circumstances: their size, climate, customs and economy. He agreed that liberty is not a fruit of every climate, and that small republics are best suited to it.

### Kant: "Rousseau set me right"

**Immanuel Kant** was deeply influenced by Rousseau. A famous story says Kant, who followed an almost unchanging daily routine, missed his walk only once, while reading *Emile*. Kant wrote that he had once despised the common people who knew nothing, and that Rousseau had set him right, teaching him to honour human beings. Rousseau's idea that freedom means obeying a law you give yourself became the basis of Kant's moral philosophy of **autonomy**. A portrait of Rousseau was the only picture on Kant's wall.

### Burke: the insane Socrates

**Edmund Burke** despised Rousseau. He called him "the insane Socrates of the National Assembly" and blamed his vanity, his abandonment of his children, and his abstract theories for the excesses of the French Revolution.

### Marx: inequality and property

**Karl Marx** inherited Rousseau's view that property and inequality corrupt human relations and that law often serves the rich. But Marx thought Rousseau's solution, a contract among equal citizens, ignored the economic structures that make people unequal. The cure, for Marx, lay in changing the economy, not just the political contract.

### Hume and the philosophes: broken friendships

Rousseau quarrelled with **Voltaire**, who mocked the *Discourse on Inequality*, writing to him that reading it made one want to walk on all fours. He quarrelled with **Diderot**, and finally with **David Hume**, who had tried to help him. These quarrels showed Rousseau's genius for alienating friends, and also the gap between his vision and the Enlightenment's faith in progress.

### Later voices

- **Robespierre** and the **Jacobins** worshipped Rousseau; the Revolution carried his body to the Panthéon.
- **Benjamin Constant** distinguished the "liberty of the ancients" (participation) from the "liberty of the moderns" (private independence), criticising Rousseau for neglecting the second.
- **Isaiah Berlin** and **J. L. Talmon** saw in him a forerunner of totalitarianism.
- **Romantic** poets and novelists, from Goethe to Wordsworth, drew on his love of nature, feeling and the individual heart.
- **Claude Lévi-Strauss**, the anthropologist, called Rousseau the founder of the sciences of man.

## See it around you

**Social media and comparison.** Every anxious refresh of likes and followers, every curated holiday photo, every pang of envy at a friend's post is amour-propre at work: living in the opinion of others.

**Big fat weddings and status spending.** Families borrowing to outdo relatives, gifts measured by price, brands bought for display: Rousseau's analysis of how society turns self-love into competition.

**Inequality debates.** Every argument about whether the rich deserve their wealth, whether laws protect the powerful, or whether land and housing should be redistributed carries an echo of the *Discourse on Inequality*.

**Gram sabhas and direct democracy.** When villagers gather to approve plans and audit spending, or when a city holds a referendum or a citizens' assembly, Rousseau's ideal of the people making laws together is alive.

**"The voice of the people."** When a leader or party claims to embody the people's will and treats critics as traitors, Rousseau's critics' warning applies.

**Child-centred education.** Activity-based learning, play schools, Montessori classrooms, learning outdoors: all trace back, in part, to *Emile*.

**National rituals.** Republic Day parades, flag hoisting, the anthem before films: modern forms of the civil religion Rousseau thought a republic needed.

## Use it

**1. Notice amour-propre.** When you feel envy, or the urge to impress, ask: is this about what I need, or about how I look to others? Rousseau's question is a quiet way out of the comparison trap.

**2. Choose equal respect over being above others.** Aim to be respected as a person among persons, not ranked above them. It's a want that can actually be satisfied.

**3. Ask "what is good for all of us?"** In a group decision, at home, in a housing society, in a classroom, try to set aside "what do I want?" and ask "what is good for the whole group?" That's the question of the general will.

**4. Be suspicious of anyone who speaks for "the people".** When someone claims to know the true will of the people better than the people themselves, ask how dissenters are protected.

**5. Participate, don't just vote.** If you only vote every five years, Rousseau would say you are free only on polling day. Attend a gram sabha or residents' meeting, join a public consultation, read and comment on draft laws.

**6. Let children learn from things.** If you teach or parent, let children explore, make mistakes, and learn from consequences, before lecturing them. Give them real tasks and a practical skill.

**7. Separate natural from social inequality.** When you hear that some people are "naturally" superior, rich or poor, ask how much of the difference is natural, and how much was made by laws, property and history.

**8. Read his life alongside his books.** Rousseau's abandonment of his children, against his own teaching, reminds us that great ideas and admirable lives don't always go together. Judge the ideas on their merits.

## Where he went wrong

**1. His children.** The author of the most influential book on raising children abandoned all five of his own to a foundling hospital where most children died. He offered excuses (poverty, his health, the children's supposed better future), but the contradiction is glaring.

**2. Women.** In *Emile*, Rousseau's vision for **Sophie**, Emile's future wife, is shockingly different: she is to be educated to please men, to be modest, obedient and dependent. **Mary Wollstonecraft**, in *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), attacked this directly. Rousseau's republic of equal citizens was a republic of men.

**3. The general will and tyranny.** His ideas of the general will, being "forced to be free" and a civil religion enforced by death allowed later regimes to claim to speak for the people while crushing individuals. He provided too few protections for minorities and dissent.

**4. No representation.** His rejection of representative government made his ideal impossible for large modern states. Most modern democracies, including India, rely on representation, combined with rights, courts and a free press.

**5. Romanticising simplicity.** Rousseau's suspicion of the arts, sciences and commerce could become a hostility to progress that ignores the real benefits of science, medicine and trade, especially for the poor.

**6. Speculation as history.** His history of inequality is conjectural, a philosophical story rather than evidence-based history. Anthropologists have since shown that early human societies were far more varied than his picture of solitary natural man.

**7. Paranoia and quarrels.** His tendency to see plots everywhere, and to break with every friend, made him a difficult man and sometimes an unreliable witness to his own life.

## The shift

**Before this page,** you may have thought that inequality, competition and status-seeking are simply human nature. You may have thought freedom means being left alone, or voting once in five years.

**Now,** you can see that much of what we call human nature, our vanity, our envy, our need to be seen, may be the work of society, and that the endless comparison of amour-propre can never be satisfied. You can see that inequality of wealth and power was made by human beings, and can be questioned by them, and that law may protect the rich as much as it protects everyone. You can see freedom as obeying laws you have helped to make, and the danger that comes when anyone claims to know your "true" will better than you. And you can see how one restless man shaped modern democracy, education and the critique of civilisation, while failing badly in his own life.

Here is how everyday life starts to look different:

- **A social media notification** becomes a test of how much you live in others' eyes.
- **A lavish wedding or status purchase** looks like amour-propre on display.
- **A fence around a piece of land** carries the shadow of Rousseau's first man saying "This is mine."
- **Election day** makes you ask whether you are free only on that day.
- **"The will of the people"** in a politician's mouth makes you ask who is being forced to be free.
- **A child learning by doing** looks like Emile in the forest.

A boy locked out of his city at sixteen spent his life asking how human beings could be free again. His answer was beautiful and dangerous, and his question, why are we born free and everywhere in chains, still burns.

## Quick recap

- **Who:** Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778), Genevan-born philosopher, novelist, composer and educational theorist.
- **Context:** the small republic of Geneva, absolutist France, the Enlightenment and the philosophes, the social contract debate of Hobbes and Locke.
- **Life:** mother died at his birth; left Geneva at sixteen; Madame de Warens; Paris and Diderot; five children left at a foundling hospital; the illumination on the road to Vincennes (1749); *First Discourse* (1750); *Discourse on Inequality* (1755); *Julie*, *The Social Contract* and *Emile* (1761–62); books burned, hunted across Europe; quarrel with Hume; *Confessions*; died 1778; Panthéon 1794.
- **Natural goodness:** natural man is peaceful, moved by self-love and pity; society corrupts.
- **Inequality:** natural inequality is small; moral inequality is made by property, comparison and law.
- **Amour-propre:** self-love that depends on others' opinion and comparison.
- **Social contract:** "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains"; legitimate authority comes from free agreement; the people are sovereign.
- **General will:** the will for the common good, not the sum of private interests.
- **Forced to be free:** the dangerous edge of his theory.
- **No representation:** the English are free only at elections; small republics and direct democracy.
- **Emile:** education according to nature, learning by experience, a practical trade.
- **Criticisms:** abandoned children, sexism toward women, totalitarian potential, impractical for large states, romanticised simplicity, speculative history.

**Key terms**

- **State of nature:** human life before society, which Rousseau imagined as peaceful.
- **Amour de soi:** natural self-love, concern for one's own survival.
- **Amour-propre:** self-love that depends on comparison and others' esteem.
- **Pitié:** natural compassion for the suffering of others.
- **Perfectibility:** the human capacity for change and development.
- **General will:** the will of citizens as members of the community, aimed at the common good.
- **Will of all:** the sum of private wills.
- **Popular sovereignty:** the principle that the people are the source of legitimate power.
- **Civil religion:** a minimal public faith meant to bind citizens to the laws.
- **Negative education:** protecting the young child from vice and premature instruction, and letting him learn from things.
